Which wine goes with lamb: roasts, chops and stews

Why lamb gets on so well with red wine, which style to choose depending on the age of the animal and how it is cooked, and some alternatives for those who prefer white or rosé.

Lamb is one of the meats that gets on best with red wine. Its fat and intense flavour soften the wine's tannins, while the red's acidity and tannins cleanse the palate between mouthfuls. But not all lamb is the same: a milk-fed lamb roasted in a wood oven is very different from chargrilled chops or a spiced stew. These are the keys to getting it right.

The general rule: a structured red

If you remember only one thing, make it this: a red with firm tannins and good acidity works very well with lamb. In Spain, Tempranillo is the classic partner, both in Ribera del Duero and in Rioja. Outside Spain, Bordeaux reds based on Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot, Rhône Syrah or a Chianti Classico play the same role.

Depending on the type of lamb

Milk-fed lamb. A very young animal, fed on milk, with tender, pale meat and a delicate flavour. A very powerful red would overwhelm it. It goes well with a red with moderate ageing, such as a Ribera del Duero or Rioja Crianza, or a young Tempranillo. In Castile, roast milk-fed lamb with a red from Ribera del Duero is almost a tradition.

Spring lamb. A little older, with more flavour and slightly more fat. It can take reds with more body and ageing: a Rioja or Ribera Reserva, a Toro or a structured Grenache.

Older lamb and mutton. Its flavour is more intense and it can stand powerful, concentrated wines: Priorat, Toro, a Cabernet Sauvignon or a Syrah.

Depending on how it is cooked

Oven-roasted. The traditional roast, with just water, salt and a little lard or butter, is simple and lets the meat speak. A Tempranillo with moderate ageing is the most common choice. If the roast is flavoured with herbs such as rosemary or thyme, Grenache and other Mediterranean reds also work very well.

Chargrilled or barbecued chops. The smoky, toasty flavours of the grill call for young reds with little or no oak and plenty of fruit. A Mencía, a young Grenache or a young Tempranillo are good options, and in hot weather a full-bodied rosé.

Roast shoulder or leg with vegetables. Crianza or Reserva reds with ripe fruit work well. If the recipe uses garlic and wine in the sauce, ideally cook with a wine similar to the one you will serve.

Stews and casseroles. A lamb stew or Irish stew calls for reds with body and fruit and without overly drying tannins: Grenache, Monastrell or Syrah.

Spiced lamb. In recipes with cumin, cinnamon or paprika, such as a tagine or a Middle Eastern-style lamb, choose a fruity, low-tannin red, since tannins and high alcohol accentuate heat. A young Grenache or Monastrell are good options.

What if I prefer white or rosé?

It is possible, especially with milk-fed lamb or chops. A full-bodied white with some ageing, such as an oaked white Rioja or a barrel-fermented Godello, can go well with milk-fed lamb. A structured rosé is a good option with chops, grilled lamb or spiced recipes. Very light, aromatic whites, on the other hand, tend to fall short.

Temperature and service

  • Serve the red at 15 to 18 °C. Too warm, and the alcohol shows more and the wine seems heavy.
  • Young, very structured reds benefit from being opened a while beforehand or decanted.
  • A large glass helps the wine show its aromas better.

In short: with lamb, a structured red as a general rule; more delicate with milk-fed lamb, more powerful with older lamb, fruity and young with the grill and with spices. And if in doubt, a Tempranillo from Ribera del Duero or Rioja rarely fails.
