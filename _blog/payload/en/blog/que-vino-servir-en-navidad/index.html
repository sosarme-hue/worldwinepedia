Which wine to serve at Christmas: a dish-by-dish guide

From the first nibbles to the Christmas pudding: which wine styles suit typical festive dishes, how many bottles to plan for and how to organise the service without fuss.

Christmas Eve dinner and Christmas lunch are often the longest meals of the year: many dishes, many guests and very different tastes. You do not need a perfect wine for every mouthful. It is enough to choose three or four styles that cover the menu from start to finish and to serve them in the right order. This guide goes through the most common festive dishes and the wines that tend to work best with them.

Starters and nibbles: sparkling wine and dry fortified wines

To begin, few things work as well as a dry sparkling wine: Champagne, Cava or any traditional-method wine in a brut or brut nature style. Its acidity and bubbles cleanse the palate and pair easily with cured ham, charcuterie, hard cheeses, canapés and nuts. If there are shellfish or smoked salmon on the table, they suit it too.

A very Spanish alternative is a well-chilled fino or manzanilla sherry. Dry, saline and full of character, they are particularly good with ham, almonds, olives, prawns and anchovies.

Shellfish and fish starters

Prawns, crab, oysters or a seafood salad call for whites with good acidity and little or no oak. An Albariño from Rías Baixas, a Godello, a Verdejo from Rueda or a Chablis are safe choices. With oysters, very dry sparkling wines and saline whites are classics that are hard to beat.

If the starter is a soup or fish in a sauce, a white with a little more body is a better fit: a white aged on its lees or a white Rioja with some oak.

The main course

Roast lamb or suckling pig. This is the great Christmas classic of central Spain. A red with structure and firm tannins, such as a Ribera del Duero or a Rioja Crianza or Reserva, stands up to the fat and the roasted flavours. For something fresher, a Mencía from Bierzo also works.

Turkey, capon or stuffed chicken. These are milder meats, and stuffings with nuts, dried fruit or cranberry sauce add sweetness. Medium-bodied reds with ripe fruit, such as a Grenache or a Pinot Noir, work well, or even a full-bodied white if the bird comes with a creamy sauce.

Red meat and game. Beef Wellington, roast beef or a venison stew can take more concentrated reds: Toro, Priorat, a Rhône Syrah or a Cabernet Sauvignon.

Baked fish. Sea bream, sea bass or salmon from the oven call for full-bodied whites or, if you prefer red, a light, low-tannin one served a little cooler than usual.

Desserts and Christmas sweets

The basic rule is that the wine should be at least as sweet as the dessert; otherwise it will taste sour and bitter. With Christmas pudding, mince pies, nougat or marzipan, try a Muscat, a Pedro Ximénez or a sweet oloroso. With chocolate desserts, a Pedro Ximénez or a sweet red. With fruit desserts or a light cake, an off-dry sparkling wine or a delicate Moscato.

How many bottles to plan for

A 75 cl bottle gives about six glasses. As a rough guide, people usually allow between half a bottle and a bottle per adult for the whole meal, shared across the different wines. It is better to have an unopened bottle left over than to run short, as it will keep for another occasion. Always have water and alcohol-free drinks to hand, and remember that whoever is driving should not drink.

Serving tips

  • Temperature: sparkling and dry fortified wines, 6 to 8 °C; whites, 8 to 12 °C; reds, 14 to 18 °C. In a heated house it is worth cooling reds a little before serving.
  • Order: from lighter to more powerful and from dry to sweet.
  • Glasses: you do not need many. A medium-sized glass works for almost everything, sparkling wine included.
  • Open young, powerful reds in advance; old wines are best opened just before serving.

With a sparkling wine to start, a fresh white for the seafood, a structured red for the roast and a sweet wine for dessert, you will cover almost any Christmas menu. Happy holidays!
