White grape

Chardonnay

The great white grape of Burgundy and Champagne, highly adaptable, faithfully reflecting climate and winemaking, from Chablis to California and Australia.

Origin

Chardonnay comes from Burgundy. In 1999 researchers at the University of California, Davis, used DNA analysis to show that it is the result of a natural cross between Pinot and Gouais Blanc, a variety widespread in medieval France. Its name is the same as that of a village in the Mâconnais.

Viticulture

It buds early, which makes it vulnerable to spring frost, and ripens early to mid-season. It is relatively easy to grow and adapts to very different climates, from cool areas such as Chablis or Champagne to warm regions. It prefers limestone and marl soils. It is susceptible to powdery mildew and grey rot.

In the glass

Chardonnay is a relatively neutral grape aromatically, which allows it to express climate, soil and winemaking. In cool climates it gives taut wines with high acidity and notes of green apple, lemon and wet stone; in moderate climates peach and melon appear; in warm areas pineapple, mango and other tropical fruit. It responds very well to barrel fermentation and ageing, malolactic fermentation and lees work, which add butter, hazelnut, toast and vanilla. The great Chardonnays of Burgundy age for years. It is also one of the three main grapes of Champagne, used on its own for Blanc de Blancs.

Where it is grown

In France its benchmark areas are Burgundy (Chablis, Côte de Beaune, Côte Chalonnaise, Mâconnais) and Champagne, and it is also grown in Languedoc and the Jura. It is one of the most widely planted white varieties in the world, prominent in the United States (California, Oregon), Australia (Margaret River, Adelaide Hills, Yarra Valley), New Zealand, Chile, Argentina and South Africa. In Italy it is grown in Franciacorta, Trentino, Alto Adige and elsewhere, as well as in Austria and Germany. In Spain it is used in Penedès, the Cava D.O., Somontano, Navarra and Costers del Segre, among others.
