Cabernet Franc

A parent of both Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot, it gives aromatic, fresh reds and is a leading grape in the Loire and Saint-Émilion.

Origin

Cabernet Franc is an old variety from south-western France; genetic studies suggest it may have originated in the Basque Country, on both sides of the Pyrenees, from where it reached Bordeaux and the Loire Valley. Its historical importance is huge: it is a parent of Cabernet Sauvignon (with Sauvignon Blanc), of Merlot and of Carmenère.

Viticulture

It buds and ripens earlier than Cabernet Sauvignon, so it is better suited to cooler climates and colder soils, such as the clay and limestone of Saint-Émilion or the tuffeau of the Loire. It withstands winter cold well. If it does not ripen fully, or with excessive yields, marked vegetal notes from pyrazines appear.

In the glass

It gives medium-coloured reds, light- to medium-bodied, with fresh acidity and finer tannins than Cabernet Sauvignon. Its aromatic profile is very recognisable: raspberry, redcurrant, strawberry, violet and an herbal hint of green pepper, tomato leaf or graphite. In the Loire it makes fresh reds for early drinking and more structured ones that age well; it is also used for rosé. In Bordeaux it adds aroma and finesse to blends with Merlot and Cabernet Sauvignon.

Where it is grown

In France its benchmark areas are the central Loire (Chinon, Bourgueil, Saint-Nicolas-de-Bourgueil, Saumur-Champigny) and Bordeaux’s Right Bank, especially Saint-Émilion. In Italy it is grown in the north-east (Friuli, Veneto) and in Tuscany. In the United States it stands out in New York State, Virginia and California. Argentina has produced well-regarded varietal wines in Mendoza, and it is also grown in Chile, South Africa, Australia and New Zealand. In Spain it is grown only on a small scale.

At the table

Its freshness and herbal profile combine well with charcuterie, roast poultry, pork, vegetable dishes and goat’s cheese. The lighter Loire reds can be served slightly cool, around 14–15 °C.
