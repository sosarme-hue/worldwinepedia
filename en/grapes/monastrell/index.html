Monastrell (Mourvèdre)

A late-ripening, sun-loving red grape, the mainstay of Jumilla, Yecla and Alicante and, as Mourvèdre, the heart of Bandol in France.

Origin

Monastrell comes from eastern Spain, where it has been grown for centuries in the provinces of Valencia, Alicante and Murcia. Its French name, Mourvèdre, is linked to Murviedro (today’s Sagunto), and the name Mataro, used in Australia and the United States, to the Catalan town of Mataró.

Viticulture

It buds and ripens late and needs plenty of sun and heat to ripen fully; it is often said to like having “its face in the sun and its feet in the water”. It tolerates drought well and adapts to poor, limestone soils. Eastern Spain has many old, ungrafted bush vines, since some sandy soils held back phylloxera. Its berries are small and thick-skinned.

In the glass

It gives deeply coloured wines with abundant, sometimes rustic tannins when young, a full body and high alcohol in warm areas. The aromas recall blackberry, plum and ripe dark fruit, with notes of wild herbs, pepper, leather and an earthy or gamey character that grows with age. Well made, it ages well. It is also used for rosés and, in Alicante, for Fondillón, a long-aged rancio wine.

Where it is grown

Spain holds most of the world’s plantings: Jumilla, Yecla, Bullas, Alicante and Valencia are its main areas, and it is also found in Almansa and Catalonia. In France, as Mourvèdre, it is the dominant grape in the red wines of Bandol in Provence and a supporting variety in Châteauneuf-du-Pape, Languedoc and Roussillon. As Mataro or Mourvèdre it is grown in Australia (Barossa, McLaren Vale), where it is part of the well-known GSM blend with Grenache and Shiraz, as well as in California and South Africa.

At the table

Monastrell’s structure and ripe fruit call for flavourful dishes: grilled red meat, game stews, spiced charcuterie and aged cheeses. The rosés go well with rice dishes and Mediterranean cooking, and Fondillón is enjoyed with nuts or desserts.
