Pinot Noir

Burgundy’s great red grape, delicate and demanding, giving fine, aromatic reds and one of the core grapes of Champagne.

Origin

Pinot Noir is one of the oldest varieties in western Europe and has been recorded in Burgundy since the Middle Ages. Its name is usually linked to the shape of its small, compact bunch, which resembles a pine cone. It is genetically unstable and has produced numerous mutations, such as Pinot Gris and Pinot Blanc, as well as many clones. Together with Gouais Blanc, it is a parent of varieties such as Chardonnay and Gamay.

Viticulture

It buds and ripens early, which suits it to cool climates but exposes it to spring frost. Its skin is thin and its bunches compact, so it is very susceptible to grey rot, downy mildew and powdery mildew. It is highly expressive of the place where it grows and of yields, and is considered difficult to grow and to make.

In the glass

It gives pale-coloured reds, light- to medium-bodied, with lively acidity and fine tannins. Young, red fruit dominates (strawberry, raspberry, cherry) with floral notes; with age it develops forest floor, mushroom, leather and spice. Besides still reds, it is one of the three main varieties of Champagne and of many sparkling wines around the world, made as a white or rosé.

Where it is grown

In France its benchmarks are Burgundy’s Côte d’Or, Champagne and Alsace, as well as the Loire (Sancerre). In Germany, as Spätburgunder, it is the most planted red grape, and in Austria it is called Blauburgunder. In Italy, as Pinot Nero, it is grown in Alto Adige, Oltrepò Pavese and Franciacorta. In the New World, Oregon and California (Sonoma, Santa Barbara), New Zealand (Central Otago, Marlborough, Martinborough), Australia (Yarra Valley, Tasmania), Chile (Casablanca) and South Africa (Hemel-en-Aarde) stand out. In Spain it is used in rosé Cava and in some reds from cool areas.

At the table

Its acidity and fine tannins make it very food-friendly: it goes with poultry, duck, salmon and tuna, mushrooms and game birds. It is served slightly cooler than other reds, at 14–16 °C, in a wide glass.
