Sangiovese

Italy’s most widely planted red grape, the soul of Chianti, Brunello di Montalcino and Vino Nobile di Montepulciano.

Origin

Sangiovese is a variety from central Italy, documented in Tuscany since the sixteenth century. Its name has traditionally been linked to the Latin sanguis Jovis, “blood of Jupiter”, although the etymology is uncertain. DNA analysis indicates that it is the offspring of Ciliegiolo, from Tuscany, and Calabrese di Montenuovo, an old variety from southern Italy. It shows great variability in clones and biotypes, which go by local names.

Viticulture

It buds early and ripens late, needing warmth and a long season to ripen fully; in cool years its wines turn out sharp and hard. It is vigorous and, being thin-skinned, susceptible to rot. It is suited to Tuscany’s hills with poor soils such as galestro (clay schist) and alberese (limestone), which give finer wines.

In the glass

It gives ruby-red wines of medium intensity that soon turn garnet, with high acidity and firm tannins. The aromas recall sour cherry, morello cherry and red plum, with notes of violet, herbs, sun-dried tomato, black tea and, with ageing, leather, tobacco and earth. Its acidity makes it a classic at the Italian table. It is made as a varietal wine, as in Brunello di Montalcino, or blended with local varieties such as Canaiolo or with Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot, as in some Super Tuscans.

Where it is grown

Italy holds almost all the world’s plantings. In Tuscany it is the basis of Chianti and Chianti Classico, Brunello di Montalcino, Vino Nobile di Montepulciano (where it is called Prugnolo Gentile) and Morellino di Scansano. It is also grown in Romagna, Umbria, the Marche, Lazio and the south of the country. In Corsica, France, it is known as Nielluccio and is the basis of the red wines of Patrimonio. Outside Europe there are plantings in the United States (California), Argentina and Australia.

At the table

Its acidity makes it very versatile with Italian cooking: pasta with ragù, pizza, grilled meat such as bistecca alla fiorentina, game, mushrooms and cheeses like Tuscan pecorino. Young Chianti is served at 15–16 °C, and Brunello and other aged wines at 16–18 °C, ideally after brief decanting.
