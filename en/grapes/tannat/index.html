Tannat

Red grape from south-west France, the basis of Madiran, famous for its abundant tannins and deep colour. In Uruguay, where it arrived in the nineteenth century, it has become the country’s signature variety.

Origin

Tannat is a variety from south-west France, grown for centuries in the foothills of the Pyrenees, in Béarn, Bigorre and the French Basque Country. Its name is often linked to the word tanin, in reference to its tannic richness. It is the main variety of Madiran, where it must make up most of the blend, and it also features in appellations such as Irouléguy, Saint-Mont, Tursan and Béarn. In the second half of the nineteenth century Basque immigrants took it to Uruguay; there it is associated above all with Pascual Harriague, who planted it in Salto, and for a long time it was known by his surname.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous, productive vine, budding and ripening late, with compact bunches of small to medium berries with thick skins. It adapts well to temperate, humid climates, although its tight bunches leave it exposed to rot in wet years, so its vigour and yields are usually controlled. Micro-oxygenation was developed in Madiran in the early 1990s, a technique that introduces small amounts of oxygen into the wine during ageing and that was created precisely to soften its tannins.

In the glass

It gives wines of very intense colour, almost black when young, with abundant, firm tannins and notable acidity. Its aromas recall blackberry, plum, blackcurrant and raspberry, with notes of liquorice, spice, cocoa and, with age, leather, tobacco and forest floor. Traditionally it needed years in bottle to soften, but modern winemaking techniques, more careful extraction and barrel ageing have produced wines that are more approachable young. It is often blended with Cabernet Sauvignon, Cabernet Franc or Merlot, which add aroma and suppleness. Tannat wines are rich in polyphenols, a feature that has attracted interest in studies of wine composition.

Where it is grown

Apart from south-west France, its great territory is Uruguay, where it is the most planted red variety and the country’s calling card. There it gives reds that are somewhat fruitier and less rustic in tannin than traditional Madiran, in styles ranging from young wines to long-aged ones, as well as rosés, sparkling and sweet wines. It is also grown in Argentina, above all in Salta, in southern Brazil and, to a lesser extent, in the United States, Australia and other countries.
