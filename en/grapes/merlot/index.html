Merlot

A Bordeaux red giving soft, fleshy, fruity wines, the leading grape of Pomerol and Saint-Émilion and one of the most widely planted varieties in the world.

Origin

Merlot comes from the Bordeaux region, where it is recorded from the late eighteenth century. DNA analysis has shown it to be the offspring of Cabernet Franc and a little-known Charentes variety, Magdeleine Noire des Charentes, making it a half-sibling of Cabernet Sauvignon. Its name is usually linked to the blackbird (merle in French).

Viticulture

It buds and ripens earlier than Cabernet Sauvignon, which suits it to cooler, clay-rich soils such as those of Bordeaux’s Right Bank. It is susceptible to coulure, downy mildew and rot, and can suffer from spring frosts. In warm climates it builds sugar quickly, so picking date is key to keeping freshness.

In the glass

It gives deeply coloured wines, medium- to full-bodied, with softer tannins than Cabernet Sauvignon and a rounded texture. The aromas recall plum, black cherry and ripe red fruit, with notes of chocolate, truffle or herbs depending on climate and ageing. It can be made for early, easy drinking, but on its best sites it gives complex wines for long ageing. It is the classic partner of Cabernet Sauvignon and Cabernet Franc in Bordeaux-style blends.

Where it is grown

In Bordeaux it is the most planted variety and the basis of the wines of Pomerol and Saint-Émilion. It is widely grown in southern France, Italy (Veneto, Friuli, Tuscany), Switzerland (Ticino), the United States (California, Washington State), Chile, where for years it was confused in the vineyard with Carmenère, and Argentina, Australia, South Africa and New Zealand. In Spain it is found in Penedès, Navarra, Somontano, Costers del Segre and many other areas.

At the table

Its soft texture makes it very versatile: it goes with roast poultry, red meat, pasta with meat sauces, mushrooms and soft or semi-cured cheeses. It is served at 15–18 °C depending on its structure.
