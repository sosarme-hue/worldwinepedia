Petit Manseng

A white grape from Béarn with tiny, thick-skinned berries, able to build up a lot of sugar without losing acidity, and the basis of the celebrated sweet Jurançon made from grapes shrivelled on the vine.

Origin

Petit Manseng is an old variety from the Pyrénées-Atlantiques in south-west France, traditionally grown in Béarn and the French Basque Country. It shares its name and area with Gros Manseng, but they are different varieties, and Petit Manseng is the more highly regarded for quality sweet wines. Its reputation is tied to the Jurançon appellation, whose sweet wines have a long tradition in the region.

Viticulture

Its bunches are small and its berries tiny and thick-skinned, so yields are low. That tough skin allows the grapes to remain on the vine well into autumn, and even into winter, without rotting: they dehydrate slowly in the sun and the warm south wind, a process known as passerillage, or shrivelling on the vine. In this way they concentrate sugar and aroma while keeping very high acidity, which is the key to the balance of the wines. Harvesting is often done in several successive passes.

In the glass

Sweet wines from Petit Manseng combine intense sweetness with lively acidity, which gives them freshness and great ageing potential. Their aromas recall pineapple, mango, passion fruit, quince, candied citrus and honey, with spicy notes of cinnamon or ginger that become more pronounced with age. Dry or medium-dry wines are also made, intense and markedly acidic.

Where it is grown

It is fundamental to sweet Jurançon and is present in dry Jurançon, in Pacherenc du Vic-Bilh, the white-wine appellation of the Madiran area, and in Irouléguy in the French Basque Country. It is also grown in other parts of the south-west, such as the Côtes de Gascogne IGP, and in the Languedoc. Outside France it has attracted interest in the United States, especially in Virginia, where its thick skin stands up well to summer humidity, and there are plantings in Australia.

At the table

Sweet Jurançon goes with foie gras, blue cheeses or aged ewe’s-milk cheeses, dishes cooked with fruit and desserts based on tropical fruit or almonds; the dry wines suit fish in sauce and spiced dishes. Serve the sweet wines at 8–11 °C.
