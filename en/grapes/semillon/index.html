Semillon

A thin-skinned Bordeaux white, key to the great sweet wines of Sauternes and the dry whites of Graves, and the star of Australia’s Hunter Valley.

Origin

Semillon (Sémillon in French) comes from the Bordeaux region, probably from the Sauternais and Graves, where it has been grown for centuries. In the nineteenth century it spread widely abroad: it became the dominant variety in South Africa, where it was called Groendruif (“green grape”), and it was established early in Australia and Chile.

Viticulture

It is a productive and fairly robust vine, with medium-sized bunches and thin-skinned berries. That thin skin makes it highly susceptible to Botrytis cinerea, a drawback in wet years but a great asset in places like Sauternes and Barsac, where autumn mists rising from the Ciron and the Garonne encourage noble rot. Botrytised grapes are hand-picked, berry by berry, in successive passes through the vineyard. Its acidity is moderate, which is why it is often blended with Sauvignon Blanc.

In the glass

Young and dry, Semillon is understated: citrus, apple, gentle grassiness and wax, with medium body and a slightly oily texture. Barrel fermentation and ageing give it breadth and notes of vanilla and nuts. Botrytised sweet wines show apricot, peaches in syrup, honey, candied orange peel and saffron, and can age for decades. The Hunter Valley makes a singular style: picked early, low in alcohol and unoaked, it is almost austere in youth and over the years develops notes of toast, honey and nuts.

Where it is grown

In Bordeaux it is the main grape of Sauternes and Barsac, blended with Sauvignon Blanc and sometimes Muscadelle, and it is part of the dry whites of Pessac-Léognan, Graves and Entre-Deux-Mers. It is also found in Bergerac and Monbazillac. In Australia the Hunter Valley and Margaret River stand out, the latter blending it with Sauvignon Blanc. It is also grown in South Africa, Chile, Argentina, the United States (Washington State and California) and New Zealand.

At the table

Dry versions suit fish, shellfish and poultry; sweet ones go with foie gras, blue cheeses such as Roquefort and fruit desserts. Serve sweet wines at 8–10 °C and dry ones at 10–12 °C.
