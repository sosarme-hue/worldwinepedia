Malvasia

A name shared by a group of varieties, many unrelated, famous since the Middle Ages for sweet, aromatic wines; in Spain the Canary Islands versions stand out.

Origin

The name Malvasia comes from Monemvasia, a fortified port in the Peloponnese from which Venetian merchants exported highly prized sweet wines in the Middle Ages. Over time the name was applied to many varieties around the Mediterranean and the Atlantic, many of them genetically unrelated. It is therefore more accurate to speak of a family of names than of a single grape. Most are white, although there are red Malvasias, such as Italy’s Malvasia Nera.

Main varieties

Among the most important are Malvasía Aromática, genetically identified with Malvasía de Sitges and Italy’s Malvasia di Lipari; Malvasía Volcánica from Lanzarote; Malvasía Riojana, which is in fact the variety Alarije; the Italian Malvasia Bianca di Candia and Malvasia Istriana; and Madeira’s Malvasia, the source of Malmsey. Several of them are aromatic, with terpenes that give floral notes, while others are more neutral.

In the glass

Aromatic Malvasias give wines with aromas of flowers (orange blossom, rose), apricot, peach, candied citrus and honey, sometimes with a spicy touch. They are made dry, with body and moderate freshness, and above all sweet: late-harvest, from dried grapes or fortified. Aged sweet Malvasia wines develop notes of nuts, caramel and spice and are very long-lived, as with Madeira’s Malmsey.

Where it is grown

In Spain the Canary Islands stand out, above all Lanzarote, where Malvasía Volcánica grows in hollows dug into the volcanic ash and protected by low stone walls, as well as La Palma, Tenerife and Gran Canaria, with dry and sweet wines; Sitges in Penedès; and Rioja and Navarra, with Malvasía Riojana. In Italy it is grown in Lazio (Frascati), Friuli, Emilia, the Aeolian Islands, Sardinia and other regions. In Portugal, Madeira stands out, and in Croatia Istrian Malvazija.
