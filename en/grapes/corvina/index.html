Corvina

The main grape of Valpolicella and Bardolino, thin-skinned and lively in acidity, and the backbone of Amarone and Recioto made from dried grapes.

Origin

Corvina, also called Corvina Veronese or Cruina, is native to the province of Verona in the Veneto, where it has been grown for centuries in the hills stretching from Lake Garda to the valleys of Valpolicella. It should not be confused with Corvinone, a distinct variety with larger berries that was long considered one of its clones. Genetic studies link it to Rondinella, which appears to be its offspring.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous vine, ripening mid to late, traditionally trained on the Veronese pergola, although vertical trellising is increasingly common. Its bunches are medium-sized and its berries thin-skinned but rich in pigments and aromatic compounds. The first buds at the base of the cane are not very fertile, so it needs long pruning. Its resistant skins and ability to dehydrate without rotting make it ideal for appassimento, the drying of grapes after harvest in well-ventilated lofts for several months.

In the glass

Made in the traditional way it gives medium-ruby reds, light- to medium-bodied, with lively acidity and soft tannins, and aromas of cherry, sour cherry, red berries, almond and herbs. With dried grapes the result changes completely: Amarone della Valpolicella is dry, powerful and high in alcohol, with aromas of cherry liqueur, prunes, chocolate and spice, while Recioto is its sweet counterpart. Ripasso is made by refermenting Valpolicella on the pomace left over from Amarone.

Where it is grown

Its territory is almost exclusively the province of Verona. It is the main variety in the Valpolicella, Valpolicella Ripasso, Amarone della Valpolicella and Recioto della Valpolicella appellations, where it is blended with Corvinone, Rondinella and sometimes Molinara and other local grapes. It is also the base of Bardolino and the rosé Chiaretto on the shores of Lake Garda. Its presence outside Italy is very limited.

At the table

Young Valpolicella goes with pasta, cured meats and white meats; Amarone with braised meat, game and well-aged cheeses. Serve the former at 14–16 °C and Amarone at 16–18 °C.
