Vidal

A white hybrid grape of French origin, very cold-hardy and thick-skinned, which has become the most widely used variety for Canadian icewine, especially in Ontario.

Origin

Vidal, also called Vidal Blanc or Vidal 256, is a hybrid variety bred in France by the hybridiser Jean-Louis Vidal, probably in the 1930s. It comes from a cross between Ugni Blanc, the French name for Trebbiano Toscano, and Rayon d’Or, a hybrid of the Seibel family. Like other French-American hybrids, it was created to combine the quality of European varieties with the resistance to disease and cold contributed by American vine species. In France it never became important, partly because European Union rules on quality wines generally exclude hybrids, but it found a place in the cold climates of North America.

Viticulture

Its main virtue is its resistance to winter cold, which allows it to survive temperatures that would damage many Vitis vinifera varieties. It is productive and late-ripening, with loose bunches and thick, tough-skinned berries. This skin means the grapes stay in good condition on the vine well into winter, withstanding wind, snow and cycles of freezing and thawing, which makes it an ideal variety for icewine, for which the grapes are harvested naturally frozen. It is also relatively resistant to some fungal diseases.

In the glass

In icewines, Vidal gives very sweet, unctuous wines balanced by notable acidity, with intense aromas of apricot, peach, pineapple, mango, honey and candied citrus. They tend to be somewhat more tropical, and to gain less complexity with age, than Riesling icewines, and are generally enjoyed young. It is also used for late-harvest wines, dry or off-dry whites with fruity aromas and marked acidity, and sparkling wines, including some sparkling icewines.

Where it is grown

Its great territory is Canada, above all the Niagara Peninsula in Ontario, where it is the most widely used variety for icewine and has helped give the style international renown. It is also grown in other provinces, such as Quebec and Nova Scotia, and in the eastern United States, in states such as New York, Michigan, Pennsylvania, Virginia and Ohio. To a lesser extent it has been planted in other cold-climate countries.
