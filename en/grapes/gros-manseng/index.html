Gros Manseng

A white grape from the French Pyrenees, more productive than Petit Manseng, which makes dry whites with lively acidity and aromas of citrus and tropical fruit in Jurançon and Gascony.

Origin

Gros Manseng comes, like Petit Manseng, from the Pyrénées-Atlantiques in south-west France. Despite the shared name, it is not a large-berried form of Petit Manseng but a different variety, although the two have long been grown together in Béarn and the French Basque Country. Today it is the more widely planted of the pair.

Viticulture

It has larger bunches and berries than Petit Manseng and is considerably more productive, which has made it more profitable for growers. It keeps very high acidity and ripens well in the mild, humid climates of the south-west, although it accumulates less sugar than its namesake, so it gives fewer highly concentrated sweet wines. Its skin is relatively thick and it stands up reasonably well to disease.

In the glass

It mostly makes dry, lively, aromatic whites, with notes of grapefruit, lemon, pineapple, passion fruit, quince and white flowers, and sometimes a spicy touch. Their acidity makes them very refreshing when young and allows them to last a few years in bottle. It is also used in medium-dry and sweet wines, on its own or blended with Petit Manseng, although the most concentrated examples usually come from the latter.

Where it is grown

It is the main variety of dry Jurançon and also goes into sweet Jurançon, Pacherenc du Vic-Bilh and Irouléguy. Its great recent spread is due to the Côtes de Gascogne IGP, where it is often blended with Colombard, Ugni Blanc and Sauvignon Blanc in fresh, fruity whites, or bottled on its own in dry and medium-dry versions. It is also used in Béarn and other appellations of the south-west, and is found in small plantings in other parts of France. Outside its home region its presence is very limited and almost experimental, so it remains essentially a grape of south-west France.

At the table

The dry whites go with shellfish, grilled fish, Basque cooking, charcuterie and Pyrenean ewe’s-milk cheeses; the medium-dry wines suit lightly spiced dishes. Serve at 8–10 °C.
