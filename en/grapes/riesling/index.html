White grape

Riesling

Germany’s great white grape, with vibrant acidity and strong expression of terroir, capable of dry and sweet wines of great longevity.

Origin

Riesling comes from the Rhine Valley in Germany, where it is documented from the fifteenth century. Genetic studies point to Gouais Blanc as one of its parents. It should not be confused with Riesling Italico or Welschriesling, a different and not closely related variety.

Viticulture

It buds and ripens late, has hard wood and is very resistant to winter frost, which allows it to thrive in northern climates. It needs a long ripening period and benefits from well-oriented slopes, such as the steep slate slopes of the Mosel, where sunlight and the warmth of the soil help ripening. It keeps high acidity even at high sugar levels. It is susceptible to rot, although noble rot is harnessed to make sweet wines.

In the glass

It gives wines with vibrant acidity and a light to medium body, with aromas ranging from white flowers, green apple, lime and peach in young wines to honey and candied fruit in sweet ones. It is highly expressive of soil, and with age it often develops a characteristic petrol or kerosene note. Styles range from dry wines to sweet late-harvest wines, botrytised wines and ice wines (Eiswein). In Germany, quality wines are classified by grape ripeness into categories such as Kabinett, Spätlese, Auslese, Beerenauslese and Trockenbeerenauslese. Its balance of acidity and sugar gives it exceptional longevity.

Where it is grown

Germany has the largest area, with the Mosel, Rheingau, Pfalz and Nahe as leading regions. In France it is one of the great varieties of Alsace, and in Austria it shines in the Wachau, Kamptal and Kremstal. Australia has made the dry Rieslings of the Clare and Eden Valleys famous, and it is also grown in New Zealand, the United States (Finger Lakes in New York, Washington State) and Italy (Alto Adige). In Spain it is grown only on a small scale.
