Bonarda

Argentina’s second red grape after Malbec, identical to the Douce Noir of Savoie and California’s Charbono, giving fruity, deeply coloured reds with soft tannins.

Origin

According to DNA analysis, the grape called Bonarda in Argentina is the Douce Noir of Savoie, in the French Alps, also known as Corbeau and, in California, as Charbono. It reached Argentina with nineteenth-century European immigration, and its name stems from an old confusion with the Italian grapes called Bonarda. It should not be confused with them: in Piedmont, Bonarda Piemontese is a different variety, and the Bonarda wine of Oltrepò Pavese, in Lombardy, is made from Croatina. In France, Douce Noir has almost disappeared.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous, productive, late-ripening vine that needs a long, warm growing season. For decades in Argentina it was planted in flat, warm areas, trained on pergolas (parral) and cropped heavily, for table wines and to add colour to blends. With moderate yields, from old vines or in somewhat cooler areas, it gives more concentrated wines.

In the glass

It gives deeply coloured, purple reds, medium-bodied, with moderate to fresh acidity and soft tannins. The aromas recall plum, black cherry, blackberry and fig, with floral violet notes and spicy or herbal touches. Most are made to be drunk young, for their fruit and easy appeal, although some producers offer oak-aged versions from selected vineyards. It is also blended with Malbec and other varieties.

Where it is grown

Argentina holds most of the world’s plantings, mainly in the province of Mendoza, particularly eastern Mendoza and San Rafael, and to a lesser extent in San Juan. For a long time it was the country’s most planted red grape, before Malbec overtook it. In California, as Charbono, it survives in small plots, mainly in the Napa Valley, where a few producers keep it as a historical curiosity.

At the table

Its juicy reds go well with Argentine asado, empanadas, pasta with tomato sauce, pizza and charcuterie. They are served at 15–17 °C.
