White grape

Arinto

A Portuguese white with very high acidity and citrus aromas, the star of Bucelas and grown across almost the whole country, where it brings freshness to blends.

Origin

Arinto is one of Portugal’s oldest and most widespread white varieties. It is traditionally associated with Bucelas, a small appellation north of Lisbon whose dry whites were already appreciated in the nineteenth century, including in England. Its exact origin is unclear, but it has been grown for centuries in various regions of the country. In the Vinho Verde region it is known as Pedernã.

Viticulture

It is a vine of medium to high vigour and medium to late ripening, with compact bunches. Its most valuable trait is its ability to retain very high acidity even in hot, dry areas, which has made it a very useful tool for winemakers in the Alentejo and other southern regions. It adapts to varied soils, from limestone and clay to granite and schist, and is somewhat susceptible to powdery mildew and to rot in wet years.

In the glass

It makes lemon-yellow whites, light- to medium-bodied with vibrant acidity, with aromas of lemon, lime, grapefruit, green apple and white flowers, and a pronounced mineral or saline sensation. Young, it is very fresh and racy; with a few years in bottle, especially in Bucelas, it develops notes of honey, wax and nuts without losing tension. Its acidity also makes it suitable as a base for sparkling wine, for example in Bairrada, and for wines aged on lees or in oak.

Where it is grown

It is found in almost every Portuguese region. It is the main grape of the Bucelas DOC in the Lisboa region and is widely grown elsewhere in Lisboa, in the Tejo, Bairrada, the Alentejo, the Setúbal Peninsula and, as Pedernã, in Vinho Verde. It is often blended with more aromatic or lower-acid varieties such as Fernão Pires, Antão Vaz or Loureiro. It is rare outside Portugal.

At the table

Its acidity and saline character suit grilled fish, shellfish, salt cod, oysters and dishes with lemon or herbs. Serve at 8–10 °C.
