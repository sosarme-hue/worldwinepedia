Sauvignon Blanc

An aromatic, high-acid white grape, emblem of Sancerre and Pouilly-Fumé, which won worldwide fame with the wines of Marlborough, New Zealand.

Origin

Sauvignon Blanc comes from France, probably from the Loire Valley or the south-west. DNA analysis shows it to be closely related to Savagnin (Traminer), and it is a parent of Cabernet Sauvignon, born of its cross with Cabernet Franc. Its name is usually linked to the French sauvage, “wild”, from the appearance of its leaves.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous vine that buds late and ripens early, adapting well to cool and moderate climates. Controlling vigour and the exposure of the bunches is essential to its aromatic profile. It is susceptible to powdery mildew and grey rot, although in Bordeaux noble rot is harnessed to make sweet wines. It prefers limestone, flint or gravel soils.

In the glass

It gives pale wines with high acidity and a light to medium body, with very recognisable aromas: freshly cut grass, tomato leaf, gooseberry, green pepper and boxwood (due partly to pyrazines and thiols), along with citrus such as grapefruit and lime, passion fruit or peach. In the Loire, mineral and flinty notes stand out; in New Zealand, intense fruit and herbal character. It is generally drunk young, though barrel-fermented versions can age. In Bordeaux it is blended with Sémillon for the dry whites of Graves and Pessac-Léognan and for the sweet wines of Sauternes and Barsac.

Where it is grown

In France its benchmarks are Sancerre, Pouilly-Fumé, Menetou-Salon and Touraine in the Loire, and Bordeaux. New Zealand, through Marlborough, made it its flagship variety from the 1980s. It is also prominent in Chile (Casablanca, Leyda), South Africa, Australia (Adelaide Hills), the United States, where it is sometimes sold as Fumé Blanc, Austria (Styria) and Italy (Friuli, Alto Adige). In Spain it is grown in Rueda, where it is an authorised variety, and in other areas such as Penedès and La Mancha.
