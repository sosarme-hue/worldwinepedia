Pedro Ximénez

An Andalusian white grape, queen of Montilla-Moriles, which gives dry finos and olorosos as well as the famous sweet wine made from sun-dried grapes.

Origin

Pedro Ximénez is an Andalusian variety grown for centuries in Córdoba and Málaga. A widespread legend credits its introduction to a soldier named Pedro Ximén or Peter Siemens, who supposedly brought it from the Rhine in the sixteenth century, but there is no evidence for this and the variety has no known relationship with German grapes.

Viticulture

It ripens early and is well adapted to the intense heat of inland Andalusia. Its berries are thin-skinned, which makes sun-drying (asoleo) easier: after harvest, the bunches are spread out on mats in the sun for several days so that they shrivel and concentrate their sugar. In Montilla-Moriles it grows on white limestone soils known as albero or albariza.

In the glass

It is a versatile grape. In Montilla-Moriles its high sugar content means it naturally reaches high alcohol levels, so the area’s finos and amontillados often need no fortification; they are aged under flor or oxidatively in criaderas and soleras. Sun-dried grapes give the sweet Pedro Ximénez wine, dark mahogany and almost opaque, with a dense texture and aromas of raisin, fig, date, coffee, chocolate and liquorice. Very long-aged versions gain complexity and relative freshness.

Where it is grown

Its main area is the Montilla-Moriles D.O., where it is the dominant variety, and it is important in Málaga and the Axarquía. In the Jerez area it is scarcely grown, and sweet Pedro Ximénez sherries are often made from grapes or must brought from Montilla-Moriles. Outside Spain it is grown in Chile, especially in the north, where it is also used to make pisco, in Argentina and in Australia.

At the table

Montilla-Moriles finos go with appetisers, ham, nuts and fried fish, and the olorosos with stews and meat. Sweet Pedro Ximénez is enjoyed with desserts, vanilla ice cream or blue cheese, or on its own after a meal.
