Clairette

An old white grape from southern France, low in acidity with floral and aniseed aromas, found in the whites of the Rhône and Provence, in Clairette du Languedoc and in the sparkling wines of Die.

Origin

Clairette is one of the oldest white varieties of southern France, grown for centuries in the Languedoc, Provence and the Rhône Valley. Its name, which alludes to clarity, has also been applied to other grapes, so it should be distinguished from varieties with similar names. For a long time it was used to make white wines and vermouths in the Midi.

Viticulture

It is a vine adapted to heat and drought, with an upright habit and late ripening. It accumulates a lot of sugar, giving wines high in alcohol, but its acidity is low. Its berries are prone to oxidation, so the wine requires careful vinification, protected from oxygen, if fresh whites are the aim. It prefers dry, stony or limestone soils.

In the glass

It makes medium- to full-bodied whites, with aromas of white flowers, apple, peach, fennel and aniseed, and a soft texture. It tends to oxidise quickly, which in the past was turned to advantage in oxidative-style wines; today it is more often made in a fresh style for drinking young or blended with more acidic varieties. In blends it contributes volume, perfume and a faintly aniseed background.

Where it is grown

It is one of the white varieties permitted in Châteauneuf-du-Pape and Côtes du Rhône, and in Provence it is important in the whites of Cassis, alongside Marsanne, and of Bellet and Palette. In the Languedoc it gives its name to the Clairette du Languedoc appellation, which produces dry whites as well as sweet and rancio-style wines. In the Drôme valley, Crémant de Die, a traditional-method sparkling wine, is made mainly from Clairette, while the better-known Clairette de Die, sweet and made by the ancestral method, is made chiefly from Muscat with a proportion of Clairette. Outside France, under the name Clairette Blanche, it is grown in South Africa, where old vineyards survive.

At the table

Its whites go with Mediterranean fish, bouillabaisse, roast vegetables, herb-scented cooking and goat’s cheeses. The sparkling wines of Die suit fruit desserts. Serve at 8–11 °C.
