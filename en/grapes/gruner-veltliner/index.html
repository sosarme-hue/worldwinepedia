White grape

Grüner Veltliner

Austria’s signature white grape and its most widely planted variety: whites with lively acidity, green fruit and a characteristic touch of white pepper.

Origin

Grüner Veltliner is Austria’s signature white variety and the most widely planted grape in the country. DNA analysis has shown that it is a natural cross between Savagnin (Traminer) and an old vine named St. Georgen, of which only a single surviving plant was found near St. Georgen am Leithagebirge, in Burgenland. Despite its name, it is not related to Roter Veltliner, another Austrian variety. In everyday speech it is often simply called “Grüner”.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous, productive vine that ripens mid- to late season, so controlling yields is decisive for quality. It is particularly well suited to the deep, fertile loess soils of the Danube valley and its tributaries, while in the Wachau it is grown on terraces of primary rock (gneiss and schist) held up by dry-stone walls. It needs summers with cool nights to retain its acidity and aroma.

In the glass

Its most recognisable trait is a spicy note of white pepper, which Austrians call Pfefferl, together with aromas of green apple, pear, citrus and herbs, and sometimes vegetal notes reminiscent of green beans or lentils. It has lively acidity and a body ranging from light to full depending on origin and ripeness. Light, young wines, typical of the wine taverns known as Heurigen, sit alongside concentrated whites from hillside vineyards and named single sites (Ried), which can develop in bottle for many years towards notes of honey, nuts and toast. Sparkling wines (Sekt) are also made.

Where it is grown

In Austria it dominates Lower Austria (Niederösterreich). The Weinviertel, the country’s largest wine region, was the first to adopt the DAC appellation system, with a fresh, peppery style of Grüner Veltliner; the variety is also prominent in Kamptal, Kremstal, Wagram and the Wachau. There, the Vinea Wachau association classifies its dry wines by ripeness as Steinfeder, Federspiel and Smaragd, from the lightest to the most concentrated. Outside Austria it is grown in Slovakia, the Czech Republic and Hungary, and on a small scale in the United States, New Zealand and Australia, where it has attracted interest in cool areas.

At the table

Its acidity and peppery touch make it a very versatile white: it goes with Austrian cooking such as Wiener Schnitzel, with vegetables that are hard to pair, like artichoke or asparagus, and with fish, Asian dishes and herb-led recipes. Young wines are served at 8–10 °C and more concentrated ones at 10–12 °C.
