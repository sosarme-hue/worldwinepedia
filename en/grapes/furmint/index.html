White grape

Furmint

Hungary’s great white grape and the main variety of Tokaj, with very high acidity and a strong susceptibility to noble rot. It gives both extremely long-lived sweet wines and taut, mineral dry whites.

Origin

Furmint has been associated for centuries with the Tokaj region, in north-eastern Hungary, where it is considered native, although it has also traditionally been grown in other parts of the Carpathian Basin and the former Habsburg monarchy. Genetic analyses link it to Gouais Blanc (Heunisch), an old Central European variety that is among the parents of many grapes, and in turn identify it as a parent of Hárslevelű. After phylloxera and during the socialist period it lost ground to more productive varieties, but since the 1990s it has regained its leading role.

Viticulture

It is an early-budding, late-ripening variety that needs long autumns to complete its cycle. Its thin-skinned berries are very susceptible to Botrytis cinerea: in favourable conditions, with humid mornings and dry afternoons, they develop noble rot and shrivel on the vine, concentrating sugar and acidity. It keeps very high acidity even at high sugar levels, which explains the freshness and longevity of its sweet wines. It adapts well to volcanic soils and is susceptible to powdery mildew and frost.

In the glass

Dry, it gives medium- to full-bodied wines with vibrant acidity, aromas of apple, quince, pear, citrus and white flowers and often smoky or mineral notes; with ageing in wood or bottle, hints of honey, nuts and spice appear. In sweet wines such as Tokaji Aszú, it combines with notes of apricot, candied orange peel, honey, tea and dried fruit, supported by its acidity. It ages very well.

Where it is grown

It is the main variety of Tokaj, in both the Hungarian and the Slovak parts, and is also grown in other Hungarian regions, such as Somló hill and the area around Lake Balaton. In Slovenia it is known as Šipon, above all in the Štajerska region, and in Croatia as Pušipel or Moslavac. In Austria, in Burgenland, it has been revived around Rust, where it was part of the local sweet wine tradition. Outside Central Europe its presence is very limited.
