White grape

Torrontés

Argentina’s signature white grape, born in the country from crosses involving Muscat of Alexandria: highly aromatic, floral, dry wines at their best in the high valleys of Salta.

Origin

In Argentina the name Torrontés covers three distinct varieties, all of them born in South America: Torrontés Riojano, Torrontés Sanjuanino and Torrontés Mendocino. DNA analysis has shown that Riojano and Sanjuanino are natural crosses between Muscat of Alexandria and Listán Prieto (called Criolla Chica in Argentina and País in Chile), two grapes brought by Spanish colonists. Mendocino shares Muscat of Alexandria with them as a parent. They are unrelated to the Galician Torrontés of Ribeiro, which is a different variety. Torrontés Riojano is the most widely planted and the finest of the three.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous, productive vine, traditionally trained on high pergolas (parral), and it needs careful management of yields and bunch exposure to keep its freshness. It is well suited to the dry, sunny climate of western Argentina, with a wide temperature range between day and night. Picking at the right moment is crucial, because when overripe it loses acidity and can develop a bitter finish.

In the glass

Its defining feature is aromatic intensity, inherited from Muscat: rose, jasmine, orange blossom, geranium, peach, lychee and citrus. Yet most wines are made dry, so a nose that suggests sweetness contrasts with a dry, medium-bodied palate with moderate acidity and sometimes a slightly bitter finish. It is made to be drunk young, although there are also late-harvest versions, sparkling wines and some lees-aged wines.

Where it is grown

Its most renowned area is the Calchaquí Valleys of Salta, around Cafayate, where the vineyards lie at high altitude and intense sunlight is balanced by cold nights. It is also grown in the Argentine province of La Rioja, especially the Famatina valley, and in Catamarca, San Juan and Mendoza. Outside Argentina its presence is very limited, with small plantings in Chile and Uruguay.

At the table

Its perfume suits spiced or mildly hot dishes, Asian cooking, empanadas from Salta, ceviche, fish and fresh goat’s cheese. It also works as an aperitif. It is served at 7–9 °C.
