Rondinella

A red grape from Verona, an offspring of Corvina, which accompanies it in Valpolicella, Amarone and Bardolino, contributing colour, fruit and reliability.

Origin

Rondinella is a red grape from the province of Verona, in Veneto, traditionally grown on the hills of Valpolicella and on the eastern shore of Lake Garda. DNA analysis has shown that it is an offspring of Corvina, the area’s main grape, crossed with a variety that has not been identified. Its name is often linked to the swallow (rondine in Italian), perhaps because of the colour of its berries or the birds’ fondness for its bunches, although neither explanation has been proven.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous, productive vine with regular yields, much appreciated by growers for its disease resistance and its ability to ripen well even in difficult vintages. Its berries have fairly thick skins that resist rot well, which makes it very well suited to appassimento, the drying of grapes after harvest practised to make Amarone and Recioto della Valpolicella. It is traditionally trained on the Veronese pergola, on the limestone, clay and sometimes volcanic soils of the Valpolicella hills or the morainic soils of Bardolino.

In the glass

It is rarely made on its own. By itself it gives ruby-coloured, medium-bodied wines with soft tannins and aromas of red cherry, plum, red berries and herbs, sometimes with a hint of pepper. Its role in the blend is to complement Corvina: it adds colour, fruit, reliable production and sugar in dried grapes, though it has less perfume and finesse than Corvina.

Where it is grown

It is grown almost exclusively in the province of Verona. It is one of the traditional varieties of the Valpolicella, Valpolicella Ripasso, Amarone della Valpolicella and Recioto della Valpolicella denominations, together with Corvina, Corvinone and Molinara, and it also forms part of Bardolino and the rosé Chiaretto. Outside this area it is very rarely found.

At the table

The wines it goes into range from light reds, for cured meats, pasta and white meat, to powerful Amarones, which call for red meat, game, stews and aged cheeses. Serve light reds at 14–16 °C and more structured ones at 16–18 °C.
