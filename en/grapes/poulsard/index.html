Poulsard

A very thin-skinned red grape from the Jura that gives pale, almost translucent reds, delicate and aromatic, with notes of red fruit, rose and spice.

Origin

Poulsard is a red variety native to the Jura in eastern France, and it is the most widely planted red grape of the region, ahead of Trousseau and Pinot Noir. In the village of Pupillin, near Arbois, it is traditionally known as Ploussard, a name that often appears on labels. Outside the Jura and the neighbouring Bugey area it is hardly grown at all.

Viticulture

It buds early, which exposes it to spring frost, and it is susceptible to several fungal diseases and to rot, so it demands an attentive grower. Its berries are elongated and very thin-skinned, with little colouring matter. It prefers the marls of the Jura, clay and limestone soils that retain water, and it ripens earlier than Trousseau.

In the glass

Because of its thin skin it makes very pale reds, sometimes almost pink or orange in hue, which surprise anyone expecting a conventional red. In the glass they are light, with soft tannins and fresh acidity, and aromas of redcurrant, raspberry, sour cherry, rose petals, spice and earthy or smoky notes. Despite their lightness, the best show remarkable complexity. It is much prized by producers of minimal-intervention wines, who often make it without added sulphites or with long macerations.

Where it is grown

It is made on its own or with Trousseau and Pinot Noir in the Arbois, Côtes du Jura and other Jura appellations, and it is especially characteristic of Arbois and Pupillin. It is also used in Crémant du Jura, in Vin de Paille alongside Chardonnay and Savagnin, and in Macvin du Jura, a liqueur wine made from grape must and local brandy. In the Bugey it goes, together with Gamay, into the sweet pink sparkling wine of Cerdon. Outside these areas its presence is minimal and almost symbolic.

At the table

Its light reds go with charcuterie, meat pies, poultry, freshwater fish, mushrooms and mountain cheeses such as Comté. Serve slightly cool, at 12–15 °C.
