Chenin Blanc

A white grape of vibrant acidity from the Loire, able to make long-lived dry, sparkling and sweet wines; it is South Africa’s most planted white variety.

Origin

Chenin Blanc comes from the middle Loire Valley, particularly Anjou and Touraine, where it has been recorded for centuries and is also known as Pineau de la Loire. Genetic studies link it to Savagnin, and it is itself a parent of other French varieties. Dutch settlers took it to South Africa in the seventeenth century, where it became known as Steen.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous vine that buds early and ripens late, so it needs long autumns. Its most striking trait is high natural acidity, which it keeps even in warm climates. In the Loire ripening is uneven within the same plot, so the grapes are often picked in several passes (tries). It is prone to rot, and in favourable years the noble form of Botrytis cinerea makes great sweet wines possible.

In the glass

Few grapes offer such a range of styles. Dry wines show apple, pear, quince, citrus and white flowers, with a texture that can recall beeswax or lanolin. Off-dry and sweet versions add honey, apricot and candied fruit, always balanced by acidity. It is the base of sparkling wines such as Crémant de Loire and sparkling Vouvray. The finest wines develop for decades towards honey, nuts and toast.

Where it is grown

In France its emblematic appellations are Vouvray and Montlouis-sur-Loire in Touraine, Savennières in Anjou, and the sweet wines of Coteaux du Layon, Bonnezeaux and Quarts de Chaume, as well as Saumur. South Africa has the largest area in the world, with wines ranging from fresh, affordable whites to carefully made old-vine wines from Swartland or Stellenbosch. It is also grown in California, Argentina, Australia and New Zealand.

At the table

Dry Chenin suits fish in sauce, poultry, pork and lightly spiced Asian cooking; sweet versions go with foie gras, blue cheeses and fruit desserts. Serve at 8–12 °C depending on style.
