Black grape

Saperavi

Georgia’s great red grape, one of the few quality varieties with coloured flesh. It gives very dark, acidic and structured wines, both dry and naturally semi-sweet, and withstands cold well.

Origin

Saperavi is a native Georgian variety, traditionally linked to the Kakheti region in the east of the country. Its name means something like “dye” or “for dyeing” in Georgian, alluding to its most striking feature: unlike most red grapes, whose flesh is colourless, Saperavi has coloured flesh, that is, it is a teinturier variety. It is the country’s most widely planted red grape and, together with the white Rkatsiteli, its main hallmark.

Viticulture

It is a late-ripening vine with steady yields that keeps high acidity even at full ripeness. It stands out for its good resistance to winter cold, which has allowed it to be grown in areas with harsh continental climates, from the Caucasus to Eastern Europe. It needs controlled yields to give concentrated wines, and in warm climates it easily reaches high alcohol levels.

In the glass

It gives very deeply coloured, almost opaque wines, with aromas of black fruit such as blackberry, plum and black cherry, together with notes of liquorice, spice, chocolate, tobacco and sometimes herbs. On the palate it combines body, firm tannins and lively acidity that gives it freshness and ageing potential. It is made in very different styles: dry reds fermented in qvevri or in tank, barrel-aged reds, rosés and, in Kakheti, the naturally semi-sweet reds of appellations such as Kindzmarauli and Akhasheni, in which fermentation is stopped to retain some of the sugar. With time in bottle it develops notes of leather, dried fruit and forest floor.

Where it is grown

Its heartland is Kakheti, where Mukuzani, Napareuli, Kindzmarauli and Akhasheni, among others, are made, but it is grown in almost every wine region of Georgia. Outside the country it is found in Moldova, Ukraine, Russia, Armenia and other countries of the former Soviet Union. Its cold resistance has attracted the interest of producers in the Finger Lakes region of New York State, and there are also small plantings in Australia. In many places it is also used to add colour to blends.
