Alicante Bouschet

A red-fleshed teinturier grape bred in France in the nineteenth century, known in Spain as Garnacha Tintorera and highly regarded today in the Alentejo.

Origin

Alicante Bouschet was bred in the Languedoc in France by the nurseryman Henri Bouschet in the second half of the nineteenth century. It is a cross between Petit Bouschet, created by his father Louis Bouschet from Aramon and Teinturier du Cher, and Grenache, which in France was then also known as Alicante. The aim was a productive grape able to add colour to the pale wines of the Midi. Unlike almost every Vitis vinifera variety, its flesh and juice are red: it is a teinturier grape.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous, productive vine of medium ripening that adapts to warm climates and a wide range of soils. After phylloxera it was planted extensively in southern France, Spain and North Africa. In the United States it became popular during Prohibition, because its thick-skinned grapes travelled well by rail and its deeply coloured juice let home winemakers make wine. Today it is valued in warm areas for its colour, concentration and drought resistance.

In the glass

It makes very dark, almost opaque wines, medium- to full-bodied, with firm tannins and moderate acidity. Its aromas recall blackberry, black plum, cherry liqueur, liquorice and spice, with notes of herbs, chocolate or earth in oak-aged wines. For a long time it was used mainly as a blending grape to boost colour, but in some regions it is made into quality single-varietal reds with long ageing potential.

Where it is grown

In Portugal it is a leading variety in the Alentejo, where some historic estates have shown its potential for age-worthy reds. In Spain, as Garnacha Tintorera, it is grown mainly in Castilla-La Mancha (Almansa, Manchuela), the Valencia region, Murcia and Galicia. In France its area has shrunk considerably. It is also found in California, Chile, Italy and other countries.

At the table

It goes with roast red meat, Iberian pork, game stews and aged cheeses. Serve at 16–18 °C.
