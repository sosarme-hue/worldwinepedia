Nerello Mascalese

The great red grape of Etna, making pale, high-acid wines with fine tannins and volcanic character, often compared with Pinot Noir or Nebbiolo.

Origin

Nerello Mascalese takes its name from the Mascali plain, between the eastern slopes of Etna and the Ionian Sea in the province of Catania, where it has been grown for centuries. DNA analysis suggests that it is a natural cross between Sangiovese and Mantonico Bianco, an old white grape from Calabria. Etna lost much of its vineyard during the twentieth century, but since the early twenty-first century the area has enjoyed a strong revival.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous, late-ripening vine: on Etna the harvest usually takes place in October. It thrives on the volcano’s slopes, from about 400 to over 1,000 metres above sea level, on poor, well-drained sandy soils of volcanic origin. Many old bush vines (alberello) survive there, some on their own roots, because phylloxera struggles to spread in these sandy soils. Wide differences between day and night temperatures preserve its acidity.

In the glass

It makes pale ruby wines that turn brick-hued with age, medium-bodied, with high acidity and fine but firm tannins. Its aromas recall cherry, raspberry, wild strawberry, dried flowers, herbs and spice, with smoky, earthy or ashy notes often associated with its volcanic origin. The best wines develop over years towards leather, tea and forest floor. Rosé and sparkling wines are also made.

Where it is grown

Its benchmark area is Etna, where it is the main grape of Etna Rosso DOC, often blended with a small proportion of Nerello Cappuccio; producers increasingly highlight the contrade, specific named sites on the northern slope. It is also grown in the province of Messina, in the Faro DOC, and in other parts of north-eastern Sicily and Calabria. It is practically absent outside Italy.

At the table

Thanks to its acidity and finesse it suits firm-fleshed fish such as tuna, white meats, mushrooms, pasta with light ragù and semi-hard cheeses. Serve at 15–17 °C.
