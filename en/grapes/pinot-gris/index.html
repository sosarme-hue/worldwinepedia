Pinot Gris

A greyish-skinned mutation of Pinot that, depending on where it is grown, gives rich, spicy whites (Alsace) or light, crisp ones (Italian Pinot Grigio).

Origin

Pinot Gris is not an independent variety but a colour mutation of Pinot, the same family that includes Pinot Noir and Pinot Blanc. Genetically they are practically identical, differing only in skin pigmentation, which in Pinot Gris ranges from bluish grey to coppery pink. It originated in Burgundy and spread across central Europe in the Middle Ages. In Germany it was called Ruländer after the merchant Johann Seger Ruland, who propagated it in the Palatinate in the early eighteenth century.

Viticulture

Like Pinot Noir it buds early and has small, compact bunches, which make it prone to rot. It ripens relatively early and easily reaches high sugar levels, though its acidity is moderate. The final style depends greatly on yield and harvest date: picked early from high-yielding vines it makes light, neutral wines; from low yields at full ripeness it produces full-bodied whites.

In the glass

Typical Italian Pinot Grigio is dry, light and crisp, with notes of pear, apple, lemon and almond. In Alsace, by contrast, Pinot Gris makes broad, unctuous wines, sometimes with a touch of sweetness, with aromas of peach, ripe pear, honey, spice and a smoky edge; Vendanges Tardives and Sélection de Grains Nobles are also made. Friuli has the “ramato” style, in which brief skin contact gives the wine a copper hue.

Where it is grown

Italy is one of the largest producers, especially in the Veneto, Friuli and Trentino-Alto Adige. In France its stronghold is Alsace, where it is one of the four varieties permitted in the Grand Cru wines. Germany (as Grauburgunder) grows it in Baden and the Pfalz, and it is also found in Austria, Hungary (Szürkebarát), Switzerland (Malvoisie in the Valais), Oregon, New Zealand and Australia.

At the table

Light styles suit aperitifs, fish and salads; richer Alsace wines go with foie gras, white meats in sauce and Asian dishes. Serve at 8–12 °C.
