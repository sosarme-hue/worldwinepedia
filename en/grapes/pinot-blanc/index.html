Pinot Blanc

The white mutation of the Pinot family, with discreet aromas and a soft texture, widely grown in Alsace, Germany, Austria and northern Italy for fresh whites and sparkling wines.

Origin

Pinot Blanc comes from Burgundy and is a colour mutation of Pinot: it shares its DNA with Pinot Noir and Pinot Gris and differs from them only in the pigmentation of its skin. For a long time it was confused with Chardonnay, which it resembles somewhat in the vineyard, and in some places the two were planted together. Today it is barely present in Burgundy, but it has become established in Alsace, the German-speaking world and northern Italy.

Viticulture

It is a mid-budding, mid-ripening variety, more productive than Pinot Noir and fairly consistent. It prefers cool climates and limestone or marl soils, where it keeps moderate acidity. Because its bunches are compact, it is prone to rot in wet autumns, and excessive yields give neutral wines.

In the glass

It makes medium-bodied whites with a discreet aroma and soft texture, with notes of apple, pear, almond, white flowers and sometimes a smoky touch. Its acidity is lower than that of Riesling and its profile more restrained than that of Chardonnay, which makes it a versatile, easy-drinking white. The best examples, from old vines or aged in large casks, gain texture and develop well for several years. It is also a valued base for sparkling wine.

Where it is grown

In Alsace it is the base of much Crémant d’Alsace; moreover, wines labelled Pinot Blanc there may include Auxerrois, a different variety. In Germany, as Weissburgunder, it is important in Baden and the Pfalz, and in Austria it is made dry and also in sweet wines from Burgenland. In Italy, as Pinot Bianco, it stands out in Alto Adige and Friuli and is permitted in Franciacorta. It is also grown in Slovenia, Hungary, British Columbia in Canada, and Oregon.

At the table

Its discretion makes it very useful with food: it goes with vegetables, asparagus, freshwater fish, savoury tarts, white meat and mild cheeses. Serve at 8–11 °C.
