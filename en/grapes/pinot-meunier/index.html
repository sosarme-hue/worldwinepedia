Pinot Meunier

A red grape of the Pinot Noir family and one of the three main varieties of Champagne, valued for its fruit, its roundness and its resistance to spring frost.

Origin

Pinot Meunier, today often called simply Meunier, is a mutation of Pinot Noir. Its name means ‘miller’ in French and refers to the dense white down that covers the underside of its leaves and its young shoots, which look as if they had been dusted with flour. Genetic studies have shown that it is a chimera: only the outer cell layers of the plant have mutated, while the inner layers keep the genetics of Pinot Noir. In Germany it is known as Schwarzriesling or Müllerrebe, although it has no connection whatever with Riesling.

Viticulture

Its great advantage is its growing cycle: it buds later and ripens earlier than Pinot Noir. That late budburst protects it from spring frost, so it has been planted above all in the coldest and most exposed parts of Champagne, such as the Marne Valley, and on heavier clay soils, where Pinot Noir and Chardonnay struggle more. It is a vigorous and fairly productive vine, more susceptible than Pinot Noir to some fungal diseases.

In the glass

In Champagne it contributes fruit, softness and quicker development than its partners, which makes it very useful in non-vintage blends intended to be drunk young. It was long regarded as a second-rank grape, but some houses value it in their prestige wines, and more and more growers make single-varietal Meunier Champagnes, with aromas of apple, cherry, raspberry and red plum, and notes of toast when aged on the lees. It also gives light, fruity reds, such as some Coteaux Champenois.

Where it is grown

Its main territory is Champagne, where it is one of the three principal varieties alongside Pinot Noir and Chardonnay. In Germany, as Schwarzriesling, it is traditional in Württemberg, where it makes light reds and rosés. It is also used for traditional-method sparkling wines in England, Australia, New Zealand and California, almost always blended with Pinot Noir and Chardonnay.

At the table

Sparkling wines with a high proportion of Meunier go well with aperitifs, poultry, fish and simple home cooking; the light reds suit charcuterie and white meat.
