Nero d’Avola

Sicily’s most widely planted red grape, with deep colour, ripe fruit and good acidity, found in simple wines and in oak-aged reds, and part of Cerasuolo di Vittoria.

Origin

Nero d’Avola (“black of Avola”) takes its name from the town of Avola in south-eastern Sicily, near Syracuse, which is considered its birthplace. It is also called Calabrese, a name that has been read as a reference to Calabria, although there is no evidence that it came from there and its history is tied above all to Sicily. For a long time it was used as a blending wine, shipped in bulk to northern Italy and France to add colour and body to lighter wines.

Viticulture

It is a vine suited to the heat and drought of the Sicilian climate, traditionally bush-trained (alberello), although trellising now predominates. It has medium vigour and ripens mid-season, and keeps notable acidity for a warm-climate variety. It responds well to yield control and to the choice of high-altitude or coastal sites, which bring freshness. It is somewhat prone to fungal diseases in wet years.

In the glass

It makes deep ruby wines, medium- to full-bodied, with soft to firm tannins and lively acidity, and aromas of black cherry, plum, blackberry, sour cherry, liquorice and spice, sometimes with notes of Mediterranean herbs and chocolate. Its style varies widely: there are young, fruity unoaked versions and more structured barrel-aged reds. Rosés are made too.

Where it is grown

It is grown throughout Sicily but above all in the south and south-east of the island, in the provinces of Syracuse, Ragusa and Agrigento. It is the base of red Sicilia DOC and of the Eloro DOC around Noto, and it is blended with Frappato in Cerasuolo di Vittoria DOCG, the island’s only DOCG. Outside Italy it has been planted in Australia, where it has attracted interest for its suitability to hot, dry climates.

At the table

It goes with Sicilian food: pasta alla Norma, caponata, grilled meat, cured meats and aged cheeses. Serve at 15–18 °C depending on style.
