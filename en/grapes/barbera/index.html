Barbera

Piedmont’s most widely planted red grape, with deep colour, high acidity and soft tannins; the grape of Barbera d’Asti, Barbera d’Alba and Nizza.

Origin

Barbera is native to Piedmont, probably to the hills of Monferrato, where it has been recorded since the late eighteenth century. Unlike Nebbiolo, it was long the grape of the region’s everyday wine: productive, easy to grow and able to give deeply coloured reds. After phylloxera it was replanted on a massive scale and became Piedmont’s most widespread red grape.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous, generous vine of medium ripening that adapts to varied soils and resists some diseases well. Its defining trait is very high natural acidity, which it keeps even in warm areas, combined with low tannin levels. If yields are not limited the wines are sharp and light, but on good slopes with moderate crops it achieves real concentration. In Piedmont it usually occupies the plots not reserved for Nebbiolo.

In the glass

It makes dark ruby wines with purple glints, medium-bodied, with juicy acidity and soft tannins. Its aromas recall cherry, sour cherry, plum, raspberry and spice. It can be made in a young, fruity style or aged in small barrels, a practice that spread from the 1980s and adds roundness and notes of vanilla and toast to a grape low in tannin.

Where it is grown

Its main appellations are Barbera d’Asti DOCG, Nizza DOCG (formerly a subzone of Barbera d’Asti), Barbera del Monferrato Superiore DOCG and Barbera d’Alba DOC. Outside Piedmont it is grown in the Oltrepò Pavese in Lombardy and in the hills around Piacenza in Emilia-Romagna, where it is sometimes made in a frizzante style. Italian emigrants took it to Argentina and California, and it is also found in Australia.

At the table

Its acidity makes it an excellent partner for rich dishes and tomato-based cooking: pasta with ragù, pizza, braised meat, cured meats and semi-hard cheeses. Serve at 15–17 °C. Young, unoaked versions can be served at the lower end of that range.
