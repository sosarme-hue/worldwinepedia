Petite Sirah

The Californian name for Durif, a French cross of Syrah and Peloursin: almost black, very tannic reds with dark fruit and spice and great ageing potential.

Origin

Petite Sirah is the name by which Durif is known in California, a variety bred in southern France in the late nineteenth century by the botanist François Durif in Montpellier. DNA analysis has confirmed that it is a cross between Syrah and Peloursin, an old variety from south-eastern France. Durif was looking for a red grape more resistant to disease; its compact bunches, however, made it prone to rot in humid climates, and in France it never spread and has now almost disappeared. Despite its name, it is not a “little Syrah”, although it is Syrah’s offspring.

California

It reached California in the late nineteenth century, and there the name Petite Sirah was used for several different vines planted in the same vineyards, including Durif, Peloursin and Syrah itself. Genetic studies carried out from the 1990s at the University of California, Davis, showed that the great majority of Californian Petite Sirah is Durif, although old vineyards still contain it interplanted with other varieties. For much of the twentieth century it was used mainly to add colour and structure to blends, often with Zinfandel.

Viticulture and the glass

It is a productive vine that ripens mid- to late season and adapts well to warm, dry climates. Its small berries, with a high ratio of skin to juice, give almost black wines with very marked tannins, noticeable acidity and a full body. The aromas recall blackberry, blueberry, plum and black-fruit jam, with notes of black pepper, liquorice, chocolate and, with age, leather and earth. The best examples can age for many years in bottle.

Where it is grown

The United States, and California in particular, holds most of the world’s plantings: it is grown in Lodi and the Central Valley, in Napa, Sonoma, Mendocino and Paso Robles, among other areas. In Australia, under the name Durif, it is prominent in Rutherglen (Victoria) and the Riverina (New South Wales), and there are also plantings in Israel and Mexico.

At the table

Its power calls for hearty dishes: barbecued meat, ribs, stews, game and aged cheeses. It is served at 16–18 °C and benefits from decanting.
