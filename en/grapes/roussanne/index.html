Roussanne

A white grape from the northern Rhône and the usual partner of Marsanne, giving aromatic, full-bodied, age-worthy wines with notes of pear, flowers and herbal tea.

Origin

Roussanne comes from the northern part of the Rhône Valley, where it has long been grown alongside Marsanne. Its name is linked to the russet colour, ‘roux’ in French, that the skins of its berries take on as they ripen. In Savoie it is known as Bergeron, the name under which it makes the highly regarded Chignin-Bergeron.

Viticulture

It is a delicate variety. It ripens late, its yields are irregular and it is susceptible to powdery mildew, rot and wind, which in the Rhône blows hard in the form of the mistral. Because of these difficulties many growers replaced it in the twentieth century with Marsanne, which is more productive and reliable, although the selection of healthier plant material has revived interest in it. It prefers poor, stony soils and warm exposures that allow it to ripen fully.

In the glass

It makes medium- to full-bodied whites, with livelier acidity than Marsanne and great aromatic complexity: pear, apricot, white flowers, honey, lime-blossom tea and herbal notes. Fermented or aged in barrel, it acquires nutty nuances and a creamy texture. The best wines age very well and may go through a closed phase before showing their full richness.

Where it is grown

In the northern Rhône it goes, almost always blended with Marsanne, into the whites of Hermitage, Crozes-Hermitage, Saint-Joseph and Saint-Péray. In the southern Rhône it is one of the white varieties of Châteauneuf-du-Pape, where some producers make whites with a large proportion of Roussanne, and it appears in Côtes du Rhône. It is also planted in the Languedoc, Provence and Savoie. Outside France it has been taken up by producers in California and other western American states, in Australia and in South Africa, often in Rhône-style blends with Marsanne, Viognier or Grenache Blanc.

At the table

It goes with fish in sauce, shellfish, roast poultry, veal, mushrooms and soft cheeses. Serve at 10–13 °C.
