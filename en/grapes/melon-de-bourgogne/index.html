Melon de Bourgogne

The grape of Muscadet, near the mouth of the Loire, making dry, light, saline whites that are often aged on their lees.

Origin

As its name suggests, Melon de Bourgogne was born in Burgundy. DNA analysis has shown that it is the offspring of Pinot and Gouais Blanc, like Chardonnay, Aligoté and Gamay. In Burgundy it was eventually almost eliminated, while in the Pays Nantais, near the mouth of the Loire, it spread after the devastating frosts of the early eighteenth century, which forced growers to replant with cold-resistant varieties. There it also took the name of the wine it makes: Muscadet.

Viticulture

It buds and ripens early and resists winter cold, but is susceptible to downy mildew, powdery mildew and grey rot. Its early ripening allows it to complete its cycle in the cool, damp oceanic climate of the lower Loire, where it is grown on a wide range of soils: gneiss, schist, granite and ancient volcanic rocks such as gabbro.

In the glass

It makes pale, light-bodied wines with moderate alcohol and lively acidity, with discreet aromas of lemon, green apple, pear and white flowers and a pronounced saline character. Much Muscadet is aged sur lie: the wine stays on its fine lees through the winter until bottling, which gives it more texture, bready or yeasty notes and sometimes a faint prickle. The cuvées of the crus communaux, with longer lees ageing, show remarkable ageing potential.

Where it is grown

Almost all of it is in the Pays Nantais, under the appellations Muscadet, Muscadet Sèvre et Maine (the largest), Muscadet Coteaux de la Loire and Muscadet Côtes de Grandlieu, together with crus communaux such as Clisson, Gorges and Le Pallet. Outside France there are small plantings in Oregon and elsewhere in the United States; in California it was discovered that some vines grown as Pinot Blanc were in fact Melon.

At the table

It is the classic partner for oysters, shellfish, white fish and seafood dishes. Serve chilled, at 8–10 °C.
