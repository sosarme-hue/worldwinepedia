Gamay

The grape of Beaujolais, giving brightly coloured reds with fresh red fruit and soft tannins; also found in the Loire, Savoie and Switzerland.

Origin

Gamay, whose full name is Gamay Noir à Jus Blanc (“with white juice”), was born in Burgundy. DNA analysis has shown that it is a natural cross between Pinot and Gouais Blanc, the same pair that gave rise to Chardonnay, Aligoté and Melon de Bourgogne. In 1395 Philip the Bold, Duke of Burgundy, ordered it uprooted from his lands, calling it a “disloyal” grape compared with Pinot Noir. This pushed it south to the granite soils of Beaujolais, where it found its true home.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous, productive vine that buds early and is therefore exposed to spring frost. It ripens early and does well on acidic granite and schist soils. In Beaujolais it is traditionally bush-trained and hand-harvested, because many producers use carbonic or semi-carbonic maceration with whole bunches, a technique that heightens fruity aromas and softens tannins.

In the glass

It gives bright purple-red wines, light to medium in body, with refreshing acidity and fine tannins. Strawberry, raspberry and cherry dominate, along with floral notes of violet and peony; carbonic maceration can add hints of banana or bubblegum. The ten Beaujolais crus, such as Morgon, Moulin-à-Vent and Fleurie, produce more structured wines that can age for several years and take on earthy nuances reminiscent of Burgundy.

Where it is grown

Most of the world’s Gamay is in France, above all in Beaujolais, though it is also grown in the Loire (Touraine, Anjou), the Côtes d’Auvergne and Savoie. In Switzerland it is the country’s second red grape, and in the Valais it is blended with Pinot Noir to make Dôle. Outside Europe there are plantings in Oregon and other cool parts of the United States, as well as in Canada and New Zealand.

At the table

Fresh and light, it is a very versatile red that suits charcuterie, poultry, soft cheeses and bistro dishes. It is usually served slightly cool, at 12–15 °C.
