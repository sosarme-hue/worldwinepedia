Mondeuse Noire

The emblematic red grape of Savoie, related to Syrah, giving deeply coloured wines with lively acidity and aromas of black fruit, violet and pepper.

Origin

Mondeuse Noire, often known simply as Mondeuse, is the main native red variety of Savoie in the French Alps, and is also grown in the neighbouring Bugey. Genetic studies link it to Syrah through Mondeuse Blanche, a white variety from the same area that is one of Syrah’s parents. For a long time it was thought to be identical to Italy’s Refosco, but DNA analysis has ruled out that identity. Before phylloxera it was widespread in Savoie; afterwards its area shrank considerably, and in recent decades it has enjoyed a revival led by producers interested in local varieties.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous, productive vine, so it needs rigorous pruning and yield control to give quality wines. It ripens in the second half of the season, so in Savoie it is planted on well-exposed slopes, often on limestone soils or mountain scree.

In the glass

It makes deeply coloured reds with lively acidity and tannins that can be firm in youth. Their aromas recall blackberry, plum, black cherry and violet, with marked notes of pepper, spice and sometimes an earthy or mountain-herb background. Wines from old vines and low yields can age well for several years, during which their tannins soften and more complex aromas appear. Simpler wines, by contrast, are drunk young for their fruit and freshness.

Where it is grown

In the Vin de Savoie appellation it is made on its own, sometimes with the name of a cru such as Arbin, known for its Mondeuse reds, or blended with Gamay and Pinot Noir. It is also grown in the Bugey and, on a limited scale, in Australia, especially in the state of Victoria. Outside these regions its presence is scarce.

At the table

It goes with mountain charcuterie, grilled meat, game, stews and pressed Alpine cheeses such as Beaufort or Tomme de Savoie. Serve at 15–17 °C.
