Tempranillo

Spain’s great red grape, the backbone of Rioja and Ribera del Duero, and also important in Portugal under the names Tinta Roriz and Aragonez.

Origin

Tempranillo is native to northern Spain and has been recorded for centuries in La Rioja and the Duero valley. Its name, meaning “little early one”, refers to its early ripening, several weeks ahead of Garnacha. DNA analysis shows it to be the offspring of a natural cross between Albillo Mayor and Benedicto, an old Aragonese variety that has almost disappeared.

Viticulture

It buds and ripens early, with compact bunches and thick-skinned berries. It thrives in continental climates with wide differences between day and night temperatures, which help it retain acidity and build colour. In very hot areas it tends to lose acidity, which is why it is often blended with Garnacha, Graciano or Mazuelo. It is susceptible to powdery mildew and to some grapevine trunk diseases.

In the glass

It produces deep ruby wines, medium- to full-bodied, with firm tannins and moderate acidity. Young, it shows red and black fruit (cherry, plum, blackberry). Its great affinity with oak has made it famous: barrel ageing adds notes of vanilla, coconut, spice, leather and tobacco, while long-aged Gran Reservas develop tertiary aromas of forest floor and dried fruit.

Where it is grown

It is the most widely planted red grape in Spain and goes by different names from region to region: Tinto Fino or Tinta del País in Ribera del Duero, Tinta de Toro in Toro, Cencibel in La Mancha and Valdepeñas, and Ull de Llebre in Catalonia. In Portugal it is one of the grapes of Port and Douro wines under the name Tinta Roriz, and in the Alentejo it is called Aragonez. Beyond the Iberian Peninsula it is grown in Argentina, the United States (California, Oregon, Washington, Texas) and Australia, where it has gained ground in recent decades.

At the table

Young Tempranillo reds go well with cured meats, rice dishes and grilled meat, while Crianzas and Reservas are traditionally paired with roast lamb, small game and aged sheep’s cheeses. Depending on style, they are served at 14–18 °C.
