Pinotage

South Africa’s own red grape, a cross of Pinot Noir and Cinsault, giving deeply coloured wines with dark fruit and smoky notes, and part of the so-called Cape Blend.

Origin

Pinotage was bred in 1925 by Abraham Izak Perold, the first professor of viticulture at Stellenbosch University, by crossing Pinot Noir with Cinsault, which was then known in South Africa as Hermitage; its name combines the two. Perold was looking for a red grape that would combine the finesse of Pinot Noir with the hardiness and productivity of Cinsault. The first commercial plantings came decades later, and today it is the red variety most closely identified with South Africa, as it is hardly grown anywhere else.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous vine that buds and ripens early and accumulates sugar quickly, so its wines often reach high alcohol levels. It is well suited to the Cape’s Mediterranean climate and is grown both on trellises and as bush vines; old, unirrigated bush vines are particularly prized. The timing of the harvest and yield control strongly influence its profile.

In the glass

It gives deeply coloured reds, medium- to full-bodied, with tannins that vary according to style. The aromas recall red and black fruit (plum, blackberry, cherry), with smoky, earthy and spicy notes and sometimes coffee or chocolate. For years it was criticised for aromas of acetone or banana, linked to overly warm fermentations, faults that better winemaking has greatly reduced. Styles vary widely: light, fruity reds, structured oak-aged wines that can age, rosés and even sparkling wines. From the 2000s, so-called “coffee Pinotage”, with marked toasty notes from oak, also became popular.

Where it is grown

It is grown in most South African regions, and is particularly important in Stellenbosch, Paarl and Swartland. As well as varietal wines, it is the characteristic component of the Cape Blend, a blend that usually combines it with Bordeaux varieties or Syrah. Outside South Africa there are small plantings in New Zealand, the United States, Zimbabwe and Israel, among other countries.

At the table

It goes well with grilled meat, such as that of the South African braai, lamb, game, spiced stews and aged cheeses. It is served at 16–18 °C.
