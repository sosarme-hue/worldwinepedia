Nebbiolo

Piedmont’s great red grape, pale in colour but high in acidity and tannin, which gives long-lived Barolo and Barbaresco.

Origin

Nebbiolo is an old variety from north-western Italy, documented in Piedmont since the Middle Ages. Its name is usually linked to nebbia, the fog that covers the Langhe hills in autumn at the time of its late harvest, although a link with the word for “noble” has also been suggested.

Viticulture

It is one of the first varieties to bud and one of the last to ripen, which exposes it to spring frost and requires a long season. It is very demanding about site: in the Langhe it is planted on the best south-facing slopes, on calcareous marl soils. It is vigorous and susceptible to powdery mildew. Small differences in soil and exposure translate into very different wines, which has led to the detailed mapping of the vineyard areas, or menzioni geografiche aggiuntive, of Barolo and Barbaresco.

In the glass

Its profile is paradoxical: the colour is pale and soon turns brick-orange, but acidity and tannin are very high. The classic aromas are rose, violet, cherry, raspberry and tar, with notes of liquorice, spice, truffle and dried herbs as it ages. Great Nebbiolos need years in bottle for the tannins to integrate and are remarkably long-lived. They are traditionally aged in large oak casks, although barriques are also used.

Where it is grown

Its cultivation is concentrated in Piedmont: Barolo, Barbaresco, Roero, Nebbiolo d’Alba and Langhe, and in the north of the region Gattinara, Ghemme and Carema, where it is sometimes called Spanna. In Lombardy, under the name Chiavennasca, it is the basis of the red wines of Valtellina, and in the Aosta Valley it is known as Picotendro. Outside Italy it is a difficult variety to transplant; there are plantings in Australia and the United States (California).
