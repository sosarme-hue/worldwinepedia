Tinta Negra

The most widely grown red grape on Madeira and the basis of most of its fortified wines: a versatile variety which, thanks to the Madeira production process, can make every style from dry to sweet.

Origin

Tinta Negra, long known as Tinta Negra Mole, is a Portuguese red variety that has become the main grape on the island of Madeira. It spread on the island after the great vine-health crises of the nineteenth century: first powdery mildew and then phylloxera devastated the vineyards, and when replanting many growers chose Tinta Negra, more productive and resistant than the traditional noble white varieties. Today it covers most of Madeira’s vineyard area. On the mainland it is grown in small quantities, mainly in the Algarve, under the name Negra Mole.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous, productive vine that adapts to a wide range of altitudes and exposures. On Madeira it is grown on the small terraces known as poios, on volcanic soils, from coastal areas up to high elevations, often on low pergolas or latadas. The altitude and orientation of each plot largely determine its ripeness and therefore the style of Madeira it will be used for.

Winemaking and wine style

Although it is a red grape, Madeira is made with little or no maceration, and oxidative ageing with heat, in estufas or in casks under the canteiro system, turns its colour to amber and mahogany. Depending on when fermentation is stopped with grape spirit, it can make dry, medium-dry, medium-rich or rich wines. Its Madeiras show notes of nuts, caramel, dried fruit, spice and wood, with the island’s characteristic acidity. For decades its name could not appear on labels, and the wines were sold by sweetness style or with an age indication; regulations now allow it, and some producers make Tinta Negra Madeira naming the variety, even with a vintage.

Where it is grown

Its main area is the island of Madeira, in the Madeira DOP, where it is the basis of the great majority of wines, from the youngest to many of those with an age indication. It is also used for the island’s unfortified wines. On the mainland its presence is small.

At the table

Dry and medium-dry Madeiras go with aperitifs, soups and nuts; sweet ones with desserts and aged cheeses. Serve at 12–14 °C.
