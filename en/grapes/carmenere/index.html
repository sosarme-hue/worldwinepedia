Carmenère

An old Bordeaux red grape that almost vanished in France and survived in Chile, mistaken for Merlot until 1994; it gives deeply coloured reds with soft tannins and notes of pepper and spice.

Origin

Carmenère is one of the historic varieties of Bordeaux, where in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries it was grown in the Médoc and Graves, sometimes under the name Grande Vidure. It belongs to the Cabernet family: genetic studies point to Cabernet Franc as one of its parents. After phylloxera, in the late nineteenth century, Bordeaux growers hardly replanted it, because its irregular flowering sharply reduced yields, and the variety became almost extinct in France.

Chile and its rediscovery

Before phylloxera, Chile had imported vines from Bordeaux, and Carmenère was planted interspersed with Merlot, for which it was mistaken for more than a century. In 1994 the French ampelographer Jean-Michel Boursiquot identified it in Chilean vineyards, explaining why much of the country’s “Merlot” ripened weeks later and had a different character. Since then it has become a signature variety of Chile. In Italy, some of the vines grown as Cabernet Franc in the north-east also turned out to be Carmenère, and in China it is grown under the name Cabernet Gernischt.

Viticulture

It ripens very late and needs a long, warm, dry autumn. It is prone to poor fruit set and to under-ripeness: picked early or with excessive vigour, its wines show very marked herbaceous and green pepper notes due to pyrazines. Careful canopy and yield management is essential.

In the glass

It gives very dark, almost purple reds, medium- to full-bodied, with moderate acidity and soft, rounded tannins. The aromas combine ripe red and black fruit (plum, blackberry, cherry), roasted red pepper, black pepper, spice, herbs, coffee and chocolate. It is made as a varietal wine or blended with Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah or Merlot, to which it adds colour and roundness.

Where it is grown

Chile holds the great majority of the world’s plantings. It stands out in the Central Valley, especially Colchagua, Cachapoal and the Maipo Valley, in warm areas that allow its long ripening. Outside Chile there are small plantings in Italy, France, China and some New World countries.

At the table

It goes well with grilled meat, pork, lamb, dishes with peppers, spiced food and semi-cured cheeses. It is served at 16–18 °C.
