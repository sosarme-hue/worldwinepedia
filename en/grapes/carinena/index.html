Carignan

A Mediterranean red with good acidity, colour and tannin, called Mazuelo in Rioja and Samsó in Catalonia; Carignan in France and Carignano in Sardinia.

Origin

Carignan takes its Spanish name, Cariñena, from the Aragonese town considered its birthplace, although, paradoxically, it is now scarce in the Cariñena D.O., which is dominated by Garnacha. From Aragon it spread to Catalonia, southern France and Sardinia and later to North Africa and the Americas.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous, productive vine that buds and ripens late and needs long, warm growing seasons. It is very susceptible to powdery mildew and rot, and at high yields it gives harsh, inexpressive wines. Old, low-yielding bush vines, on the other hand, give concentrated, balanced fruit and have been the basis of its revival.

In the glass

Its wines have deep colour, high acidity and firm, sometimes rustic tannins, so it has traditionally been used in blends to add freshness and structure, especially alongside Grenache. From old vines it gives reds with dark and red fruit (black cherry, plum, raspberry), notes of liquorice, spice, herbs and an earthy component, with good ageing potential. Carbonic maceration has been used to soften its character.

Where it is grown

In Spain it is the Samsó or Carinyena of Priorat, Montsant, Empordà and Terra Alta, and the Mazuelo of Rioja, where it is one of the authorised varieties. In France it was for decades the country’s most planted grape, above all in Languedoc-Roussillon, although its area has fallen sharply. In Italy it is the Carignano del Sulcis of south-western Sardinia. It is also grown in Chile, where old dry-farmed vines in Maule have gained recognition, and in California.

At the table

Its acidity and structure allow it to partner rich, flavourful dishes: stewed meat, charcuterie, lamb, game and aged cheeses. The more concentrated old-vine reds of Priorat or Montsant benefit from decanting and are best served at 16–18 °C.
