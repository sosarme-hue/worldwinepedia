Castelão

A red grape of southern Portugal, closely tied to the sandy soils of Palmela on the Setúbal Peninsula and long known as Periquita.

Origin

Castelão is a traditional red variety of southern and central Portugal. For a long time it was called Periquita, a name taken from Cova da Periquita, an estate near Setúbal whose wines, made by the house of José Maria da Fonseca since the nineteenth century, became very well known. Because Periquita became a brand name, the grape was officially renamed Castelão. In the Tejo it has also been known as João de Santarém.

Viticulture

It is a vigorous, productive vine that ripens early to mid-season and copes well with heat and drought. For much of the twentieth century it was one of the most widely planted red grapes in Portugal. It gives its best results on the poor, sandy soils of Palmela on the Setúbal Peninsula, where it makes more concentrated and elegant wines; on fertile soils with high yields, by contrast, it tends to give light, inexpressive wines.

In the glass

It makes ruby-coloured, medium-bodied reds with firm tannins in youth and adequate acidity, with aromas of red fruit such as redcurrant, raspberry and cherry, plum, gamey notes and spice. Young, it can seem somewhat rustic, but wines from old vines on sandy soils age well in bottle, developing aromas of candied fruit, leather and forest floor and sometimes notes reminiscent of cherry liqueur. It is also used for rosés and in blends with other Portuguese and international varieties.

Where it is grown

Its most notable area is the Setúbal Peninsula, with the Palmela DOC, where it is the main red grape. It is also grown in the Tejo, the Lisboa region, the Alentejo and other parts of the south and centre of the country. Its area has shrunk in recent decades in favour of varieties such as Touriga Nacional, Aragonez and Syrah. It is very rare outside Portugal.

At the table

It goes well with roast meat, cured meats, traditional Portuguese stews and sheep’s cheeses such as Azeitão. Serve at 15–17 °C.
