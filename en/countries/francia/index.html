France

France is one of the world's benchmark wine countries, the birthplace of the concept of terroir and of an appellation system that has served as a model for many others. From Bordeaux to Burgundy and from Champagne to the Rhône, its regions have shaped styles imitated around the world.

History

Viticulture reached what is now France with the Greeks of Phocaea, who founded Massalia, present-day Marseille, around 600 BC. After the Roman conquest the vine spread through Gallia Narbonensis and then moved up the valleys of the Rhône, Garonne, Loire and Moselle, eventually reaching northern regions such as Burgundy and Champagne. In the Middle Ages the monastic orders, especially the Benedictines of Cluny and the Cistercians of Cîteaux, played a decisive role in Burgundy. The marriage of Eleanor of Aquitaine to Henry Plantagenet in 1152 opened the English market to the wines of Bordeaux, which for centuries were exported under the name claret.

Many of today's styles took shape between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries: Dutch engineers helped to drain the marshes of the Médoc, sparkling Champagne became established and the Bordeaux trade gained great prestige, reflected in the 1855 classification. From the 1860s onwards phylloxera, an insect introduced from America, devastated much of the French vineyard; the lasting solution was to graft European varieties onto American rootstocks. Fraud and overproduction, which sparked unrest such as the Midi revolt of 1907, led to legislation on origin that culminated in the creation in 1935 of the committee that would later become the INAO, and in the first appellations d'origine contrôlée in 1936.

Climate and soils

France brings together three major climatic influences. The west, with Bordeaux and the Atlantic Loire, has a mild, humid oceanic climate with gentle winters and vintages strongly shaped by rainfall. The east and north, with Burgundy, Champagne and Alsace, have a continental or semi-continental climate with cold winters, a risk of spring frost and warm summers. The south, with Provence, Languedoc-Roussillon and the southern Rhône valley, is Mediterranean, sunny and dry, and often swept by winds such as the mistral and the tramontane.

The geological diversity is just as striking: gravel on the left bank of Bordeaux, clay and limestone in Saint-Émilion, Jurassic marl and limestone in Burgundy and Chablis, chalk in Champagne, granite in Beaujolais and the northern Rhône, schist in parts of Languedoc and Roussillon, and a mosaic of very different soils in Alsace. This variety underlies the concept of terroir, which combines soil, climate, topography and human know-how, and which structures the entire French appellation system.

The appellation system

French wine law rests on the idea that geographical origin determines a wine's character. Each AOC defines an area and sets the permitted varieties, maximum yields, minimum alcohol and a range of growing and winemaking practices. Since the European reform of 2009 the top category has been called AOP (appellation d'origine protégée), although the traditional term AOC is still used on labels. Below it come the IGPs (indication géographique protégée), successors to the former vins de pays, with more flexible rules and the option of naming the grape variety, as in the extensive IGP Pays d'Oc; and the Vin de France category, which replaced table wine and requires no regional origin.

Appellations are often arranged in tiers: regional, such as Bordeaux or Bourgogne; subregional, such as Haut-Médoc or Côte de Nuits-Villages; and communal, such as Pauillac, Margaux or Gevrey-Chambertin.

Classifications

Alongside the appellations, each region has its own classifications. The most famous is that of 1855, drawn up by the Bordeaux brokers for the Universal Exhibition in Paris: it ranked the red wines of the Médoc, together with Château Haut-Brion from Graves, in five tiers, and also classified the sweet wines of Sauternes and Barsac, with Château d'Yquem as the sole premier cru supérieur. Its only significant change was the promotion of Château Mouton Rothschild to first growth in 1973. Bordeaux also has the Graves classification, from 1953 and completed in 1959, the Saint-Émilion classification, created in 1955 and revised periodically, and the Crus Bourgeois of the Médoc.

In Burgundy the hierarchy applies not to estates but to vineyard plots, the climats: regional appellations, communal or village appellations, premiers crus and, at the top, 33 grands crus, such as Chambertin, Romanée-Conti, Clos de Vougeot, Corton and Montrachet. Alsace has 51 delimited grands crus, and in Champagne 17 villages hold grand cru status, a legacy of the former échelle des crus.

Regions and styles

Bordeaux is the benchmark for blended reds based on Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot, and for the botrytised sweet wines of Sauternes. Burgundy has made Pinot Noir and Chardonnay the expression of specific plots, while Beaujolais is devoted to Gamay. Champagne made famous the method of second fermentation in bottle, also used for the crémants of other regions. The Loire valley offers Sauvignon Blanc, Chenin Blanc, Melon de Bourgogne and Cabernet Franc in a wide range of styles; the Rhône, Syrah reds in the north and Grenache-based blends in the south; Alsace, varietal whites from Riesling, Gewürztraminer and Pinot Gris; Provence, mainly rosé; and Languedoc-Roussillon, the country's largest wine area, Mediterranean reds and vins doux naturels.

Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Cabernet Franc, Pinot Noir, Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc, Syrah (Shiraz), Grenache, Chenin Blanc, Gamay, Semillon, Riesling, Melon de Bourgogne, Viognier

Regions & appellations

Bordeaux

Wine region (AOC Bordeaux and Bordeaux Supérieur) · Bordeaux, in the Gironde département, is one of the most influential wine regions in the world. It is famous for its blended reds based on Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot, its dry whites and the sweet wines of Sauternes.

Médoc

Bordeaux subregion (AOC Médoc, Haut-Médoc and communal appellations) · The Médoc is the peninsula on the left bank of the Gironde, north of the city of Bordeaux. Its gravel soils and Cabernet Sauvignon-dominated reds account for most of the wines in the 1855 classification.

Saint-Émilion

AOC (Saint-Émilion and Saint-Émilion Grand Cru) · Saint-Émilion, on the right bank of the Dordogne, is one of the great appellations of Bordeaux, known for its reds dominated by Merlot and Cabernet Franc. Its medieval town and vineyard landscape are on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

Pomerol

AOC · Pomerol is a small appellation on the right bank of Bordeaux, next to Libourne, devoted to Merlot-based reds with some Cabernet Franc. It has never had an official classification, yet it is home to some of the region's most sought-after wines.

Pessac-Léognan

AOC · Pessac-Léognan is the appellation created in 1987 in the northern part of Graves, on the outskirts of the city of Bordeaux. It produces reds and dry whites and includes all the estates in the Graves classification, among them Château Haut-Brion.

Sauternes

AOC (sweet wine) · Sauternes, south of Graves, is the appellation for the great sweet wines of Bordeaux, made from grapes affected by noble rot. Its best-known wine, Château d'Yquem, was the only one classified as premier cru supérieur in 1855.

Burgundy

Wine region · Burgundy, in east-central France, is the home of Pinot Noir and Chardonnay expressed through precisely defined vineyard plots, the climats. Its hierarchy of appellations culminates in the grands crus of the Côte d'Or and Chablis.

Chablis

AOC (Petit Chablis, Chablis, Chablis Premier Cru and Chablis Grand Cru) · Chablis, in the Yonne département, is Burgundy's northernmost vineyard and is devoted exclusively to Chardonnay. Its whites, with lively acidity and a mineral character, are associated with Kimmeridgian marl soils.

Côte de Nuits

Burgundy subregion (Côte d'Or) · The Côte de Nuits is the northern half of the Côte d'Or, between Dijon and Corgoloin. Devoted almost entirely to Pinot Noir, it is home to the great majority of Burgundy's red grands crus.

Côte de Beaune

Burgundy subregion (Côte d'Or) · The Côte de Beaune is the southern half of the Côte d'Or, from Ladoix to Maranges. It produces some of the world's most highly regarded Chardonnay whites, such as Montrachet, Meursault and Corton-Charlemagne, as well as Pinot Noir reds such as Pommard and Volnay.

Beaujolais

Wine region (AOC Beaujolais, Beaujolais-Villages and ten crus) · Beaujolais, between the Mâconnais and Lyon, is the home of Gamay. Its wines range from Beaujolais Nouveau, released just weeks after the harvest, to the ten crus of the north, grown on granite soils.

Champagne

AOC (sparkling wine) · Champagne, north-east of Paris, is the region that made sparkling wines with a second fermentation in bottle famous around the world. Its wines are based on Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Meunier, grown over a chalk subsoil.

Loire

Wine region · The Loire valley brings together a succession of vineyards along France's longest river, from the Atlantic coast to the centre of the country. It is known for the diversity of its styles, with Melon de Bourgogne, Chenin Blanc, Sauvignon Blanc and Cabernet Franc in the leading roles.

Sancerre

AOC · Sancerre, on the left bank of the Loire in the Cher département, is one of the benchmark appellations for Sauvignon Blanc. It also produces reds and rosés from Pinot Noir.

Northern Rhône

Rhône valley subregion · The Northern Rhône is the narrow strip of terraced vineyards that follows the river between Vienne and Valence. It is the home of Syrah in its most classic form, in appellations such as Côte-Rôtie, Hermitage and Cornas, and of the Viognier of Condrieu.

Châteauneuf-du-Pape

AOC · Châteauneuf-du-Pape, near Avignon, is the most famous appellation of the Southern Rhône. Its reds, based mainly on Grenache, may blend up to thirteen varieties, and its rules of 1923 served as a basis for the French appellation system.

Alsace

Wine region (AOC Alsace, Alsace Grand Cru and Crémant d'Alsace) · Alsace, between the Vosges and the Rhine, is a region of aromatic white wines which, unlike most of France, are identified by grape variety. Riesling, Gewürztraminer, Pinot Gris and Muscat are its most prestigious grapes.

Provence

Wine region · Provence, in south-eastern France between the Rhône and the Côte d'Azur, is the French region most closely associated with rosé, which makes up the great majority of its production. It also has appellations for distinctive reds and whites, such as Bandol and Cassis.

Languedoc-Roussillon

Wine region · Languedoc-Roussillon, along the Mediterranean between the Rhône and the Pyrenees, is France's largest wine area. Once known for table wine, it now produces Mediterranean appellation reds, varietal wines under the IGP Pays d'Oc, sparkling wines from Limoux and vins doux naturels.

Margaux

AOC (communal appellation of the Haut-Médoc, Bordeaux) · Margaux is the southernmost communal appellation of the Haut-Médoc and the one with the most growths classified in 1855. Its Cabernet Sauvignon-based reds are known for their finesse and perfume.

Pauillac

AOC (communal appellation of the Haut-Médoc, Bordeaux) · Pauillac, in the heart of the Haut-Médoc, is home to three of the five first growths of the 1855 classification. Its Cabernet Sauvignon reds are powerful, firm and very long-lived.

Saint-Julien

AOC (communal appellation of the Haut-Médoc, Bordeaux) · Saint-Julien, between Margaux and Pauillac, is a compact Haut-Médoc appellation in which classified growths occupy much of the vineyard. Its reds are prized for their balance between power and elegance.

Saint-Estèphe

AOC (communal appellation of the Haut-Médoc, Bordeaux) · Saint-Estèphe is the northernmost of the great communal appellations of the Haut-Médoc. Its soils, richer in clay than those of its neighbours, give firm, fresh reds in which Merlot often plays an important role.

Graves

AOC (Graves and Graves Supérieures), Bordeaux subregion · Graves, south of the city of Bordeaux on the left bank of the Garonne, takes its name from its gravel soils. It is one of the few Bordeaux areas where reds and dry whites carry comparable weight.

Entre-Deux-Mers

Bordeaux area and AOC (dry whites) · Entre-Deux-Mers is the broad area of hills lying between the Garonne and the Dordogne. Its own appellation is reserved for dry whites, while the area's reds are sold as Bordeaux or Bordeaux Supérieur.

Côtes de Bordeaux

AOC (with additional geographical names: Blaye, Cadillac, Castillon, Francs, Sainte-Foy) · Côtes de Bordeaux is an appellation that brings together several hilly areas on the right banks of the Garonne, the Dordogne and the Gironde. It produces mainly Merlot-dominated reds and, to a lesser extent, whites.

Côte Chalonnaise

Burgundy subregion (regional AOC Bourgogne Côte Chalonnaise and village AOCs) · The Côte Chalonnaise extends the Côte de Beaune southwards, west of Chalon-sur-Saône. Its five village appellations, Bouzeron, Rully, Mercurey, Givry and Montagny, offer Burgundian-style Pinot Noir, Chardonnay and Aligoté at more moderate prices.

Mâconnais

Burgundy subregion (Mâcon and Mâcon-Villages AOCs and village AOCs) · The Mâconnais, around the town of Mâcon, is Burgundy's southernmost subregion before Beaujolais. It is Chardonnay country, giving everything from fresh, fruity whites to the most ambitious Pouilly-Fuissé.

Pouilly-Fuissé

Village AOC of the Mâconnais (with premiers crus) · Pouilly-Fuissé is the most prestigious appellation of the Mâconnais, at the foot of the rocks of Solutré and Vergisson. It produces only Chardonnay whites, broad and mineral, and since the 2020 vintage has had vineyards classified as premier cru.

Côte-Rôtie

AOC (Northern Rhône cru) · Côte-Rôtie is the northernmost appellation of the Rhône valley, on dizzying slopes above the village of Ampuis. Its Syrah reds, sometimes co-fermented with a little Viognier, combine perfume, finesse and ageing potential.

Hermitage

AOC (Northern Rhône cru) · Hermitage is a hill on the left bank of the Rhône, above Tain-l'Hermitage, that produces some of France's longest-lived Syrah reds and broad whites from Marsanne and Roussanne.

Condrieu

AOC (Northern Rhône cru) · Condrieu, south of Côte-Rôtie, is the benchmark appellation for Viognier. Its very aromatic, unctuous whites come from terraced granite slopes above the Rhône.

Cornas

AOC (Northern Rhône cru) · Cornas, opposite the city of Valence, is a small appellation on the right bank of the Rhône devoted exclusively to Syrah reds. Its dark, tannic wines come from very sunny granite slopes.

Saint-Joseph

AOC (Northern Rhône cru) · Saint-Joseph is a long strip of vineyards on the right bank of the Rhône, between Condrieu and Cornas. It produces mainly Syrah reds, lighter on their feet and fruitier than those of Hermitage or Côte-Rôtie, and Marsanne and Roussanne whites.

Gigondas

AOC (Southern Rhône cru) · Gigondas, at the foot of the Dentelles de Montmirail in the Vaucluse, is one of the most highly regarded crus of the Southern Rhône. Its Grenache-based reds, with Syrah and Mourvèdre, are powerful yet fresh thanks to the altitude of part of the vineyard.

Vacqueyras

AOC (Southern Rhône cru) · Vacqueyras, Gigondas's neighbour in the Vaucluse, has been a Southern Rhône cru since 1990. It produces mainly generous, spicy Grenache and Syrah reds, along with smaller amounts of rosé and white.

Côtes du Rhône

Regional AOC of the Rhône valley (Côtes du Rhône and Côtes du Rhône Villages) · Côtes du Rhône is the regional appellation of the Rhône valley and one of the largest in France. Most of its output comes from the south of the valley and consists of red blends based on Grenache, Syrah and Mourvèdre.

Tavel

AOC (Southern Rhône cru, rosé only) · Tavel, on the right bank of the Rhône near Avignon, is an appellation devoted exclusively to rosé. Its wines, deep in colour and well structured, are food rosés made to accompany a meal.

Vouvray

AOC (Touraine, Loire valley) · Vouvray, east of Tours on the right bank of the Loire, is one of the benchmark appellations for Chenin Blanc. It produces dry, off-dry, sweet and sparkling whites, often of great longevity.

Savennières

AOC (Anjou, Loire valley) · Savennières, on the right bank of the Loire south-west of Angers, is a small appellation for Chenin Blanc whites, mostly dry, known for their intensity, tension and long ageing.

Muscadet

AOC (Muscadet, Sèvre et Maine, Coteaux de la Loire, Côtes de Grandlieu and crus communaux), Pays Nantais · Muscadet, in the Pays Nantais near the mouth of the Loire, produces light, saline dry whites from Melon de Bourgogne. Many are bottled after ageing on their lees, and the crus communaux offer long-lived versions.

Chinon

AOC (Touraine, Loire valley) · Chinon, in Touraine beside the river Vienne, is one of the great red-wine appellations of the Loire. Its Cabernet Franc wines range from light, fruity reds to structured, age-worthy versions, complemented by some rosés and Chenin whites.

Anjou

Loire wine area and AOC (Anjou, Anjou Villages, Rosé d'Anjou, Cabernet d'Anjou and others) · Anjou, around the city of Angers, is one of the most varied areas of the Loire: it produces dry and sweet Chenin Blanc whites, Cabernet Franc reds and a large quantity of rosé, including Rosé d'Anjou and Cabernet d'Anjou.

Pouilly-Fumé

AOC (Pouilly-Fumé or Blanc Fumé de Pouilly), Central Loire · Pouilly-Fumé, on the right bank of the Loire opposite Sancerre, is one of the benchmark appellations for Sauvignon Blanc. Its dry whites combine freshness, aromatic intensity and notes often described as flinty or smoky.

Jura

Wine region (Arbois, Côtes du Jura, L'Étoile, Château-Chalon, Crémant du Jura and Macvin du Jura AOCs) · The Jura, between Burgundy and Switzerland, is a small wine region with a strong personality. It is known for vin jaune, a Savagnin white aged under a veil of yeast, and for its light Poulsard and Trousseau reds.

Savoie

Wine region (Vin de Savoie / Savoie AOC with crus, Roussette de Savoie, Seyssel, Crémant de Savoie) · Savoie, in the French Alps, brings together scattered mountain vineyards between Lake Geneva and the Isère valley. It produces mainly fresh whites from Jacquère, Altesse and Roussanne, and Mondeuse reds.

Cahors

AOC (South West France) · Cahors, in the Lot valley, is the historic home of Malbec, known here as Côt or Auxerrois. Its dark, tannic reds were known for centuries as “black wine”.

Madiran

AOC (South West France) · Madiran, at the foot of the Pyrenees in Gascony, is the emblematic appellation for Tannat. Its deeply coloured reds with powerful tannins rank among the most structured in France.

Jurançon

AOC (Jurançon and Jurançon sec), South West France · Jurançon, in the Béarn south of Pau, produces whites from Petit Manseng and Gros Manseng on slopes at the foot of the Pyrenees. It is best known for its sweet wines from grapes shrivelled on the vine, although today most of the output is Jurançon sec.

Bandol

AOC (Provence) · Bandol, on the Provençal coast west of Toulon, is the benchmark appellation for Mourvèdre. Its structured, long-lived reds are joined by well-structured rosés and some whites.

Corsica

Wine region (Patrimonio, Ajaccio, Vin de Corse / Corse with geographical names, and Muscat du Cap Corse AOCs) · Corsica, a mountainous Mediterranean island, grows its own varieties such as Niellucciu, Sciaccarellu and Vermentinu. Its best-known appellations are Patrimonio, in the north, and Ajaccio, in the west.

Limoux

AOC (Limoux, Blanquette de Limoux, Blanquette méthode ancestrale and Crémant de Limoux), Languedoc · Limoux, in the upper Aude valley, is the great sparkling-wine area of the Languedoc. It produces Blanquette de Limoux, based on Mauzac, Crémant de Limoux and still Chardonnay and Chenin whites, fresh thanks to the altitude.

Banyuls

AOC (Banyuls and Banyuls Grand Cru, vin doux naturel), Roussillon · Banyuls, on the Côte Vermeille of Roussillon near the Spanish border, produces Grenache-based vins doux naturels, fortified during fermentation. Its vineyards on schist terraces run down to the Mediterranean.
