Romania

Romania has one of the largest vineyard areas in Europe, spread around the arc of the Carpathians and down to the Black Sea. Alongside international varieties, it preserves its own grapes such as Fetească Neagră, Fetească Albă, Tămâioasă Românească and Grasă de Cotnari.

History

Vines have been grown in what is now Romania since antiquity. The Dacians already knew wine, the Greek colonies on the Black Sea coast, such as Histria and Tomis, traded it, and after the Roman conquest of Dacia winegrowing spread across the whole region. In the Middle Ages the principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia developed important vineyards, often linked to monasteries and the nobility; Cotnari, in Moldavia, is traditionally associated with Prince Stephen the Great. In Transylvania, Saxon settlers encouraged the cultivation of white varieties.

Phylloxera arrived at the end of the nineteenth century and forced the replanting of vineyards on American rootstocks, although direct-producer hybrids also spread and still survive in family plots. During the communist period production was organised in large state farms and cooperatives, geared to volume and to exports to the Soviet bloc. After 1989 a long process of privatisation and restructuring began, accelerated by accession to the European Union in 2007, which has given rise to a new generation of wineries focused on quality and on reviving native varieties.

Climate and soils

The climate is continental, with cold winters and warm summers, but strongly shaped by relief. The arc of the Carpathians shelters the hills of the south and east, where many of the best vineyards lie, while Transylvania, in the interior, is cooler. Dobrogea, between the Danube and the Black Sea, is the driest and sunniest area. Soils include chernozem, loess, clay, marl, sand and limestone outcrops.

Regions

Several main regions are distinguished. In Moldavia, in the north-east, are Cotnari, Iași, Huși, Odobești, Panciu and Cotești, among others. The hills of Muntenia and Oltenia, south of the Carpathians, include Dealu Mare, known for its reds, and Drăgășani. Transylvania, with the Târnave plateau, is a land of fresh, aromatic whites. Banat and Crișana, in the west, have areas such as Recaș and Miniș, and Dobrogea, in the south-east, includes Murfatlar and Sarica Niculițel. There are also vineyard areas on the Danube terraces and on the sands of the south.

Grapes and styles

Leading native varieties include Fetească Neagră, a red with black fruit and spice; Fetească Albă and Fetească Regală, widely planted whites; Tămâioasă Românească, a member of the Muscat family that gives aromatic, often sweet wines; Grasă de Cotnari, the basis of long-lived sweet wines; Frâncușă; Crâmpoșie Selecționată from Drăgășani; and reds such as Băbească Neagră and Novac. Merlot, Cabernet Sauvignon, Pinot Noir, Sauvignon Blanc, Aligoté, Muscat Ottonel and so-called Riesling Italian, that is Welschriesling, are also grown. Romanian taste traditionally favoured medium-dry and medium-sweet wines, but production of dry wines has grown strongly.

Regulations

The classification distinguishes wines without a geographical indication, wines with a geographical indication (IG) and wines with a controlled designation of origin (Denumire de Origine Controlată, DOC). Within the DOC there are three terms based on the ripeness of the grapes: DOC-CMD (cules la maturitate deplină, harvested at full ripeness), DOC-CT (cules târziu, late harvest) and DOC-CIB (cules la înnobilarea boabelor, harvest of grapes affected by noble rot). Labels also state the sugar content: sec, demisec, demidulce or dulce.

Regions & appellations

Dealu Mare

Controlled designation of origin (DOC) · Dealu Mare, “the big hill”, is a strip of south-facing slopes in the foothills of the Carpathians, north of Bucharest. It is Romania’s most renowned red-wine area, with Fetească Neagră, Merlot, Cabernet Sauvignon and Pinot Noir.

Cotnari

Controlled designation of origin (DOC) · Cotnari, in the hills of Iași county in Romanian Moldavia, is Romania’s historic appellation for sweet white wines. It is based on four local varieties: Grasă de Cotnari, Tămâioasă Românească, Fetească Albă and Frâncușă.
