Canada

Canada makes wine mainly in Ontario and British Columbia, in areas moderated by large lakes. It is famous for its icewine, but it also produces fresh Riesling and Chardonnay whites and reds from Pinot Noir, Cabernet Franc and Merlot.

History

The first European settlers found wild vines in what is now Canada, but the wine made from them, and later from American varieties such as Concord, was of modest quality. Johann Schiller, a former German soldier who settled in Ontario in the early nineteenth century, is usually regarded as the father of Canadian winemaking. During the nineteenth century wineries appeared on the Niagara Peninsula and on Pelee Island, in Lake Erie, but in the early twentieth century Prohibition in several provinces held back the industry, which for decades produced mainly sweet and fortified wines from American and hybrid grapes.

Transformation came in the 1970s. In 1975 Inniskillin, in Niagara-on-the-Lake, obtained the first winery licence granted in Ontario since the late 1920s, opening the way for a generation of small wineries focused on Vitis vinifera varieties. At the end of the 1980s, the free trade agreement with the United States forced the industry to become more competitive and led to the uprooting of much of the American and hybrid vineyard, replaced by European varieties. In British Columbia, the Okanagan Valley followed a similar path and became the country’s second great region.

Climate

Canada’s climate is continental, with very cold winters, so quality viticulture is only possible where large bodies of water moderate temperatures. In Ontario, Lakes Ontario and Erie delay budbreak in spring, extend the autumn and reduce the risk of extreme frosts; in Niagara, the escarpment rising behind the lakeshore also encourages air circulation. In British Columbia, the Okanagan Valley is sheltered by mountains that keep out Pacific moisture, giving hot, dry and very sunny summers, especially in the south. Winter cold remains a threat to the vines, and in some places they are buried or protected over winter.

Regions

Ontario is the leading producing province, with the Niagara Peninsula as its most important area, followed by Lake Erie North Shore and Prince Edward County. In British Columbia the Okanagan Valley stands out, alongside the Similkameen Valley and the coastal areas of Vancouver Island and the Fraser Valley. Quebec and Nova Scotia have smaller production, based largely on cold-hardy hybrid varieties; Nova Scotia is known for its fresh whites and sparkling wines.

Styles

The most famous style is icewine, made from grapes left on the vine until they freeze naturally in the depths of winter. When they are pressed frozen, the water stays behind as ice crystals and the juice obtained is highly concentrated in sugar and acidity. It is made mainly from Vidal, a thick-skinned hybrid variety, and from Riesling, and also from Cabernet Franc. Alongside it, Canada produces whites with lively acidity from Riesling and Chardonnay, reds from Pinot Noir, Cabernet Franc and Gamay in Ontario, and riper reds from Merlot, Cabernet Sauvignon and Syrah in the southern Okanagan, as well as sparkling wines.

Regulations

Quality wines made entirely from Canadian grapes come under the VQA system (Vintners Quality Alliance), created in Ontario in 1988 and later adopted in British Columbia. The system defines geographical appellations, from regions down to sub-appellations, and sets rules for production and labelling. For icewine it requires the grapes to be harvested naturally frozen, at temperatures of at least minus eight degrees Celsius, and prohibits artificial freezing. These wines should be distinguished from those labelled as bottled in Canada, made from imported wines blended with local wine.

Key grapes
Vidal, Riesling, Chardonnay, Pinot Noir, Cabernet Franc, Merlot

Regions & appellations

Niagara Peninsula

VQA Ontario appellation · The Niagara Peninsula, between Lakes Ontario and Erie, is Canada’s leading wine region. Thanks to the influence of the lake and the Niagara Escarpment, it produces Riesling, Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Cabernet Franc, as well as much of the country’s icewine.

Okanagan Valley

BC VQA geographical indication · The Okanagan Valley, in the interior of British Columbia, is western Canada’s leading wine region. It stretches along a chain of lakes, from a cool north to a hot, semi-desert south, and produces everything from Riesling and Pinot Noir to Merlot and Syrah.
