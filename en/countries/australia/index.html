Australia

Australia is one of the great New World producers, with regions ranging from the warm valleys of South Australia to the cool island of Tasmania. It is best known for its Shiraz, but also for its Cabernet Sauvignon, Chardonnay, Riesling and Semillon.

History

The first vines reached Australia with the First Fleet, which landed in Sydney Cove in 1788 to found the penal colony of New South Wales. The early attempts met with little success because of the humid coastal climate, but cultivation spread over the following decades. A prominent role was played by James Busby, who in the 1830s assembled a large collection of grape varieties in Europe and brought it to the colony; many of those plants were grown in the Hunter Valley. In the 1840s Lutheran settlers from Silesia and Prussia arrived in South Australia, settled in the Barossa Valley and shaped a winegrowing culture that still endures.

In the second half of the nineteenth century phylloxera devastated much of Victoria’s vineyards, but South Australia applied strict quarantine measures and remained free of the pest. As a result it still has some of the oldest producing vines in the world, planted on their own roots. For much of the twentieth century production was dominated by fortified wines in the style of port and sherry, largely intended for the British market. From the 1950s and 1960s consumption shifted towards table wines; reds such as Penfolds Grange, whose first experimental vintage was 1951, marked that change. In the closing decades of the century Australia became a major exporter, known for generously fruited varietal wines, and since then many producers have turned towards fresher styles and towards the expression of each region.

Climate and soils

Almost all the vineyards are concentrated in the south-east of the country, in South Australia, Victoria and New South Wales, with an important core in the south-west of Western Australia and in Tasmania. Many areas have a warm, dry, Mediterranean-type climate, and on the great inland plains along the Murray and Murrumbidgee rivers irrigation makes large volumes possible. The quality regions seek the moderating influence of the ocean, altitude or latitude: Coonawarra, Margaret River, the Yarra Valley, the Adelaide Hills and Tasmania have much cooler climates than the Barossa or McLaren Vale. Soils are generally very old and varied; among the best known is the terra rossa over limestone of Coonawarra.

Grapes and styles

Syrah, known here as Shiraz, is the country’s emblematic grape. It gives generous, spicy reds in the Barossa and McLaren Vale, and fresher, peppery versions in temperate areas. Cabernet Sauvignon shines in Coonawarra and Margaret River, and the Cabernet–Shiraz blend is an Australian tradition. Grenache and Mourvèdre, locally called Mataro, have been grown since the nineteenth century and have gained prestige from old vines. Among the whites, Chardonnay is made throughout the country, dry Riesling from the Clare and Eden valleys is a style of its own, and Hunter Valley Semillon is one of the most distinctive whites of the New World. Tasmania and other cool areas produce Pinot Noir and traditional-method sparkling wines. Australia was also a pioneer in the widespread use of the screwcap, promoted in 2000 by a group of Clare Valley Riesling producers.

Regulations

The system of Geographical Indications (GIs), managed by Wine Australia, divides the territory into states, zones, regions and subregions, without imposing grape varieties or winemaking methods. If the label states a region, a variety or a vintage, at least 85% of the wine must correspond to what is stated. There is also a broad indication, South Eastern Australia, which allows grapes from several states to be blended and is used for many high-volume wines.

Syrah (Shiraz), Cabernet Sauvignon, Chardonnay, Riesling, Semillon, Grenache, Pinot Noir, Monastrell (Mourvèdre)

Regions & appellations

Barossa Valley

Geographical Indication (GI) · The Barossa Valley, north-east of Adelaide, is Australia’s most iconic region for Shiraz. It preserves very old own-rooted vines and a strong cultural heritage from settlers of German origin.

McLaren Vale

Geographical Indication (GI) · McLaren Vale, south of Adelaide and next to Gulf St Vincent, is one of South Australia’s historic regions. It stands out for its old-vine Shiraz and Grenache and for great geological diversity.

Clare Valley

Geographical Indication (GI) · The Clare Valley, in the northern Mount Lofty Ranges of South Australia, is one of the world’s great references for dry Riesling. It also produces well-structured Shiraz and Cabernet Sauvignon.

Eden Valley

Geographical Indication (GI) · The Eden Valley occupies the eastern hills of the Barossa, at a higher altitude than the valley floor. Its cooler climate gives Riesling of great finesse and elegant Shiraz, including some of Australia’s most famous reds.

Coonawarra

Geographical Indication (GI) · Coonawarra, in the south-east of South Australia, is famous for a narrow strip of terra rossa over limestone. It is the Australian region most closely associated with Cabernet Sauvignon.

Hunter Valley

Geographical Indication (GI) · The Hunter Valley, in New South Wales, is one of Australia’s oldest wine regions. It is famous for its light, long-lived Semillon and for medium-bodied Shiraz.

Yarra Valley

Geographical Indication (GI) · The Yarra Valley, east of Melbourne, was the cradle of winegrowing in Victoria. Today it is one of Australia’s most highly regarded cool-climate regions for Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and sparkling wine.

Margaret River

Geographical Indication (GI) · Margaret River, in the far south-west of Western Australia, is a young region that has established itself as one of the country’s best for Cabernet Sauvignon and Chardonnay.

Tasmania

Geographical Indication (GI) · Tasmania, the island south of the Australian mainland, is the country’s coolest wine region. It has made its name with traditional-method sparkling wines, Pinot Noir and Chardonnay.
