Uruguay

Uruguay, with a temperate, humid climate under Atlantic influence, has made Tannat its signature grape. Its vineyards, largely in the hands of family wineries of immigrant origin, are concentrated in the south of the country, around Montevideo and Canelones.

History

Although the vine reached the Río de la Plata in colonial times, modern Uruguayan winegrowing was born in the second half of the nineteenth century thanks to European immigrants, above all Basque, French, Italian and Spanish. Two names are linked to its beginnings: Pascual Harriague, of French Basque origin, who introduced Tannat in the 1870s in the department of Salto, in the north-west of the country, and Francisco Vidiella, who planted vineyards near Montevideo with varieties brought from Europe. Tannat adapted so well that for a long time it was known locally as “Harriague”, in honour of the man who introduced it.

Phylloxera arrived at the end of the nineteenth century, and replanting was done on American rootstocks. For much of the twentieth century the industry, made up of numerous family wineries, supplied mainly the domestic market with everyday wines. From the 1970s and 1980s a profound renewal began: vineyards were replanted with selected clones, wineries were modernised and the goal of exporting quality wine was set. In 1987 the National Institute of Viticulture (INAVI) was created to regulate and promote the industry. Since then Tannat has become the country’s calling card, and in recent decades new areas have emerged, particularly near the Atlantic coast.

Climate

Unlike most South American wine regions, Uruguay has no high mountains and no dry climate. Its climate is temperate and humid, with rainfall spread throughout the year and a very strong maritime influence, both from the Atlantic Ocean and from the broad estuary of the Río de la Plata. Summers are warm but moderated by breezes, and cool nights help preserve acidity. Humidity calls for particular care over vineyard health, with training systems that improve air flow around the bunches and attentive canopy management. The wines tend to show more freshness and more moderate alcohol than those of other Southern Cone countries.

Soils and regions

Vineyards are concentrated in the south, in the departments of Canelones, which accounts for most of the planted area, and Montevideo, on clay and silt soils with good water retention. In the south-east, the department of Maldonado has developed recent viticulture on hills close to the Atlantic, on weathered granite soils. Other areas are Colonia, in the west, beside the Río de la Plata; San José and Florida, in the south-centre; and Rivera, on the border with Brazil, with sandy soils, as well as Salto and Paysandú in the warmer north-west.

Varieties and styles

Tannat, native to south-west France, is the signature variety. In Uruguay it gives deeply coloured reds with firm tannins and lively acidity, made in very diverse styles: young and fruity, barrel-aged, blended with Merlot, Cabernet Franc or Cabernet Sauvignon, and also rosés and some sweet wines. The country also grows Merlot, Cabernet Sauvignon, Cabernet Franc, Syrah and Pinot Noir among red varieties. Among whites, Sauvignon Blanc and Chardonnay stand out and, especially in recent years, Albariño, which has found in the cool, humid coastal climate conditions similar to those of its native Galicia. Sparkling wines are also made.

Regulations

INAVI regulates production and labelling. Quality wines are classified as VCP (Vino de Calidad Preferente), which must be made from Vitis vinifera varieties and bottled at source, as opposed to common wines intended for everyday consumption. The country has also made progress in defining geographical indications for its wine areas.

Key grapes
Tannat, Merlot, Cabernet Franc, Cabernet Sauvignon, Albariño, Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay

Regions & appellations

Canelones

Wine department · The department of Canelones, to the north and east of Montevideo, holds most of Uruguay’s vineyards. On clay soils and under the influence of the Río de la Plata and the Atlantic, it is the heartland of Uruguayan Tannat and home to many historic family wineries.

Maldonado

Wine department · Maldonado, in south-eastern Uruguay, is a young wine area set on hills close to the Atlantic Ocean. Its weathered granite soils and cool, windy climate give Albariño, Tannat and other varieties with a taut profile and marked freshness.
