Austria

Austria’s vineyards are concentrated in the east of the country, between the Danube and the Pannonian plain. It is best known for dry Grüner Veltliner and Riesling from the Danube, for reds from Blaufränkisch and Zweigelt, and for the nobly sweet wines of Burgenland.

History

Vines were grown in what is now Austria before the arrival of the Romans, who encouraged their development in the Danube provinces. In the Middle Ages the great monasteries, such as Klosterneuburg, Melk and Göttweig, owned and tended extensive vineyards, and many of the Danube terraces were built at that time. In 1784 a decree by Emperor Joseph II allowed growers to sell their own wine in their homes, the origin of the Heurigen, growers’ taverns that remain a living tradition, above all in Vienna.

For much of the twentieth century Austria produced mostly light wines for local consumption and bulk wine for export. In 1985 a scandal broke: some merchants had adulterated wines with diethylene glycol to make them seem richer. Exports collapsed, but the crisis led to one of the strictest wine laws in Europe and a shift towards quality. Since then a generation of growers has made the dry whites of the Danube and the reds of Burgenland known internationally.

Climate and soils

Almost all the vineyards are in the east of the country, in the states of Lower Austria, Burgenland, Styria and Vienna. The climate combines continental influences with the warm Pannonian plain to the east, with a marked contrast between warm days and cool nights in the Danube valleys, where cold winds from the forests of the Waldviertel cool the vines during ripening. In Burgenland, Lake Neusiedl creates the autumn humidity that favours noble rot. Soils range from the gneiss and granite of the Danube terraces to the loess of Kremstal, Kamptal and Weinviertel, and the slate, limestone and clay soils of Burgenland.

Regions

Lower Austria (Niederösterreich) is the largest wine region and includes areas such as the Wachau, Kremstal, Kamptal, Traisental, Wagram, Weinviertel, Carnuntum and Thermenregion. Burgenland, along the Hungarian border, is the land of red and sweet wines. Styria (Steiermark), in the south, has vineyards on very steep slopes and is known for its aromatic whites and for Schilcher, a rosé made from the Blauer Wildbacher grape. Vienna is one of the few capitals in the world with significant vineyards within its city limits.

Grapes and styles

Grüner Veltliner is the country’s most widely planted variety and its hallmark: it produces everything from light, fresh whites with its characteristic peppery note to full-bodied wines capable of ageing. Riesling, far less widespread, reaches a very high level in the Wachau, Kremstal and Kamptal. Other whites include Welschriesling, Weissburgunder, Sauvignon Blanc, highly regarded in Styria, and Vienna’s Gemischter Satz, a blend of varieties planted together. The leading reds are Zweigelt, the most planted, Blaufränkisch, of great potential in Burgenland, and St. Laurent. The nobly sweet wines of Lake Neusiedl and Ruster Ausbruch have a long tradition.

Regulations and classifications

Austrian law uses ripeness categories related to the German ones: Qualitätswein (which includes Kabinett) and Prädikatswein, with Spätlese, Auslese, Beerenauslese, Ausbruch, Trockenbeerenauslese, Eiswein and Strohwein. Since the early 2000s the DAC system (Districtus Austriae Controllatus) has been developed, linking each area to its typical grapes and styles; the Weinviertel was the first DAC. Many DACs distinguish between regional, village and single-vineyard (Ried) wines. In the Wachau, the Vinea Wachau association also uses its own categories, Steinfeder, Federspiel and Smaragd, according to alcohol level and ripeness.

Regions & appellations

Wachau

DAC (Districtus Austriae Controllatus) · The Wachau is a narrow stretch of the Danube valley between Melk and Krems, with vineyards on dry-stone terraces over primary rock. It is Austria’s most famous region for dry Grüner Veltliner and Riesling.

Kamptal

DAC (Districtus Austriae Controllatus) · The Kamptal follows the valley of the river Kamp, a tributary of the Danube, around the town of Langenlois. Its Grüner Veltliners from loess and its Rieslings from the Heiligenstein vineyard are among Austria’s most admired whites.

Kremstal

DAC (Districtus Austriae Controllatus) · The Kremstal surrounds the historic town of Krems, where the Wachau ends, and combines primary-rock terraces in the west with broad loess terraces in the east. It produces outstanding dry Grüner Veltliner and Riesling.

Burgenland

Wine-growing region (Weinbaugebiet), with several DACs · Burgenland, Austria’s easternmost state, borders Hungary and is shaped by the Pannonian climate and by Lake Neusiedl. It is Austria’s great region for red wines, above all from Blaufränkisch and Zweigelt, and for nobly sweet wines.

Weinviertel

DAC (Districtus Austriae Controllatus) · The Weinviertel (“wine quarter”) is Austria’s largest wine region and stretches north of Vienna to the Czech and Slovak borders. It was the country’s first DAC and is known for its fresh, peppery Grüner Veltliner.
