Chile

Chile is a long, narrow winegrowing country, enclosed between the Andes and the Pacific, that was never affected by phylloxera. It is known for its Maipo Cabernet Sauvignon, for Carmenère and, increasingly, for fresh coastal wines and the old vines of the south.

History

The vine reached Chile in the mid-sixteenth century with Spanish conquerors and missionaries. The variety they planted, the País grape, identical to the Canary Islands’ Listán Prieto, dominated the vineyards for centuries and was used to make sacramental wine and everyday wine. In the mid-nineteenth century, landowners enriched by mining and agriculture and inspired by France imported Bordeaux varieties such as Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Malbec and Carmenère and hired French experts; this was the origin of the great historic estates around Santiago, many of them in the Maipo Valley.

These plantings were made shortly before phylloxera devastated Europe. Thanks to its natural barriers, Chile was never affected by the pest, so most of its vines are still grown on their own roots. For much of the twentieth century production was aimed at the domestic market, with a restrictive legal framework and little investment. From the 1980s, with the arrival of stainless steel, new barrels and foreign capital, rapid export-led growth began. In 1994 the French ampelographer Jean-Michel Boursiquot identified as Carmenère many vines that were being grown as Merlot, and this variety, almost forgotten in Europe, became a hallmark of Chile.

Geography and climate

The country stretches more than 4,000 kilometres from north to south but is rarely more than 200 kilometres wide. Its vineyards are isolated by the Atacama Desert to the north, the Andes to the east, the Pacific Ocean to the west and the Patagonian ice fields to the south. The climate of the main areas is Mediterranean, with rainy winters and dry, sunny summers. The cold Humboldt Current cools the coast and creates morning fogs, while air descending from the mountains cools the nights in the inland valleys. As a result, the climatic differences between coast and mountains are as important as those from north to south.

Soils

In the Central Valley, alluvial and colluvial soils of Andean origin dominate, with gravels, sands and clays; in the Coastal Range there are granitic soils and, in some areas, schists and slates. Further south, in Itata and Biobío, decomposed granite and soils of volcanic origin are common.

Regions

The appellation of origin system, in force since the mid-1990s, divides the country into broad regions, subregions and zones: Atacama and Coquimbo in the north, with the Elqui and Limarí valleys; Aconcagua, which includes the Aconcagua, Casablanca and San Antonio valleys; the Central Valley, with Maipo, Rapel (Cachapoal and Colchagua), Curicó and Maule; and the Southern Region, with Itata, Biobío and Malleco, plus small southernmost areas. Since the early 2010s labels may add the terms Costa, Entre Cordilleras and Andes, reflecting the west-to-east division of the territory.

Grapes and styles

Cabernet Sauvignon is the most planted red and produces highly regarded wines in Maipo, often with notes of cassis and herbs. Carmenère gives deeply coloured reds with ripe fruit and a characteristic spicy, herbaceous touch. Merlot, Syrah, Pinot Noir from coastal areas and old-vine Carignan from Maule also stand out. In the south, País and Cinsault from century-old dry-farmed vineyards have enjoyed a remarkable revival. Among whites, Sauvignon Blanc and Chardonnay from coastal valleys such as Casablanca, San Antonio and Limarí stand out, along with the traditional Muscat of Alexandria of the south and north, where it is also used for pisco.

Key grapes
Cabernet Sauvignon, Carmenère, Merlot, Syrah (Shiraz), Pinot Noir, Carignan, País, Cinsault, Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay, Muscat

Regions & appellations

Maipo Valley

Designation of Origin (subregion) · The Maipo Valley, which surrounds Santiago, is the cradle of modern Chilean viticulture and the area most associated with the country’s Cabernet Sauvignon. Many of the historic estates founded in the nineteenth century are located there.

Colchagua Valley

Designation of Origin (zone within the Rapel Valley) · The Colchagua Valley, in the southern part of the Rapel Valley, is one of Chile’s best-known red wine areas. Apalta, with its horseshoe-shaped slopes, is its most famous sector.

Casablanca Valley

Designation of Origin (subregion) · The Casablanca Valley, between Santiago and Valparaíso, was the first major cool coastal area in Chilean viticulture. It is known for its Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay and Pinot Noir.

Aconcagua Valley

Designation of Origin (region and subregion) · The Aconcagua Valley, north of Santiago, takes its name from the Aconcagua River, which flows down from the Andes not far from the highest mountain in the Americas. It combines a warm inland sector with a red wine tradition and recent cool-climate coastal vineyards.

Maule Valley

Designation of Origin (subregion) · The Maule Valley, at the southern end of the Central Valley, is the area with the largest vineyard surface in Chile. Alongside large-scale production, it retains old dry-farmed Carignan and País vines that have given rise to some of the country’s most original wines.

Itata Valley

Designation of Origin (subregion) · The Itata Valley, in Chile’s Southern Region, is one of the cradles of the country’s viticulture. Its old dry-farmed vineyards of País, Cinsault and Muscat of Alexandria, worked by smallholders, have been rediscovered over the last two decades.

Leyda Valley

Designation of Origin (zone within the San Antonio Valley) · A coastal zone of the San Antonio Valley, in the Valparaíso region, very close to the Pacific Ocean. Its cool climate, shaped by the Humboldt Current, has made it a Chilean benchmark for Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Noir and Chardonnay.

Curicó Valley

Designation of Origin (subregion of the Central Valley) · A subregion of Chile’s Central Valley, between Colchagua and Maule, watered by the Teno and Lontué rivers. It is one of the country’s most productive areas, with a wide diversity of white and red varieties.

Central Valley

Designation of Origin (wine region grouping Maipo, Rapel, Curicó and Maule) · Chile’s great wine region, a depression between the Andes and the Coastal Range stretching south of Santiago. It groups the Maipo, Rapel, Curicó and Maule valleys and holds most of the country’s vineyards.

Limarí Valley

Designation of Origin (subregion of the Coquimbo region) · A semi-arid valley in Chile’s Norte Chico, around Ovalle, cooled by the coastal fog known as camanchaca. Its limestone soils, rare in the country, give highly regarded Chardonnay, as well as Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Noir and Syrah.

Bío-Bío Valley

Designation of Origin (subregion of the Southern region) · A subregion in southern Chile, around the Biobío river, cooler and wetter than the central valleys. With a long tradition of País and Muscat, it now also stands out for aromatic whites and cool-climate Pinot Noir.
