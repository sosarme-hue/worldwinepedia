Americas

United States

The United States is one of the world’s major wine producers, led by California and with notable regions in Oregon, Washington and New York. Its appellation system, the AVAs, defines geographical areas without prescribing grape varieties or winemaking methods.

History

The first European settlers found many species of wild vine in North America, such as Vitis labrusca and Vitis riparia, but for centuries their attempts to grow the European vine, Vitis vinifera, on the East Coast failed because of disease, harsh winters and phylloxera, an insect native to that same region. In the nineteenth century a viticulture based on native and hybrid varieties prospered, with grapes such as Catawba in Ohio and Concord. In the West, Spanish missionaries brought the vine to California in the late eighteenth century with the so-called Mission grape, identical to Chile’s País and to the Listán Prieto of the Canary Islands. The Gold Rush and the arrival of European immigrants later drove the growth of Californian vineyards, with figures such as the Hungarian Agoston Haraszthy.

Prohibition, in force from 1920 to 1933, devastated the industry: many wineries closed and vineyards were replanted with grapes suited to shipping and home winemaking. Recovery was slow. From the 1960s a new generation of producers, symbolised by the winery Robert Mondavi founded in Napa in 1966, committed itself to quality varietal wines. In 1976, at the tasting known as the Judgment of Paris, a Californian Cabernet Sauvignon and a Californian Chardonnay were preferred by French judges over wines from Bordeaux and Burgundy, an episode that brought American wine great international attention. Since then vineyards have spread across the country, although California still accounts for the great majority of production.

Climate and soils

Diversity is enormous. The Californian coast has a Mediterranean climate, with dry summers and a decisive Pacific influence: the cold California Current generates fogs and breezes that push through valleys and gaps in the Coast Ranges and cool areas that would otherwise be very hot. In Washington State most vineyards lie east of the Cascade Range, in a semi-arid, sunny climate with cold winters, where irrigation is essential. Oregon, above all the Willamette Valley, offers a cooler, damper climate well suited to Pinot Noir. In New York State, the Finger Lakes owe their viticulture to the moderating effect of large lakes of glacial origin. Soils range from the volcanic and alluvial soils of Napa to the deposits of the Ice Age Missoula floods in the Northwest and the shales of upstate New York.

Grapes and styles

The United States has built its wine identity on varietal wines, which carry the name of the grape on the label. Napa Cabernet Sauvignon and Californian Chardonnay are its most recognisable styles, together with Pinot Noir from Sonoma, Santa Barbara and Oregon. Zinfandel, genetically identical to Italy’s Primitivo, is almost a Californian emblem, often accompanied by Petite Sirah in old vineyards. Washington stands out for Merlot, Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah and Riesling, and the Finger Lakes for Riesling. There are also Bordeaux-style blends, sometimes called Meritage, wines from Rhône varieties and traditional-method sparkling wines.

Regulations: the AVA system

American appellations are called American Viticultural Areas (AVAs). The system was created in the late 1970s and the first AVA, Augusta in Missouri, was recognised in 1980; today it is administered by the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau (TTB), a federal agency. Unlike European appellations, an AVA only defines an area with distinctive geographical features: it does not regulate grape varieties, yields, ageing or winemaking methods. Many AVAs overlap or are nested inside one another, such as Oakville within Napa Valley. There are also political appellations, such as the name of a state or a county.

Federal labelling rules set minimums: a wine bearing an AVA name must come from at least 85% grapes grown in that area, one with a state or county name from at least 75%, and a varietal wine must contain at least 75% of the named variety. Some states are stricter: Oregon, for example, requires a higher percentage for most varietal wines.

State-by-state sales

After the end of Prohibition, the Twenty-first Amendment to the Constitution left the regulation of alcohol sales to each state. This gave rise to the so-called three-tier system, which separates producer, wholesale distributor and retailer, although each state applies it in its own way. Rules on direct shipping from winery to consumer, sales in supermarkets or state distribution monopolies vary widely from one state to another, and some counties even maintain local restrictions on alcohol sales.

Regions & appellations

Napa Valley

AVA · A valley north of San Francisco Bay and the most famous wine region in the United States, known above all for its Cabernet Sauvignon. It was the first AVA recognised in California.

Sonoma

County with several AVAs · Sonoma County, between the Pacific and Napa, brings together a wide range of climates and AVAs, from the cool Russian River Valley and Sonoma Coast to the warm Dry Creek and Alexander valleys.

Paso Robles

AVA · A large AVA on California’s Central Coast, halfway between San Francisco and Los Angeles, known for its Zinfandel, its blends of Rhône varieties and its Cabernet Sauvignon.

Santa Barbara

County with several AVAs · Santa Barbara County, at the southern end of California’s Central Coast, owes its coolness to east-west valleys that funnel in Pacific fog. It is a benchmark for Californian Pinot Noir and Chardonnay.

Willamette Valley

AVA · A valley in north-west Oregon, between the Coast Range and the Cascades, which has become the leading American reference for cool-climate Pinot Noir.

Columbia Valley

AVA · A large AVA in Washington State, with a small part in Oregon, lying east of the Cascades in a semi-arid climate with sunny summers. It contains most of Washington’s vineyards.

Finger Lakes

AVA · A region in western New York State, around several long lakes of glacial origin, known above all for its cool-climate Riesling.

Russian River Valley

AVA (within Sonoma County) · An AVA in Sonoma County, California, cooled by fog flowing in from the Pacific. It is one of America’s benchmark areas for Pinot Noir and Chardonnay, and also preserves old Zinfandel vines.

Los Carneros

AVA (shared by Napa and Sonoma counties) · An AVA at the southern end of the Napa and Sonoma valleys, beside San Pablo Bay. Cool and windy, it is a classic area for Pinot Noir, Chardonnay and base wines for sparkling wine.

Santa Cruz Mountains

AVA · A mountain AVA south of San Francisco, between the Pacific and the bay, defined by the elevation of its vineyards. Small and scattered among forests, it is one of the historic cradles of fine Californian wine, with long-lived Cabernet Sauvignon, Pinot Noir and Chardonnay.

Lodi

AVA, with seven sub-AVAs · An AVA in the northern Central Valley of California, between Sacramento and Stockton, cooled by breezes from the Delta. It is one of the state’s most productive areas and a great reservoir of old Zinfandel vines.

Sta. Rita Hills

AVA (within Santa Ynez Valley, Santa Barbara County) · An AVA in the western Santa Ynez Valley, in Santa Barbara County, open to Pacific winds and fog. It is one of California’s coolest areas and a benchmark for Pinot Noir and Chardonnay.

Walla Walla Valley

AVA (shared by Washington and Oregon, within Columbia Valley) · An AVA in south-eastern Washington State that also extends into north-eastern Oregon. With a dry continental climate, it is known for Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and Syrah reds and for the cobblestone soils of The Rocks District.
