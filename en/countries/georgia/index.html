Georgia

Georgia, in the Caucasus, is home to some of the oldest evidence of winemaking in the world. It preserves an enormous diversity of native grape varieties and traditional winemaking in qvevri, large buried clay vessels.

History

Archaeological finds at Neolithic sites in southern Georgia, with wine residues in pottery fragments dated to around 6000 BC, are among the oldest known evidence of winemaking. Since then, wine has been bound up with everyday life, religion and the identity of the country. After Christianisation in the fourth century, the vine took on strong symbolic value: according to tradition, Saint Nino, who brought Christianity to Georgia, carried a cross made of vine stems. Monasteries such as Alaverdi, in Kakheti, have kept vineyards and cellars for centuries.

In the nineteenth century, under the Russian Empire, Georgian aristocrats such as the Chavchavadze family at Tsinandali introduced European-style winemaking techniques alongside traditional ones. During the Soviet period, Georgia was one of the main wine suppliers of the USSR; production was concentrated in large state complexes, volume and a handful of varieties were favoured, and semi-sweet reds became popular. The anti-alcohol campaign of the mid-1980s led to the uprooting of many vineyards. After independence in 1991 the sector was privatised, and the Russian embargo imposed in 2006 pushed producers to seek other markets and to improve quality. Since then many family wineries have emerged and interest in local grapes and qvevri winemaking has revived; UNESCO added the qvevri method to its list of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2013.

Climate and soils

The Greater Caucasus range shields the country from cold air masses from the north, while the Likhi range divides it into two large zones. The west, open to the Black Sea, has a humid, temperate climate, subtropical on the coast. The east, where Kakheti lies, is drier and continental, with warm summers and cold winters. Soils are highly varied: alluvial in the river valleys, clay-limestone, brown and stony on the slopes.

Grapes and styles

Georgia preserves more than five hundred native grape varieties, although only a few dozen are grown commercially. The most widely planted white is Rkatsiteli, followed by varieties such as Mtsvane Kakhuri, Kisi and Khikhvi in Kakheti, or Tsolikouri, Tsitska and Krakhuna in the west. The emblematic red is Saperavi, with coloured flesh, which gives deep, acidic wines; in the west, Aleksandrouli and Mujuretuli from Racha, used for the semi-sweet Khvanchkara, and Ojaleshi from Samegrelo stand out.

Two broad winemaking methods coexist. In the traditional one, the must ferments in the qvevri, often with skins, seeds and sometimes stems, and the wine may stay in contact with them for months before being racked. Applied to white grapes, this method produces the so-called amber or orange wines, tannic and aromatically complex. Alongside it, “European” winemaking is practised in steel tanks or barrels. Many wineries also make natural semi-sweet wines, in which fermentation is stopped to retain some of the grape sugar, and a pomace brandy, chacha.

Regions and regulations

Kakheti, in the east, holds most of the vineyard area. Other regions are Kartli, around Tbilisi, Imereti, Racha-Lechkhumi, Guria, Samegrelo and Adjara. The country has more than twenty designations of origin, many of them in Kakheti, such as Tsinandali, Mukuzani, Napareuli and Kindzmarauli, which regulate grapes, zones and styles.

Regions & appellations

Kakheti

Wine region (with several designations of origin) · A region in eastern Georgia, in the Alazani and Iori valleys, which holds most of the country’s vineyards. It is the heartland of traditional qvevri winemaking and of varieties such as Saperavi and Rkatsiteli.
