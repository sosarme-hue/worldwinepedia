New Zealand

New Zealand is a cool-climate producer of modest size that made its name worldwide with Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc. Today it also stands out for its Pinot Noir from Central Otago and Martinborough and for the reds of Hawke's Bay.

History

New Zealand’s first vines were planted in 1819 by the missionary Samuel Marsden at Kerikeri, in the far north of the North Island. In the 1830s James Busby, who had previously promoted winegrowing in Australia, made wine at Waitangi. In 1851 Marist missionaries founded in Hawke’s Bay the winery now known as Mission Estate, regarded as the oldest in the country still operating. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries Dalmatian immigrants, from present-day Croatia, set up small wineries around Auckland, and several of the family businesses they founded are still active.

For much of the twentieth century the industry was small and focused on fortified wines and simple whites, held back also by phylloxera, prohibitionist movements and restrictive laws on the sale of alcohol. The great transformation began in the 1970s. In 1973 the Montana company planted the first commercial vineyards in Marlborough, in the north of the South Island, and in the late 1970s and the 1980s the region’s intensely aromatic Sauvignon Blancs drew international attention. At the same time, Müller-Thurgau, which had been the most widely planted variety, gradually gave way to the classic French grapes. From the 1980s and 1990s Pinot Noir became established in Martinborough and Central Otago, and the country built a solid reputation as a producer of cool-climate wines.

Climate and soils

New Zealand stretches some 1,500 kilometres from north to south, with vineyards roughly between latitudes 36 and 45 degrees south. No part of the country is very far from the sea, so the climate is mostly maritime and cool, with a long ripening season, bright light and intense ultraviolet radiation. Cool nights preserve the grapes’ acidity and aromas. The exception is Central Otago, inland in the South Island, which has a continental climate with warm, dry summers and cold winters. Many vineyards lie on young alluvial soils of gravel and silt laid down by rivers; there are also clays, loess and, in Central Otago, soils derived from schist.

Grapes and styles

Sauvignon Blanc is by far the most widely planted variety and the basis of most exports. The Marlborough style, lively and highly aromatic, with notes of citrus, passion fruit and fresh-cut grass, has become a worldwide reference. Pinot Noir is the main red grape and gives wines of pure fruit and fine tannins in Martinborough, Marlborough, Central Otago and other areas. Chardonnay is also made in several regions, along with Pinot Gris, Riesling and traditional-method sparkling wines. In Hawke’s Bay, the warmest of the major regions, Bordeaux-style blends based on Merlot and Cabernet Sauvignon are produced, as well as fresh, spicy Syrah.

Regulations and practices

New Zealand has a register of geographical indications that protects the names of its wine regions but does not impose grape varieties or winemaking methods. The industry has been marked by a strong commitment to sustainability, organised around the Sustainable Winegrowing New Zealand programme, and by the very early and almost universal adoption of the screwcap, promoted from 2001 by an initiative of the country’s producers.

Key grapes
Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Noir, Chardonnay, Pinot Gris, Riesling, Merlot, Syrah (Shiraz), Müller-Thurgau

Regions & appellations

Marlborough

Geographical Indication (GI) · Marlborough, at the north-eastern tip of the South Island, is New Zealand’s largest wine region. Its intensely aromatic Sauvignon Blanc brought international fame to New Zealand wine.

Central Otago

Geographical Indication (GI) · Central Otago, in the mountainous interior of the southern South Island, is one of the world’s most southerly wine regions and the only one in New Zealand with a continental climate. It is famous for its Pinot Noir.

Hawke's Bay

Geographical Indication (GI) · Hawke’s Bay, on the east coast of the North Island, is one of New Zealand’s oldest wine regions and the leading one for Bordeaux-style reds and Syrah.

Martinborough

Geographical Indication (GI) · Martinborough, in the south of the Wairarapa region on the North Island, is a small wine area that pioneered quality Pinot Noir in New Zealand.
