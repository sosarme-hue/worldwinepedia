Mexico

Mexico is the cradle of viticulture in the Americas, where the vine arrived with the Spanish conquerors in the sixteenth century. Today most of its wine comes from Baja California, especially the Valle de Guadalupe, although production is growing in high-altitude areas in the centre and north of the country.

History

Mexico was the first place in the Americas where European vines were planted. After the conquest, the Spanish needed wine for Mass and for their own consumption, and in 1524 Hernán Cortés ordered the Spaniards who received land and indigenous labour in encomienda to plant vines every year. Cultivation spread rapidly, but the Crown, keen to protect wine exports from the peninsula, later restricted new plantings and production in the colony. Even so, viticulture survived on haciendas and in religious missions. In Parras, in today’s state of Coahuila, the hacienda that gave rise to Casa Madero, regarded as the oldest winery in the Americas, was founded at the end of the sixteenth century.

In Baja California, Jesuit missionaries and, later, Dominicans and Franciscans planted vines in their missions from the late seventeenth century; the Santo Tomás mission, founded by the Dominicans at the end of the eighteenth century, gave its name to the Santo Tomás winery, established in Ensenada in 1888. At the beginning of the twentieth century Russian settlers from the Molokan religious community settled in the Valle de Guadalupe, growing cereals and vines, and in the following decades Italian immigrants founded companies that would become major producers. For much of the twentieth century, however, a large share of Mexican grapes went into brandy. From the late 1980s, the emergence of small and medium-sized wineries focused on quality wine transformed the Valle de Guadalupe, which is today also a destination for wine tourism and gastronomy.

Climate and regions

Although much of the country lies within the tropics, Mexico has areas suited to viticulture thanks to ocean influence or altitude. Baja California, in the north-west, accounts for most wine production. There, valleys such as Guadalupe, San Vicente, Santo Tomás, Ojos Negros and San Antonio de las Minas have a semi-arid Mediterranean climate, with scant winter rain and dry, sunny summers moderated by cool Pacific breezes. Water scarcity and quality, sometimes slightly saline, are the main challenge.

In the centre and north of the country viticulture relies on altitude, which offsets latitude with cool nights and wide diurnal temperature ranges. Coahuila, with the Parras valley; Querétaro, known for its sparkling wines; Zacatecas, Aguascalientes, Guanajuato, San Luis Potosí and Chihuahua all have high-altitude vineyards, and in several of these areas summer rains coincide with ripening and call for careful management.

Soils

In the valleys of Baja California sandy and sandy-clay soils derived from decomposed granite predominate; they are poor in organic matter and well drained, with more clayey areas on the valley floors. In the high-altitude regions of the interior, alluvial and volcanic soils are common.

Varieties and styles

Mexico produces mainly red wine, generally ripe in profile with intense fruit, often with saline notes in the wines of Baja California. Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Tempranillo, Syrah, Grenache, Carignan, Malbec and Petite Sirah are widespread, and Nebbiolo, introduced by Italian immigrants, has become a characteristic variety of the Valle de Guadalupe, although the wines it gives there are very different from those of Piedmont. Blends of several varieties are common. Among whites, Chenin Blanc, Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay and Colombard stand out. In Querétaro sparkling wines are made by the traditional method and in sealed tanks.

Regulations

Mexico does not have a system of wine designations of origin comparable to European ones. Wineries state the valley or region of origin on the label, and producer associations have worked on quality standards and on the recognition of geographical indications for the main areas.

Regions & appellations

Valle de Guadalupe

Wine valley · The Valle de Guadalupe, in the municipality of Ensenada (Baja California), is Mexico’s best-known wine region. With a semi-arid Mediterranean climate and granitic soils, it produces mainly ripe blended reds and has become a leading destination for wine tourism and gastronomy.
