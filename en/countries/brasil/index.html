Brazil

Brazil produces wine mainly in the state of Rio Grande do Sul, where viticulture was driven by Italian immigrants. It has become known especially for its fresh, aromatic sparkling wines, and it also has unusual areas of tropical viticulture.

History

Portuguese colonists introduced the vine to Brazil in the sixteenth century, and the Jesuits grew it in their southern missions, but for a long time viticulture was of little importance. The great impetus came from 1875, with the arrival in the highlands of the state of Rio Grande do Sul of thousands of immigrants from northern Italy, mainly from Veneto and Trentino. In the colonies they founded, the origin of towns such as Bento Gonçalves, Caxias do Sul and Garibaldi, they planted vines and made wine according to their traditions. Because of the humid climate, American varieties such as Isabel, more resistant to fungal diseases, spread most widely.

During the twentieth century large cooperatives were formed and, from the 1970s, foreign companies set up in the Serra Gaúcha, including subsidiaries of international wine groups, which helped spread European varieties and modern techniques, above all in sparkling wine production. From the 1990s many family wineries of Italian origin began to bottle Vitis vinifera wines with quality ambitions. Today, however, a very large share of the country’s grapes still comes from American and hybrid varieties, used for grape juice and everyday wines.

Climate and soils

Brazil is a largely tropical country, and its viticulture adapts to very different conditions. In the Serra Gaúcha, the main region, the climate is temperate and humid, with abundant rain even during ripening, which calls for attentive vineyard management and favours wines of moderate alcohol and good acidity, well suited to sparkling wine. The soils are of basaltic origin, often clayey. Further south, the Campanha Gaúcha, on the border with Uruguay, is drier and flatter and produces riper reds. In the state of Santa Catarina, the high-altitude areas of the Catarinense plateau offer a cool climate. In the São Francisco river valley, in the semi-arid north-east, irrigated tropical viticulture is practised and, with no winter, more than one harvest a year is possible.

Regions

Rio Grande do Sul accounts for most of the country’s wine production. The Serra Gaúcha is home to the Vale dos Vinhedos, the first geographical indication recognised in the country, and other areas such as Pinto Bandeira, Farroupilha and Altos Montes. Alongside the Campanha Gaúcha and the Serra do Sudeste in Rio Grande do Sul, the map is completed by the Santa Catarina plateau, the São Francisco valley, between Pernambuco and Bahia, and, more recently, parts of the states of Minas Gerais and São Paulo where the vine’s cycle is altered by double pruning so that grapes are harvested in the dry winter season.

Styles and varieties

Sparkling wines are the most recognised style of Brazilian wine. They are made both by the traditional method, with a second fermentation in bottle, and in sealed tanks, from Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Riesling Itálico, and there is also a distinctive tradition of sweet, aromatic sparkling Moscatel, associated especially with Farroupilha. Among reds Merlot stands out, having adapted well to the Serra Gaúcha, along with Cabernet Sauvignon, Cabernet Franc, Tannat and Teroldego; among whites, Chardonnay and Sauvignon Blanc. American varieties such as Isabel and Bordô remain essential for juice and table wine.

Regulations

Brazil has developed a two-tier system of geographical indications: the Indicação de Procedência (IP), based on an area’s reputation, and the Denominação de Origem (DO), with stricter rules on varieties, yields and production. The Vale dos Vinhedos was the first area recognised as an IP and later obtained DO status, under which Merlot is the main red variety and Chardonnay the main white for sparkling wines.

Key grapes
Merlot, Chardonnay, Pinot Noir, Cabernet Sauvignon, Muscat, Tannat

Regions & appellations

Serra Gaúcha

Wine region (with IP and DO geographical indications) · The Serra Gaúcha, in the north-east of the state of Rio Grande do Sul, is Brazil’s leading wine region. Settled by Italian immigrants from 1875, it is known above all for its sparkling wines and is home to the Vale dos Vinhedos, the country’s first designation of origin.
