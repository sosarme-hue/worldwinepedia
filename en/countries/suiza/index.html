Switzerland

Switzerland is a small and highly diverse wine country of mountain and lakeside vineyards, which drinks most of what it produces. It is the home of Chasselas and preserves a remarkable heritage of native grape varieties, above all in the Valais.

History

Vines were already grown in what is now Switzerland in Roman times, when legions and settlers spread wine along the Rhône and Rhine valleys and the shores of the lakes. After the fall of the Empire it was mainly the monasteries that maintained and extended the vineyards. In the twelfth century Cistercian monks began clearing and terracing the slopes above Lake Geneva, in what is now Lavaux, and through the Middle Ages and early modern period wine became an essential part of the economy of many cantons. In the nineteenth century powdery mildew, downy mildew and phylloxera devastated much of the vineyard area, which shrank considerably and was replanted on American rootstocks, concentrating on the best slopes.

For much of the twentieth century the sector was sheltered by an agricultural policy that limited imports of white wine, which encouraged almost purely domestic consumption. The gradual opening of the market from the 1990s forced producers to raise quality, cut yields and place new value on local varieties. Today Switzerland exports very little wine, so its finest bottles remain relatively unknown outside the country.

Climate and soils

Swiss vineyards lie in conditions that are, in principle, marginal for the vine, and they depend on favourable microclimates. The great lakes, such as Lake Geneva, Lake Neuchâtel and the lakes of Italian-speaking Switzerland, moderate temperatures and reflect sunlight onto the slopes. In the Valais, the upper Rhône valley is sheltered by mountains and is one of the driest and sunniest places in the country, although the vines often need irrigation. The warm southerly wind known as the foehn helps the grapes ripen in autumn. Soils are extremely varied: moraines and glacial deposits, molasse, limestone, schist, gneiss and alluvium, often on slopes so steep that they require terraces held up by stone walls.

Regions

Six main regions are usually distinguished. The Valais is the canton with the most vineyard land and the greatest range of varieties. Vaud, with areas such as La Côte, Lavaux and the Chablais, is the great territory of Chasselas. Geneva has a large and relatively flat vineyard area around the city. The Three Lakes region (Neuchâtel, Biel and Murten) is known for its Pinot Noir and its Œil-de-Perdrix rosés. German-speaking Switzerland, from the canton of Graubünden to Zurich and Schaffhausen, focuses on Pinot Noir (Blauburgunder) and Müller-Thurgau, while Italian-speaking Ticino has made Merlot its main variety.

Grapes and styles

The most characteristic white grape is Chasselas, called Fendant in the Valais, which gives light whites with moderate acidity and a great capacity to reflect their terroir. The most widely planted red is Pinot Noir, followed by Gamay, Merlot and Gamaret, a cross created in Switzerland. The Valais preserves old varieties such as Petite Arvine, Amigne, Humagne Blanche, Humagne Rouge and Cornalin, and calls Silvaner Johannisberg. Styles range from fresh, dry whites to elegant Pinot Noir reds and sweet wines made from grapes shrivelled on the vine.

Regulations

The organisation of appellations largely rests with the cantons, which define their own Appellations d’origine contrôlée (AOC) and, in some cases, terms such as Grand Cru or Premier Grand Cru, with criteria that vary from one canton to another. In Vaud, for example, Dézaley and Calamin in Lavaux are Grand Cru appellations. This regulatory diversity reflects the country’s federal structure and the strong identity of each wine region.

Regions & appellations

Valais

AOC Valais · The Valais, in the upper Rhône valley, is Switzerland’s largest wine canton and the one with the greatest diversity of varieties. Its dry, sunny terraced slopes give Fendant, Petite Arvine, Pinot Noir and a long list of native grapes.

Vaud

AOC Vaud · The canton of Vaud, on the shores of Lake Geneva, is the great territory of Chasselas. Its Lavaux terraces, a World Heritage Site, and areas such as La Côte and the Chablais give delicate whites that clearly express their place of origin.
