Croatia

Croatia brings together two wine worlds: the continental interior, dominated by Graševina whites, and the Adriatic coast, from Istria to Dalmatia, with Plavac Mali reds and a wealth of native varieties. It is also the homeland of the grape known in California as Zinfandel.

History

The coast and islands of the eastern Adriatic have one of the oldest winegrowing traditions in Europe. Greek settlers who established themselves on islands such as Vis, Hvar and Korčula from the fourth century BC grew vines, and the Romans later spread viticulture across the whole region. In the Middle Ages and the early modern period the Dalmatian coast was long under Venetian rule, while the interior was linked to Hungary and the Habsburgs, which partly explains the differences between the wine traditions of the coast and the mainland.

In the nineteenth century phylloxera reached Dalmatia later than France, so for some years Dalmatian wines were exported in large quantities. When the pest finally reached the region, the ruin of many growers contributed to large-scale emigration to the Americas and Oceania; some of those emigrants played an important part in the beginnings of modern winegrowing in New Zealand. In the twentieth century, within Yugoslavia, large cooperatives predominated. After independence in 1991 many private wineries emerged, and Croatia joined the European Union in 2013.

Climate and soils

Inland the climate is continental, with cold winters and warm summers, and the vineyards occupy hills of loess, clay and marl. On the coast the climate is Mediterranean, with dry summers, abundant light and the influence of the sea; soils are karstic, of limestone and dolomite, often covered with terra rossa. In Istria, red soils, white soils of marly flysch and grey soils are traditionally distinguished.

Regions

The regulations group the wine areas into continental Croatia and coastal Croatia, each divided into subregions, but four broad areas are usually described. Slavonia and the Danube, in the east, is the great territory of Graševina, with areas such as Kutjevo and Ilok, where Traminac (Gewürztraminer) is also notable. The hills of central Croatia, around Zagreb and the Zagorje, mainly produce fresh whites. Istria and Kvarner are the home of Malvazija istarska, a local white variety, and of the red Teran. Dalmatia, with its coast and islands, is the region of Plavac Mali reds and of whites such as Pošip, Grk and Debit.

Grapes and styles

The most widely planted variety is Graševina, the Croatian name for Welschriesling, which gives fresh, fruity dry whites as well as late-harvest sweet wines. Malvazija istarska produces aromatic, savoury whites, sometimes aged in wood or macerated on their skins. Plavac Mali gives powerful, tannic reds on the slopes of the Dalmatian coast. Other local varieties include Babić, Teran, Pošip, Grk, Maraština, Vugava and Bogdanuša. Genetic studies showed that Crljenak Kaštelanski, also called Tribidrag, is the same variety as Californian Zinfandel and Italian Primitivo; this grape is also one of the parents of Plavac Mali. Dalmatia has a tradition of prošek, a sweet wine made from dried grapes.

Regulations

Croatian wines with a designation of origin are classified as kvalitetno vino (quality wine) or vrhunsko vino (premium quality wine). There are also designations for sweet wines, such as kasna berba (late harvest), izborna berba (selected harvest), prosušeno grožđe (dried grapes) and ledeno vino (ice wine). Some historic sites have their own protection; the best known is Dingač, on the Pelješac peninsula, which was the country’s first protected wine area.

Key grapes
Graševina (Welschriesling), Plavac Mali, Malvasia, Primitivo, Gewürztraminer, Blaufränkisch, Chardonnay

Dalmatia

Wine region · Dalmatia, the long coastal and island strip of southern Croatia, is the region of Plavac Mali reds and of a great variety of native grapes. Its vineyards, on karst slopes dropping to the Adriatic, include historic sites such as Dingač and Postup.
