Tuscany

Cypress-lined hills, walled towns and Sangiovese: from Chianti Classico to the Val d'Orcia, Italy's most famous wine landscape.

Tuscany is the heartland of Sangiovese, the red grape that, under various names and styles, makes Chianti Classico, Brunello di Montalcino and Vino Nobile di Montepulciano.

Chianti Classico

Between Florence and Siena, the old Chiantigiana road crosses hills of vines, olives and woodland. Villages such as Greve in Chianti, Panzano, Castellina, Radda and Gaiole have wineries, castles and wine shops. The black rooster is the appellation's emblem.

Montalcino, Montepulciano and the Val d'Orcia

South of Siena, Montalcino, with its fortress, is the home of Brunello. A few kilometres away, Montepulciano hides historic cellars beneath its Renaissance palaces. Between the two lies the Val d'Orcia, a World Heritage site since 2004, with Pienza and its sheep's cheeses.

The coast: Bolgheri

On the coast, Bolgheri became famous from the 1970s for its Cabernet and Merlot reds. Its straight avenue of cypresses is one of the region's best-known images. San Gimignano, with its towers and white Vernaccia, completes the route.

What to see

  • The Chiantigiana road.
  • Montalcino fortress.
  • Montepulciano's underground cellars.
  • Pienza and the Val d'Orcia.
  • Bolgheri's Viale dei Cipressi.

When to go

Spring and autumn (harvest in September–October); summer is hot and busy.

Food

Bistecca alla fiorentina, pici, ribollita, Pienza pecorino and new olive oil in November.

Tip

Many estates offer accommodation (agriturismo): sleeping among the vines saves driving after tastings.
