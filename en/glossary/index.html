Glossary

Wine terms explained in a few words.

results
#ABCDEFGHIKLMNOPQRSTUVWY

#

1855 Classification

The official classification of Bordeaux wines drawn up for the 1855 Paris Universal Exhibition. It ranks the reds of the Médoc, plus Château Haut-Brion, in five growths, and the sweet whites of Sauternes and Barsac separately.

A

Acidification

The authorised addition of acid, usually tartaric, to must or wine to correct insufficient acidity. It is common in warm regions or in very hot vintages.

Acidity

The freshness and liveliness given by the wine's acids, chiefly tartaric, malic, lactic and citric. It is key to balance, mouthfeel and ageing potential.

Aeration

Exposing wine to air, in the glass or a decanter, so that it expresses its aromas better. It can soften some young wines or help dissipate reductive smells.

Aftertaste

The aromatic and taste sensations that linger or appear after swallowing the wine, perceived largely retronasally.

Age-worthy wine

A wine with the structure, acidity and concentration needed to improve for years in bottle.

Albariza

A white soil very rich in calcium carbonate and containing diatom remains, characteristic of the Jerez area. It retains winter rainfall and reflects light, and underpins the region's Palomino wines.

Alcohol content (ABV)

The percentage of ethyl alcohol by volume in a wine, measured at 20 °C and mandatory on the label.

Alcoholic fermentation

The process by which yeasts convert the sugars in must into ethyl alcohol and carbon dioxide, releasing heat. It is the essential transformation that turns must into wine.

American oak

Wood from North American white oak (Quercus alba) used in cooperage. It gives pronounced aromas of vanilla, coconut and sweet notes, and has been traditional in Rioja.

Amontillado

A fortified wine from Jerez or Montilla-Moriles that begins ageing under flor and continues oxidatively once the flor disappears. It is dry and amber-coloured, with nutty notes and great complexity.

Ampelography

The discipline that studies, describes and identifies grape varieties from their leaves, bunches, berries and other features. Today it is complemented by DNA analysis.

Amphora

A fired clay vessel used to ferment or age wine, with a tradition going back thousands of years. Its porosity allows slight oxygenation without adding wood aromas.

Ancestral method

A sparkling-wine method in which the wine is bottled before the first fermentation has finished, so that it ends in the bottle and creates the bubbles. It is the basis of pét-nat wines and some traditional ones such as Blanquette de Limoux méthode ancestrale.

Anthocyanins

Pigments of the polyphenol family responsible for the red and purple colour of black grapes. They are found in the skins and are extracted during maceration.

AOC / AOP

Appellation d'Origine Contrôlée, the French system of designations of origin that regulates area, grape varieties, yields and winemaking practices. AOP (Appellation d'Origine Protégée) is its equivalent in EU terminology.

Appassimento

An Italian technique of drying grapes after harvest, in crates or on mats in ventilated rooms, to concentrate sugars and aromas. It is the basis of wines such as Amarone, Recioto and many passiti.

Aspect

The orientation of a vineyard in relation to the sun, which affects the light and heat the vines receive. In cool climates sun-facing slopes are sought to aid ripening.

Astringency

A tactile sensation of dryness and roughness in the mouth caused by tannins binding to proteins in saliva. It is more pronounced in young, tannic reds.

Auslese

A category of German Prädikatswein made from selected, very ripe bunches, sometimes affected by botrytis. It can be dry or, more often, sweet.

Autolysis

The breakdown of dead yeast cells in contact with the wine during ageing on lees. It adds texture and aromas of bread, brioche or nuts, very characteristic of traditional-method sparkling wines.

B

Back label

A label on the back of the bottle with additional information. In many Spanish appellations it includes a numbered seal from the Regulatory Council guaranteeing origin and category.

Balance

Harmony between the components of a wine, such as acidity, alcohol, tannins, sugar and fruit, with none dominating excessively. It is one of the main criteria of quality.

Barrel

A wooden vessel, usually oak, used to ferment or age wine. The most common are the 225-litre Bordeaux barrel and the Burgundy barrel of about 228 litres.

Barrel fermentation

Fermenting must in oak barrels rather than in tanks, widely used for quality white wines. It integrates the oak better and adds volume and complexity.

Base wine

A still wine, usually high in acidity and moderate in alcohol, that undergoes a second fermentation to make a sparkling wine.

Beerenauslese

A category of German Prädikatswein made from overripe berries selected one by one, generally affected by noble rot. It produces sweet, concentrated and long-lived wines.

Berry

Each of the individual grapes that make up the bunch. It consists of skin, pulp and seeds.

Biodynamic wine

Wine from vineyards farmed according to biodynamics, based on the ideas of Rudolf Steiner, which adds specific preparations and a calendar linked to lunar and cosmic cycles to organic farming. There are private certifications such as Demeter and Biodyvin.

Biological ageing

Ageing beneath a layer of yeasts, known as flor, which protects the wine from oxygen and changes its composition. It is characteristic of fino and manzanilla, and also of Jura's vin jaune.

Blanc de blancs

A white wine, especially a sparkling one, made exclusively from white grapes. In Champagne it is usually Chardonnay.

Blanc de noirs

A white wine, usually sparkling, made from black grapes whose juice is separated quickly from the skins so that it does not take on colour. In Champagne it comes from Pinot Noir and/or Meunier.

Blend

The blending of wines from different varieties, plots, tanks or vintages to obtain a more balanced or complex final wine.

Blending (cabeceo)

In the Jerez area, the blending of different wines to obtain a particular style, especially the sweetened fortified wines such as Cream or Medium. It combines dry wines with sweet wines or concentrated musts.

Blind tasting

A tasting in which the identity of the wines is unknown and the bottles are hidden. It seeks an assessment free from bias about brand, price or origin.

Body

The sensation of weight and volume of wine in the mouth, linked mainly to alcohol, extract and, where present, sugar. A wine can be light, medium- or full-bodied.

Botrytis cinerea

A fungus that attacks grapes. In damp conditions it causes harmful grey rot, but with alternating morning mists and dry afternoons it gives rise to noble rot, which concentrates sugars and makes great sweet wines possible.

Bottle ageing

The period a wine spends in bottle before or after release, in an almost oxygen-free environment. It favours the integration of components and the development of bouquet.

Bottling

Filling and closing bottles at the end of winemaking or of ageing in tank or barrel. It must be done carefully to avoid oxidation and contamination.

Bouquet

The set of complex aromas a wine develops with ageing, especially in bottle. It is distinguished from the primary aromas of the grape.

Brett (Brettanomyces)

A spoilage yeast that produces compounds such as 4-ethylphenol, with smells of stable, leather, horse sweat or sticking plaster. In excess it is considered a fault.

Brut

A sweetness designation for sparkling wine corresponding to less than 12 grams of sugar per litre. It is the most common style in Cava and Champagne.

Brut Nature

A sparkling wine to which no sugar is added in the dosage and which contains less than 3 grams of sugar per litre. It is the driest style.

Bud

The organ of the vine located at the nodes of the canes from which shoots, leaves and bunches develop. A set number of buds per vine is left at pruning.

Budbreak

The moment in spring when the vine's buds open and the first shoots appear, marking the start of the growing cycle. At this stage the vine is especially vulnerable to frost.

Bunch (cluster)

The group of berries attached to the stem that forms the fruit of the vine. Its size and compactness vary with the variety and growing conditions.

Bush vine (gobelet)

A traditional training system in which the vine grows freely, without wires, with a short trunk and several arms forming a cup shape. It is common in hot, dry Mediterranean climates.

Butt

The traditional oak cask of the Jerez area, usually holding around 500 to 600 litres. It is used in the criadera and solera system and is not filled completely when the wine ages under flor.

Bâtonnage

The periodic stirring of the fine lees in barrel or tank to put them back into suspension. It adds volume, a creamy texture and protection against oxidation, especially in white wines.

C

Cane

A one-year-old vine shoot that has become woody after lignification. The following season's shoots grow from its buds, and it is the part that is cut during pruning.

Carbonic maceration

A technique in which whole bunches are enclosed in a tank saturated with carbon dioxide, so that fermentation begins inside the berries themselves. It produces very fruity, light reds, such as those of Beaujolais or traditional Rioja Alavesa wines.

Cask

A bulging wooden vessel made of staves held together by hoops, larger than a barrique. It is used to age or store wine.

Cava

A Spanish sparkling wine with designation of origin, made by the traditional method with second fermentation in bottle. Most is produced in Penedès, from varieties such as Macabeo, Xarel·lo and Parellada.

Cellar master (capataz)

The person in charge of the cellar in the Jerez area and other fortified-wine regions. They oversee ageing, withdrawals and the classification of the butts.

Chaptalisation

The addition of sugar to must before or during fermentation to raise the final alcohol level. It is permitted only in certain cool regions and is not authorised in countries such as Spain, Italy or Portugal.

Charmat method

A sparkling-wine method in which the second fermentation takes place in large pressurised tanks rather than in the bottle. In Spain it is also called granvás; it is the usual method for Prosecco.

Château

A French term for a wine estate with its vineyards and cellar, very common in Bordeaux. It does not necessarily imply the existence of a castle.

Clarete

In Spain, a wine whose colour lies between rosé and red, traditionally made from red and white grapes or with a short maceration, as in Cigales. In English, by contrast, claret refers to red Bordeaux.

Clarification

The set of operations used to remove suspended particles and leave the wine clear and bright. It includes natural settling, fining, centrifugation and filtration.

Classico

An Italian designation indicating that the wine comes from the historic, original zone of an appellation, as in Soave Classico or Valpolicella Classico.

Clay

A soil component made up of very fine particles that retains water and nutrients well. Clay soils tend to give wines with good structure and body.

Climat

In Burgundy, a precisely delimited, named vineyard plot with specific natural characteristics, recognised for centuries. The climats of Burgundy have been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2015.

Clone

A group of plants obtained by vegetative propagation from a single vine selected for its qualities. Within the same variety there are clones with differences in productivity, bunch size or aromatic profile.

Clos

A vineyard enclosed by a wall, a very common term in Burgundy and other French regions. Today it is also used in other countries to designate a particular vineyard.

Closed

A wine that shows few aromas and flavours at a given moment, even though it has potential. It is common in young age-worthy wines or just after opening, and may improve with aeration or time.

Co-fermentation

Fermenting two or more varieties together in the same vessel rather than blending them afterwards. A classic example is Syrah fermented with a small proportion of Viognier in Côte-Rôtie.

Cold soak

Keeping crushed red grapes at low temperature for several days before fermentation. It favours the extraction of colour and fruity aromas with little tannin extraction.

Colheita

A single-vintage tawny Port, aged in wood for at least seven years before bottling. The label states the harvest year.

Colour intensity

The intensity or depth of a wine's colour, especially in reds. It is described as light, medium or deep according to the opacity observed in the glass.

Complexity

The quality of a wine that shows many layered aromas and flavours that evolve in the glass. It is usually considered a sign of quality.

Concrete tank

A concrete vessel for fermenting or storing wine, traditional in many wineries and revived today in shapes such as the egg. It offers good thermal inertia and, if unlined, slight micro-oxygenation.

Cooperage

The craft and industry of making barrels and casks, and also the workshop where they are made. It includes selecting and seasoning the wood, assembling the staves and toasting.

Cordon Royat

A pruning system in which the vine forms one or two permanent horizontal arms along a wire, bearing short two-bud spurs. It is very common in trellised vineyards.

Cork

The bark of the cork oak (Quercus suber), used to make traditional bottle stoppers. It is elastic and impermeable and allows a very slow exchange of oxygen; Portugal and Spain are major producers.

Corked

A fault that gives wine a smell of mould, wet cardboard or damp cellar, caused mainly by trichloroanisole (TCA). It usually comes from the cork, although it can also originate in the winery.

Corkscrew

A tool for removing the cork from a bottle. The type most used by professionals is the lever or waiter's friend.

Coulure

The shedding of flowers or newly set berries, often due to cold, rain or imbalances during flowering. It reduces the number of berries per bunch and thus the crop.

Cover crop

Natural or sown vegetation kept between vineyard rows. It protects the soil from erosion, encourages biodiversity and can compete with the vine for water to moderate its vigour.

Cream Sherry

A sweet, dark fortified wine from the Jerez area, usually made by blending oloroso with sweet Pedro Ximénez wine.

Criadera

Each of the tiers of butts in the criadera and solera system, holding wines of different ages. The wine moves gradually from the youngest criaderas to the oldest until it reaches the solera.

Crianza

The process of ageing wine in barrel and bottle and, in Spain, also a legal category. A red Crianza must age for at least two years, with a minimum of six months in barrel under the general rule; some appellations, such as Rioja or Ribera del Duero, require a year in barrel.

Cru

A French term for a vineyard or terroir of recognised quality, and by extension the wine that comes from it. Its legal meaning varies by region.

Cru Bourgeois

A classification of Médoc châteaux not included in the 1855 Classification. It is revised periodically and since 2020 has three levels: Cru Bourgeois, Supérieur and Exceptionnel.

Cru Classé

A wine or château included in an official French classification, such as the 1855 Classification in Bordeaux, the Graves classification or that of Saint-Émilion.

Crushing

The gentle breaking of the berries to release the juice without crushing the seeds or stems. It helps fermentation and maceration get under way.

Crémant

A French appellation sparkling wine made by the traditional method outside Champagne, for example in Alsace, Burgundy, the Loire, Jura or Limoux. It also exists in Luxembourg.

Cuvée

A French term for a particular wine or blend made by a producer. In Champagne it also refers to the first and finest juice obtained from pressing.

D

Decanting

Pouring wine from the bottle into a decanter, either to separate it from its sediment or to aerate it. It is mainly used for old reds with sediment and for powerful young wines.

Degrees Brix

A measure of the soluble solids, mainly sugars, in must, expressed in grams per 100 grams. It is used to follow ripening and estimate potential alcohol.

Designation of Origin (DO)

A category protecting wines from a delimited geographical area whose quality and characteristics are due to that origin. In Spain each DO has its own rules and a Regulatory Council that controls varieties, yields and winemaking.

Destemming

Separating the berries from the stems before fermentation. It prevents green tannins and herbaceous flavours from the stems from passing into the wine.

Disgorgement

The traditional-method operation in which the yeast sediment collected in the neck is expelled from the bottle, usually after freezing the neck. The dosage is then added and the bottle is finally corked.

DOC

Denominazione di Origine Controllata in Italy and Denominação de Origem Controlada in Portugal: a category of wines with protected origin and production rules set out in specific regulations.

DOCG

Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita, the highest category of Italian wines, with stricter rules than DOC and compulsory analysis and tasting. It includes wines such as Barolo, Brunello di Montalcino and Chianti Classico.

Domaine

A French term for a wine estate that grows its own vineyards and makes its own wine, widely used in Burgundy and other regions.

Dosage

The addition of the expedition liqueur to a sparkling wine after disgorgement, which determines its final sugar content. Terms such as Brut Nature, Extra Brut, Brut or Demi-Sec depend on it.

Double paste (doble pasta)

A traditional method from eastern Spain in which must ferments with double the usual amount of skins, using those left in a tank from which juice was bled for rosé. It produces very dark, concentrated reds.

Downy mildew

A vine disease caused by Plasmopara viticola, which arrived from America in the 19th century and attacks leaves and bunches in warm, humid conditions. It is traditionally controlled with copper-based products such as Bordeaux mixture.

Dry

A still wine with little or no residual sugar: in the European Union up to 4 grams per litre, or up to 9 if acidity is high enough. For sparkling wines, sec corresponds to a sweetness level of 17 to 32 grams per litre.

Dry farming

Growing vines without irrigation, relying only on rainfall. It is traditional in many European regions, and some appellations restrict irrigation.

E

Eiswein (ice wine)

A sweet German or Austrian wine made from grapes frozen naturally on the vine and pressed while still frozen, so that the water stays behind as ice and the must is highly concentrated. In Germany it is a Prädikatswein category.

En primeur

The advance sale of wines, especially from Bordeaux, while they are still in barrel, some months after the harvest and before bottling. The buyer receives the bottles when the wine is released.

En rama

A fortified wine, especially fino or manzanilla, bottled straight from the butt with minimal or no fining and filtration. It retains greater aromatic intensity and the imprint of the flor.

Erste Lage

The second level of the vineyard classification of the German VDP association, below Grosse Lage. It designates first-class vineyards with limited yields.

Esca

A trunk disease of the vine caused by a complex of fungi that degrade the wood and can cause the sudden death of the vine. There is no fully effective curative treatment.

Estate

An agricultural property with vineyards, often with its own winery. On a label it usually indicates that the wine comes from that property's vines.

Estufagem

An accelerated ageing method for Madeira in which the wine is heated in tanks for at least three months. The finest wines are instead aged by the canteiro system, using the natural warmth of the lodges.

Expedition liqueur

A mixture of wine and, usually, sugar added to sparkling wine after disgorgement to top up the bottle and adjust its final sweetness.

Extra Brut

A designation for a very dry sparkling wine, with a sugar content of between 0 and 6 grams per litre.

Extra Dry

A designation for a sparkling wine with between 12 and 17 grams of sugar per litre; despite its name, it is slightly sweeter than brut. It is a common style for Prosecco.

F

Feinherb

A German term without a precise legal definition indicating a wine with slight sweetness, similar to or a little sweeter than halbtrocken.

Filtration

Passing wine through a filter to remove particles, yeasts or bacteria before bottling. Some producers avoid it or keep it to a minimum.

Fining

A clarification technique in which a substance such as egg white, bentonite, gelatine or plant proteins is added to the wine, carrying suspended particles to the bottom of the vessel.

Finish

The impressions a wine leaves in the mouth after it is swallowed or spat out, in terms of flavour, texture and aroma. A long, pleasant finish is usually associated with quality.

Fino

A pale, dry fortified wine from Jerez or Montilla-Moriles, aged entirely under flor. It is light in colour, with saline, yeasty and almond notes, and is served chilled.

Flor

A layer of yeast that forms on the surface of wine in partly filled butts, in wines of around 15% alcohol. It protects the wine from oxidation and gives it saline and almond notes.

Flowering

The stage of the vine's cycle, in late spring, when the flowers open and pollination takes place. The weather during flowering affects fruit set and the size of the crop.

Food and wine pairing

Matching a wine with a dish so that each complements or enhances the other.

Fortification

Adding grape spirit to wine or must to raise its alcohol level. It is used to make fortified wines such as Sherry and, during fermentation, to stop it and retain sugar, as in Port.

Fortified wine

A wine fortified with grape spirit, such as those of Jerez, Montilla-Moriles, Port or Madeira. In Spain the term refers especially to dry wines with biological or oxidative ageing such as fino, amontillado or oloroso.

Foudre

A large-capacity wooden vat, usually holding several thousand litres. Because less wood surface is in contact with the wine than in a barrel, it imparts little oak flavour.

Free-run juice

The first juice that flows from crushed grapes with no pressure or very gentle pressure. It is the finest and best-balanced.

Free-run wine

Wine obtained from the must or liquid that flows by gravity, without pressing. It is usually finer and less tannic than press wine.

French oak

Wood from European oak (Quercus petraea and Quercus robur) from French forests such as Allier, Tronçais or the Vosges. It gives finer tannins and subtler aromas than American oak.

Freshness

A sense of liveliness on the palate, provided mainly by acidity, that makes a wine lively and appetising.

Frizzante

A lightly sparkling Italian wine with a pressure of between 1 and 2.5 bar. It is equivalent to Spanish vino de aguja or French pétillant.

Frost

A drop in temperature below 0 °C that can damage the vine. Spring frosts after budbreak are the most dangerous, as they destroy the shoots and reduce the crop.

Fruity

A wine whose aromas and flavours mainly recall fresh or ripe fruit. It is a typical trait of young wines and aromatic varieties.

G

Garrafeira

A Portuguese designation for wines from a good vintage with a longer minimum ageing period than Reserva, including time in bottle. In Port it denotes a rare single-vintage style aged for a long time in glass demijohns.

Governo all'uso toscano

A traditional Tuscan technique of adding dried grapes or their must to freshly fermented wine to trigger a slight refermentation. It adds softness, some glycerol and sometimes a light spritz.

Grafting

Joining a Vitis vinifera variety to a rootstock, generally of American origin and resistant to phylloxera. It is standard practice in most of the world's vineyards.

Gran Reserva

The Spanish category with the longest ageing. Reds require at least five years of ageing, with a minimum of 18 months in barrel under the general rule; appellations such as Rioja require two years in barrel.

Gran Selezione

The highest category of Chianti Classico, introduced in 2014, for wines made from the estate's own grapes with a longer minimum ageing than Riserva.

Grand Cru

A top-quality category in several French regions whose meaning varies: in Burgundy and Alsace it designates the best vineyards, in Champagne certain villages, and in Saint-Émilion it is part of a classification of châteaux.

Granite

An igneous rock that weathers into sandy, acidic, well-drained soils. It is characteristic of areas such as Rías Baixas, the northern Rhône and Beaujolais.

Grape seed

The seed of the grape, rich in tannins and oils. If crushed or over-extracted it can add bitterness and astringency.

Grape skin

The skin of the grape, which contains most of the pigments, tannins and aroma compounds. Its contact with the must during maceration determines the wine's colour and structure.

Grape variety

A type of vine with its own stable characteristics, such as Tempranillo, Chardonnay or Nebbiolo. Most wine grape varieties belong to the species Vitis vinifera.

Gravel

A soil of small stones and pebbles deposited by ancient rivers, very free-draining and heat-retaining. It is famous in the Médoc and in the Graves region of Bordeaux.

Green harvest

The removal of some bunches before veraison, also called green harvesting. It reduces yield so that ripening is concentrated in the remaining bunches.

Grosse Lage

The highest category of the German VDP association's vineyard classification, equivalent to a grand cru. Dry wines from these vineyards are called Grosses Gewächs.

Grosses Gewächs

A top-quality dry wine from a vineyard classified as Grosse Lage by the German VDP association. It is often identified by the initials GG.

Guyot

A cane-pruning system, named after the French agronomist Jules Guyot, in which one cane (single) or two (double) are kept along with one or two renewal spurs. It is very common in France and in trellised vineyards.

H

Halbtrocken

A German term equivalent to medium-dry: a still wine with up to 12 grams of sugar per litre, or up to 18 if the acidity is high enough.

Harvest

Picking grapes to make wine, which in the northern hemisphere usually takes place between August and October. By extension, it also refers to a year's crop.

Herbaceous

A descriptor for aromas reminiscent of cut grass, green pepper or leaves, often due to pyrazines. It can be typical of some varieties or a sign of underripe grapes.

Hybrid

A variety obtained by crossing Vitis vinifera with other vine species, usually American or Asian, to achieve resistance to disease or cold. Its use is restricted in many European appellations.

I

IGT

Indicazione Geografica Tipica, an Italian category of wines with a geographical indication and more flexible rules than DOC. Many innovative wines, such as some Super Tuscans, have been sold under this category.

K

Kabinett

The first level of German Prädikatswein, made from ripe grapes picked at normal harvest. It usually gives light, low-alcohol wines with lively acidity, either dry or with some sweetness.

L

Lagar

An open, shallow vessel, traditionally made of stone, in which grapes are trodden. It is still used for some quality Ports and, in Spain, the word also refers to the place where grapes are pressed.

Landwein

A German category of wines with protected geographical indication, equivalent to country wines. It sits above wine without geographical indication and below Qualitätswein.

Late harvest

Picking grapes later than usual so that they accumulate more sugar through overripening or shrivelling on the vine. It usually produces sweet wines; in Alsace it is a regulated designation (vendanges tardives).

LBV (Late Bottled Vintage)

A single-vintage Port bottled between four and six years after the harvest, following a longer period in wood than vintage Port. It is usually ready to drink on release.

Leaf removal

Removing leaves around the bunches to improve their airflow and sun exposure. It reduces the risk of fungal disease, although too much can cause sunburn on the grapes.

Lees

The sediment of dead yeast and other particles that settles at the bottom of the vessel after fermentation. Fine lees are used in ageing to add texture and complexity.

Legs (tears)

The drops that run down the inside of the glass after swirling, caused mainly by the evaporation of alcohol. They indicate alcohol and sugar content, not quality.

Length

How long a wine's aromatic and flavour sensations last after swallowing, sometimes measured in seconds (caudalies). Long length is associated with higher quality.

Lieu-dit

In France, a locality or plot with a traditional name, which does not necessarily imply an official classification. It may appear on the label alongside the appellation.

Lignification

The process by which the vine's green shoots turn woody and become canes, storing reserves for the winter. It usually takes place in late summer.

Limestone

A sedimentary rock rich in calcium carbonate that produces well-drained soils with good water reserves. It is associated with wines of marked acidity and finesse, as in Burgundy, Champagne or Jerez.

Liqueur de tirage

A mixture of wine, sugar and yeast added to the base wine at bottling to trigger the second fermentation in bottle in the traditional method.

Llicorella

A soil of slate broken into thin layers, dark in colour with ochre or golden glints, characteristic of Priorat. It is poor and free-draining, forcing the roots to grow deep.

M

Maceration

Contact between must or wine and the skins, seeds and sometimes stems to extract colour, tannins and aromas. It is fundamental in the making of red and orange wines.

Machine harvesting

Picking grapes with harvesting machines that shake the vines to detach the berries. It is fast and economical but requires trellised vineyards and does not allow bunch selection.

Maderisation

Oxidation of wine, often favoured by heat, which gives it an amber colour and notes of walnut, caramel or dried fruit. It is a fault in young wines but a sought-after trait in wines such as Madeira.

Magnum

A 1.5-litre bottle, equivalent to two standard bottles. It is considered to favour slower development of the wine.

Malic acid

An acid found in grapes, with a sharp, green-apple-like taste, which decreases as the grapes ripen. During malolactic fermentation it is converted into the softer lactic acid.

Malolactic fermentation

The conversion of malic acid into lactic acid by lactic acid bacteria, which softens the wine's acidity. It is almost systematic in reds and optional in whites, where it can add buttery notes.

Manzanilla

A dry fortified wine aged under flor exclusively in Sanlúcar de Barrameda, with its own designation of origin. The maritime influence encourages a thicker flor and gives a particularly delicate, saline wine.

Massal selection

A method of vine propagation that takes material from many vines in a vineyard, chosen for their qualities, rather than from a single clone. It preserves the genetic diversity of the variety.

Medium-dry

A wine with a slight but perceptible sweetness, between dry and medium-sweet. In Spain it is a traditional term for still wines with some residual sugar.

Medium-dry / demi-sec

For still wines, a wine with up to 12 grams of sugar per litre, or up to 18 if acidity is high. For sparkling wines, demi-sec corresponds to 32 to 50 grams per litre.

Micro-oxygenation

A technique that introduces small, controlled doses of oxygen into wine in tank to stabilise colour and soften tannins, partly imitating the effect of a barrel.

Microclimate

The particular climatic conditions of a very small area, such as a plot or the immediate surroundings of a vine, which may differ from the region's general climate.

Minerality

A tasting descriptor of debated definition, referring to sensations of wet stone, chalk, flint or salinity. Its direct link to soil minerals has not been scientifically demonstrated.

Mistelle

A drink made by adding grape spirit to unfermented must, which thus retains all the grape's sugar. It is produced in various parts of Spain and France, often from Muscat.

Monopole

A French term, common in Burgundy, indicating that a named vineyard is entirely owned by a single proprietor.

Must

Grape juice obtained by crushing or pressing, with or without skins, before or during fermentation.

Must settling

The removal of solid particles from the must, especially for white and rosé wines, before fermentation. It is usually done by letting the must settle in the cold and then racking it.

N

Native yeasts

Yeasts naturally present in the vineyard and winery, as opposed to commercial cultured yeasts. They are responsible for spontaneous fermentation.

Natural wine

Wine made with minimal intervention, usually from organic grapes, with native yeasts and few or no additives or added sulphites. It lacks a legal definition in most countries, although in France the designation vin méthode nature has existed since 2020.

Noble rot

A beneficial form of Botrytis cinerea that dehydrates ripe berries and concentrates their sugars and acids. It is the basis of sweet wines such as Sauternes, Tokaji Aszú and Trockenbeerenauslese.

Nose

The aromas perceived when smelling a wine, and the olfactory stage of tasting in which their intensity, quality and complexity are assessed.

Négociant

A French company that buys grapes, must or wine from growers to make, age and sell it under its own label. It has played a historic role in Burgundy, Bordeaux and Champagne.

O

Oechsle

A scale of must density used in Germany, Austria and Switzerland to measure sugar content. It underpins the minimum ripeness levels for Prädikatswein.

Oenologist / winemaker

A professional trained in oenology who is responsible for directing the making and ageing of wines at a winery.

Oenology

The science and technique of making, ageing, preserving and analysing wine.

Old vines

Vines of advanced age, which usually produce fewer grapes but with greater concentration. The term has no common legal definition of minimum age.

Oloroso

A fortified wine from Jerez or Montilla-Moriles aged exclusively oxidatively, without flor. In its traditional form it is dry, mahogany-coloured, with notes of walnut, spice and fine wood.

Orange wine

Wine made from white grapes fermented with their skins, like a red, giving it an amber or orange colour and some tannic structure. It has a long tradition in Georgia, where it is made in clay amphorae called qvevri.

Order of service

The recommended sequence for serving several wines at a meal or tasting: generally from sparkling to white, rosé and red, from light to powerful, from young to old and from dry to sweet.

Organic wine

Wine made from certified organically grown grapes, without synthetic herbicides or pesticides, and in accordance with EU organic winemaking rules, which limit additives and sulphites.

Oxidation

The reaction of wine with oxygen, which shifts its colour towards amber or brick tones and its aromas towards bruised apple or nutty notes. It can be a fault or a sought-after feature in certain styles.

Oxidative ageing

Ageing in which the wine is in contact with oxygen, with no protective flor. It yields amber or mahogany wines with notes of nuts, toast and spice, such as oloroso or tawny Port.

P

Palate

The stage of tasting in which a wine's flavours, texture, body, acidity and tannins are assessed. It also refers to the sum of those impressions.

Palo cortado

An uncommon fortified wine from Jerez that combines the aromatic delicacy of an amontillado with the body of an oloroso. It usually begins ageing under flor and continues oxidatively.

Passito

An Italian wine made from dried grapes, usually sweet, such as Passito di Pantelleria or Recioto di Soave.

PDO

Protected Designation of Origin, the European Union category covering wines with a protected geographical origin and a close link to their area. It includes, for example, Spanish DOs, DOCas and Vinos de Pago and Italian DOCs and DOCGs.

Pebbles (galets roulés)

Stones rounded by the erosion of ancient rivers that cover some vineyards. They store heat during the day and release it at night, helping ripening, as in Châteauneuf-du-Pape.

Pergola

A training system in which the vine's foliage spreads over a raised horizontal framework supported by posts. It is traditional in Rías Baixas, northern Portugal and several regions of Italy.

Perlage (bead)

The stream of bubbles that forms and rises in a glass of sparkling wine. Their fineness, abundance and persistence are assessed.

PGI

Protected Geographical Indication, a European category for wines with a less strict link to their area than PDO. In Spain it corresponds to Vino de la Tierra and in France to the former vins de pays.

pH

A measure of a wine's actual acidity, usually between 3 and 4. A low pH favours microbiological stability, colour and freshness.

Phenolic ripeness

The degree of ripeness of the skins and seeds, especially their tannins and anthocyanins, which does not always coincide with sugar ripeness. Grapes lacking phenolic ripeness give green, bitter tannins.

Phylloxera

An aphid-like insect native to North America that attacks the roots of European vines. It devastated Europe's vineyards in the second half of the 19th century, and the solution was to graft European varieties onto resistant American rootstocks.

Polyphenols

A family of compounds found mainly in skins and seeds, including tannins and anthocyanins. They determine a wine's colour, structure and much of its ageing potential.

Powdery mildew

A vine disease caused by the fungus Erysiphe necator, which arrived from America in the 19th century; it covers leaves and bunches with a whitish powder and can split the berries. It is traditionally controlled with sulphur.

Premier Cru

A French quality category whose meaning depends on the region: in Burgundy and Champagne it designates vineyards or villages just below Grand Cru, whereas in the 1855 Classification it is the highest level.

Press wine

Wine obtained by pressing the skins after running off. It is more tannic and deeply coloured and may be partly blended with the free-run wine.

Pressing

Extracting liquid from grapes or fermented skins by applying pressure. For whites it is done before fermentation and for reds after running off.

Primary aromas

Aromas that come from the grape itself and its variety, such as fruity, floral or herbaceous notes. They predominate in young wines.

Prise de mousse

The second fermentation of a base wine, in bottle or in a sealed tank, which creates the carbon dioxide in sparkling wines.

Pruning

Cutting the vine's canes and arms, mainly in winter, to shape it, regulate its yield and balance its vigour. Summer pruning complements this work during the growing season.

Prädikatswein

The top category of German quality wines, classified by grape ripeness at harvest into Kabinett, Spätlese, Auslese, Beerenauslese, Trockenbeerenauslese and Eiswein. Chaptalisation is not permitted.

Pulp

The fleshy part of the berry, containing most of its water, sugars and acids. In almost all varieties it is colourless.

Pumping over

Drawing must from the bottom of the tank and spraying it over the cap of skins during red-wine fermentation. It aids extraction, evens out temperature and aerates the must.

Punching down

Pushing the cap of skins down into the must during the fermentation of red wines. It aids the extraction of colour and tannins and prevents the cap from drying out.

Pétillant naturel (pét-nat)

A sparkling or semi-sparkling wine made by the ancestral method, bottled during the first fermentation. It usually has gentle bubbles and is often cloudy from the lees, without disgorgement.

Q

Qualified Designation of Origin (DOCa)

The highest category of Spanish designations of origin, requiring additional quality and control standards. It is held by Rioja and Priorat (DOQ in Catalan).

Qualitätswein

The German category of quality wine with designation of origin, coming from one of the country's 13 wine regions (Anbaugebiete). It ranks below Prädikatswein and allows chaptalisation.

Quinta

A Portuguese term for a farm estate, in particular a wine property, very common in the Douro Valley.

R

Rack and return (délestage)

A red winemaking technique in which the fermenting must is drained from the tank, leaving the cap of skins behind, and then poured back over it. It promotes gentle extraction and aeration.

Racking

Moving wine from one vessel to another to separate it from the sediment that has settled at the bottom. It also provides slight aeration.

Raisining

The dehydration of grapes, on the vine or after harvest, which concentrates sugars, acids and aromas. It is used to make sweet wines or concentrated reds.

Rancio

The aromatic character of wines aged for a long time under oxidative conditions, with notes of nuts, wax, cocoa or old wood. It gives its name to the traditional rancio wines of Catalonia and Roussillon.

Recioto

A sweet wine from the Veneto made from dried grapes, such as the red Recioto della Valpolicella or the white Recioto di Soave.

Red wine

Wine made from black grapes fermented together with their skins, from which it extracts colour, tannins and much of its aroma.

Reduction

The state of a wine kept without contact with oxygen, which can produce sulphurous smells such as rotten egg, struck match or rubber. They often diminish with aeration.

Refreshing (rocío)

In the criadera and solera system, refilling each tier with wine from the next-youngest tier after part of it has been drawn off.

Regulatory Council

The body that manages and controls a designation of origin in Spain. It ensures compliance with the rules, certifies the wines and promotes the appellation.

Reserva

A Spanish ageing category: reds must age for at least three years, with a minimum of twelve months in barrel. In Portugal, Reserva denotes wines of notable quality with their own requirements.

Residual sugar

The sugar left unfermented in a wine, usually expressed in grams per litre. It determines whether a wine is dry, medium-dry, medium-sweet or sweet.

Retronasal perception

Perceiving a wine's aromas from the mouth, through the connection between the back of the mouth and the nose. It accounts for much of what we call flavour.

Riddling

The traditional-method operation in which bottles are gradually turned and tilted until upside down, to bring the sediment into the neck before disgorgement. It is done by hand on riddling racks or with mechanical cages called gyropalettes.

Riddling rack (pupitre)

A wooden A-frame with angled holes in which sparkling-wine bottles are placed for manual riddling, so that the sediment moves towards the neck.

Rim

The edge of the wine in a tilted glass, where the colour is less intense. Its hues help estimate development: purple in young reds and brick or orange in old wines.

Ripasso

A Valpolicella technique in which the wine is refermented on the pomace of the dried grapes used for Amarone or Recioto. It gives a fuller, more concentrated red, sold as Valpolicella Ripasso.

Riserva

An Italian designation for wines with a longer minimum ageing period than the basic version of their appellation, set by each set of rules.

Roble

In Spain, a term used for young reds with a short spell in barrel, shorter than that required for Crianza. It is not regulated by general legislation, although some appellations define it.

Rootstock

A plant, usually from American vine species or hybrids, that provides the roots onto which the cultivated variety is grafted. It is chosen for its resistance to phylloxera and its suitability to soil and climate.

Rosé

A wine made from black grapes, or a mix of black and white where the rules allow, with a short maceration that gives it a pink colour. It is obtained by direct pressing or by bleeding.

Ruby Port

A young, fruity style of Port, ruby in colour, aged only briefly in large vessels to preserve its fruit. Higher-quality versions are labelled Reserve.

Running off

Separating the liquid wine from the skins and seeds at the end of red-wine maceration. The wine that runs off by gravity is called free-run wine; the remaining pomace is pressed.

Récoltant-manipulant (grower Champagne)

In Champagne, a grower who makes and sells their own Champagne from grapes from their own vineyards. It is identified on the label by the initials RM.

S

Saca (withdrawal)

Drawing off part of the wine from the solera, the oldest tier, for bottling. The volume removed is replaced with wine from the first criadera.

Saignée

Drawing off part of the juice from a tank of black grapes shortly after vatting, either to make rosé from that juice or to concentrate the red left in the tank.

Screw cap

A threaded metal closure that avoids the risk of TCA taint and allows precise oxygen control. It is very widespread in Australia and New Zealand.

Second wine

A wine made by a château, especially in Bordeaux, from grapes or lots not selected for its main wine, often from younger vines.

Secondary aromas

Aromas produced during alcoholic and malolactic fermentation, such as notes of yeast, bread, butter or dairy.

Sekt

A German or Austrian sparkling wine, made by the traditional method or in tank. Sekt b.A. comes from grapes from a specific German wine region.

Semi-sparkling wine

A wine with a small amount of carbon dioxide, at a pressure of between 1 and 2.5 bar, which gives a slight tingle on the palate.

Serving temperature

The appropriate temperature for serving each wine. As a general guide, sparkling and light whites at 6–10 °C, fuller whites and rosés at 10–13 °C and reds at 14–18 °C.

Single-varietal

A wine made from a single grape variety, or with a minimum percentage of it set by regulation, such as 85% in the European Union to name it on the label.

Skin contact

Brief contact between white grape must and its skins before fermentation, usually in the cold, to extract varietal aromas.

Slate

A metamorphic rock that splits into layers and produces poor, free-draining, heat-retaining soils. It is characteristic of the Mosel, the Portuguese Douro, Bierzo and Priorat.

Sobretabla

In the Jerez area, young wine that has already been fortified and is awaiting classification before entering the criadera and solera system.

Solera

A dynamic ageing system typical of Jerez in which wines of different ages are gradually blended across tiers of butts. It also refers to the oldest tier, traditionally on the floor, from which wine is drawn for bottling.

Sommelier

The professional in charge of wine in a restaurant or other establishment: they select the list, manage the cellar, advise guests and serve the wines.

Sparkling wine

A wine containing dissolved carbon dioxide from a second fermentation, with a bottle pressure of at least 3 bar. It is made by the traditional method, the Charmat method or others.

Spontaneous fermentation

Fermentation started by the yeasts naturally present on the grapes and in the winery, without adding cultured yeasts. It can add complexity but is less predictable.

Spumante

The Italian term for sparkling wine, made by the classic method or the Charmat (Martinotti) method.

Spätlese

A category of German Prädikatswein made from late-harvested grapes, riper than those for Kabinett. It can be dry or sweet.

Stacking (en rima)

The arrangement of bottles horizontally, stacked in rows, during the second fermentation and ageing of traditional-method sparkling wines such as Cava.

Stainless steel

The usual material for fermentation and storage tanks, as it is inert, easy to clean and allows precise temperature control. It is associated with wines that aim to preserve fruit and freshness.

Stem

The woody structure of the bunch that holds the berries. If fermented with the must it adds tannin and freshness, but possibly also green notes.

Structure

The framework of a wine formed by its acidity, tannins, alcohol and extract, which supports the aromas and flavours. A wine with good structure usually has greater ageing potential.

Sulphites

Compounds derived from sulphur dioxide (SO₂), used in winemaking as antioxidants and antiseptics; yeasts also produce small amounts. In the European Union their presence must be stated on the label from 10 mg/l.

Sun-drying (asoleo)

Exposing grapes to the sun after harvest, traditionally on mats, to raisin them and concentrate their sugar. It is typical of the production of sweet Pedro Ximénez and Muscat wines in Andalusia.

Super Tuscan

An informal name for the quality Tuscan reds that, from the 1970s onwards, were made outside the rules of the traditional appellations, often with French varieties such as Cabernet Sauvignon. Sassicaia and Tignanello are pioneering examples.

Superiore

An Italian designation for wines of an appellation that meet stricter requirements than the basic version, such as a higher minimum alcohol level or lower yields.

Sur lie

A French expression indicating that the wine has been aged on its lees until bottling, without racking. It is a recognised designation in Muscadet, where it adds texture and a slight prickle.

Sweet wine

A wine with a high residual sugar content, in the European Union more than 45 grams per litre. It is obtained by overripening, raisining, noble rot, freezing the grapes or stopping fermentation.

Sélection de grains nobles (SGN)

A designation used in Alsace and other French areas for sweet wines made from selected grapes affected by noble rot. It is more concentrated and demanding than vendanges tardives.

T

Table wine

A former European category of wines without geographical indication, replaced since 2009 by the generic designation wine. Colloquially it refers to ordinary wine for everyday drinking.

Tannins

Polyphenols from skins, seeds, stems and wood that give structure and astringency, especially to reds. Over time they soften and contribute to the wine's longevity.

Tartaric acid

The main acid in grapes and wine, fairly stable during ripening. It can precipitate as harmless potassium bitartrate crystals in the bottle or on the cork.

Tartrate stabilisation

A treatment to prevent tartrate crystals from precipitating in the bottle, usually by chilling the wine for a few days before bottling. Crystals that appear without this treatment are harmless.

Tasting

The sensory analysis of wine through sight, smell and taste in order to describe and assess it. It normally follows visual, olfactory and palate stages.

Tawny Port

A style of Port aged oxidatively in casks, which acquires a tawny colour and notes of nuts and caramel. Age-indicated categories (10, 20, 30 and 40 years) express the average character of the blend.

Terroir

The combination of natural factors, such as soil, climate, topography and aspect, and human ones, such as growing and winemaking traditions, that give a wine a distinctive character linked to its place of origin.

Tertiary aromas

Aromas that develop during oak ageing and bottle ageing, such as spice, leather, tobacco, nuts or forest floor. Together they are also known as bouquet.

Tinaja (clay jar)

A large fired-clay jar traditionally used in Spain to ferment and store wine, especially in La Mancha, Montilla-Moriles and Valdepeñas. It is being revived today in low-intervention winemaking.

Toasting

Heating the inside of the barrel over a fire during its manufacture, at light, medium or heavy levels. It influences the aromas the wood gives the wine, such as vanilla, toast, coffee or spice.

Traditional method

A sparkling-wine method with a second fermentation in the same bottle in which the wine is sold, followed by ageing on lees, riddling and disgorgement. It is the method used for Champagne, Cava, Crémant and Franciacorta.

Trellis (vertical shoot positioning)

A training system in which the vine is supported by posts and wires forming vertical rows. It makes mechanisation and canopy management easier.

Trocken

A German term equivalent to dry: a still wine with up to 4 grams of sugar per litre, or up to 9 if acidity is high enough.

Trockenbeerenauslese

The highest ripeness category of German Prädikatswein, made from berries shrivelled by noble rot and selected one by one. It gives very sweet, concentrated and scarce wines.

U

Unctuous

A wine with an oily, dense and mouth-coating texture, due to glycerol, alcohol, sugar or lees ageing.

Ungrafted vine

A vine grown on its own roots, without a rootstock. It survives phylloxera only in soils where the pest does not thrive, such as sandy ones, or in isolated areas it never reached.

V

Vatting

Placing crushed or destemmed grapes, or must, into the vessel where it will ferment. In red winemaking it marks the start of maceration.

VDP

Verband Deutscher Prädikatsweingüter, an association of German quality wine estates that applies its own vineyard classification: Gutswein, Ortswein, Erste Lage and Grosse Lage. Members are recognisable by the eagle on the capsule.

Veraison

The stage of the vine's cycle in which the berries change colour, soften and begin to accumulate sugars. It marks the start of ripening.

Vin de France

The French category for wines without geographical indication, which replaced the former vin de table in 2009. It allows the variety and vintage to be stated and grapes from different regions to be blended.

Vin doux naturel

A sweet wine whose fermentation is stopped by adding grape spirit, so that it retains part of the grape's natural sugar. Examples include the vins doux naturels of Roussillon, such as Banyuls or Rivesaltes, and Mediterranean Muscat wines.

Vin jaune

A white wine from the Jura made from Savagnin and aged for at least six years in barrels that are not topped up, under a film of yeast. It is bottled in the 62 cl clavelin and shows notes of walnut and curry.

Vin Santo

A traditional wine from Tuscany and other parts of Italy made from dried grapes, mainly Trebbiano and Malvasia, and aged for a long time in small barrels called caratelli. It can be dry or sweet.

Vine

A grapevine plant, especially its trunk. By extension, the Spanish word is also used as a synonym for grape variety.

Vine spacing

The distance between rows and between vines in a vineyard, which determines the number of plants per hectare. It affects vigour, competition between vines and mechanisation.

Vinho Regional

A Portuguese category of wines with protected geographical indication and more flexible rules than DOC, such as Alentejano or Lisboa.

Vino de Calidad con Indicación Geográfica

A Spanish category of protected-designation-of-origin wines ranking below DO, intended as a preliminary step for areas aspiring to become a DO.

Vino de la Tierra

The Spanish category of wines with protected geographical indication (PGI), with less strict rules than designations of origin, such as Vino de la Tierra de Castilla.

Vino de Pago

A Spanish protected-designation-of-origin category for wines from a specific site or estate with distinctive soil and microclimatic features. It was created by the 2003 Wine and Vineyard Law.

Vintage

The year in which the grapes used to make a wine were harvested. Each year's weather conditions influence the style and quality of the wine.

Vintage Port

A Port from a single exceptional vintage, declared by each producer, bottled after about two years in wood and developing for decades in bottle. It usually throws a sediment and should be decanted.

Viticulture

The science and practice of growing vines to produce grapes, especially those intended for winemaking.

Viñedo Singular

A designation used by some Spanish appellations, such as Rioja since 2017, for wines from a specific delimited plot, with limited yields and specific quality requirements.

Volatile acidity

The volatile acids in wine, mainly acetic acid, produced by bacteria or yeasts. In small amounts it can add complexity, but in excess it smells of vinegar or nail varnish and is considered a fault.

VOS / VORS

Designations for Sherry with certified age: VOS (Vinum Optimum Signatum) indicates an average age of over 20 years and VORS (Vinum Optimum Rare Signatum) of over 30 years.

W

White wine

Wine usually made from white grapes, or from black grapes with colourless pulp, by fermenting the juice without the skins.

Wine glass

A stemmed vessel for serving wine, whose shape influences the perception of aromas and flavours. It is held by the stem so as not to warm the wine.

Wine tourism

Tourism focused on the world of wine: visits to wineries and vineyards, tastings, museums and routes through wine regions.

Winemaking (vinification)

The set of operations that turn grapes into wine, from receiving the harvest to the end of fermentation and, in a broad sense, up to bottling.

Winery / cellar

The establishment where wine is made, aged and bottled, and also the company that produces it. It also refers to the place where bottles are stored.

Y

Yeast

A single-celled microscopic fungus responsible for alcoholic fermentation. The most important species in winemaking is Saccharomyces cerevisiae.

Yield

The quantity of grapes or wine produced per unit of area, expressed in kilograms or hectolitres per hectare. Appellations set maximum yields.

Young wine

A wine with no oak ageing or only a very short period, intended to be drunk within its first years to enjoy its fruit and freshness.
