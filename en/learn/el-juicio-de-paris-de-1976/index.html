History & culture

The Judgment of Paris, 1976

The 1976 blind tasting in Paris in which California wines came out ahead of great French wines, and its influence on how the world viewed New World wine.

On 24 May 1976 a blind tasting was held at a hotel in Paris which, over time, would become one of the most frequently cited episodes in the modern history of wine. A group of renowned French professionals compared, without knowing what was in their glasses, white and red wines from California with some of the most prestigious from Burgundy and Bordeaux. The winners of both flights turned out to be Californian. The press dubbed it the Judgment of Paris, after the myth in which Paris had to choose between three goddesses.

The organiser and his aim

The tasting was the idea of Steven Spurrier, an English wine merchant based in Paris, where he ran a shop and a wine school. Together with his American associate Patricia Gallagher, he wanted to use the atmosphere of the bicentenary of American independence to introduce France to the new wines of California, which at the time hardly anyone in Europe took seriously.

It was not, at first, meant as a challenge. The aim was to show the quality some Californian wineries were achieving, and few expected them to rival the great French names.

How it was organised

The format was simple. Two flights were prepared:

  • Whites: several California Chardonnays against white Burgundies, also made from Chardonnay, from the Côte de Beaune.
  • Reds: several California Cabernet Sauvignons against red Bordeaux, including classified châteaux from the Médoc and Graves.

The wines were served without the tasters knowing their identity. The panel consisted mainly of highly respected figures in French wine: sommeliers, estate owners, critics, restaurateurs and heads of wine institutions. Each judge assessed the wines, and a ranking was drawn up from the combined assessments.

The outcome

In the white flight, the top-rated wine was the 1973 Chateau Montelena from the Napa Valley, made by a team that included the Croatian-born winemaker Miljenko Mike Grgich. In the red flight, first place went to the Stag's Leap Wine Cellars Cabernet Sauvignon, also from 1973 and also from Napa, from the winery founded by Warren Winiarski.

Several French wines finished in strong positions, and the differences between many of the wines were small. But the fact that a Californian white and a Californian red headed the list before a French panel came as a surprise to many, including some of the tasters themselves.

The role of the press

The tasting would probably have gone unnoticed had it not been for George M. Taber, the Paris correspondent of the American magazine Time. Taber was, as has often been told, the only journalist present. His short article, published soon afterwards, spread the news in the United States and turned it into an event. Years later, Taber devoted an entire book to the story of that day.

In France the reaction was more muted and, in some cases, irritated. Some questioned the tasting's methodology, the choice of wines or the way the assessments were combined.

Criticisms and caveats

The Judgment of Paris has been analysed many times. Among the most common objections are:

  • The wines were young, and great red Bordeaux usually needs more time in bottle to show its full potential.
  • A single tasting, with a small number of tasters and wines, cannot support general conclusions about two entire regions.
  • Ranking by summing individual assessments can produce results that are sensitive to small differences.

These points are reasonable. Yet the historical value of the episode lies less in the exact result than in what it showed: that wines able to stand comparison with the best could be made outside Europe, and that an expert panel, with no labels in sight, could not always tell them apart.

Impact on the wine world

For California, and for the Napa Valley in particular, the Judgment of Paris was a boost to confidence and image. It drew international attention, investment and new vocations. Some observers see it as a symbolic starting point for the rise of New World wine in the following decades, as regions in Australia, Chile, Argentina, South Africa and New Zealand developed.

It also had an effect in France, where, along with other factors, it fed into reflection on international competition. And it strengthened the standing of blind tasting as a tool for judging wine without prejudice.

Remembering the episode

Over the years, commemorative tastings were held, including one for the thirtieth anniversary in 2006, staged simultaneously on both sides of the Atlantic. The story also inspired the 2008 film Bottle Shock, which recreates it with considerable licence. The Judgment of Paris is now part of the narrative of modern wine as an example of how a modest event can change perceptions for a long time.
