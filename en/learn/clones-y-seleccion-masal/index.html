Clones and massal selection: how vines are propagated

What a grapevine clone is, how it differs from massal selection, and why the choice of plant material affects vineyard health, diversity and wine style.

When a grower plants a new vineyard, no seeds are sown. Vines are almost always propagated vegetatively, from cuttings that are grafted or rooted, so each new plant is a genetic copy of the vine it came from. This practice, known since antiquity, allows the characteristics of a variety to be preserved over centuries. The key question is: from which vines is that material taken? From the answer come two approaches, clonal selection and massal selection.

A variety is not uniform

Although all the vines of a given variety descend from a single original plant, small mutations have built up over centuries of propagation. That is why, within a variety such as Pinot Noir or Tempranillo, there are plants that differ in the size and compactness of their bunches, the thickness of their skins, their productivity, their ripening date or their aromatic profile. Some of these mutations are so marked that they give rise to variants with their own names, as with the white, grey or black forms of certain grapes.

To this genetic diversity is added a health dimension: many old vines carry viruses that reduce their vigour, delay ripening or lower the crop.

Massal selection

Massal selection is the traditional method. The grower walks the vineyard, often over several years, marks the vines considered best —healthy, balanced, with good-quality bunches— and takes cuttings from them for new plantings. The result is a population of plants that preserves the diversity of the original vineyard, because it comes from many different vines.

Its supporters point out that this internal variety adds complexity to the wine and a better capacity to adapt to changing conditions, since not every plant reacts in the same way to drought, heat or disease. It also makes it possible to preserve the character of an old vineyard and pass it on to new plantings. Its main drawback concerns health: without specific checks, viruses can be spread along with the plant material.

Clonal selection

In viticulture, a clone is the offspring of a single vine chosen for its qualities. Clonal selection, developed mainly from the mid-twentieth century onwards, follows a long and rigorous process: interesting vines are identified, their health status is checked through testing, their agronomic behaviour and the quality of their wines are observed for years, and finally the best are registered and multiplied in nurseries under official control.

Clones are identified by numbers or codes. In Burgundy, for example, many clones of Pinot Noir and Chardonnay were selected that are now grown in numerous countries and are often known as Dijon clones. Each is described by its productivity, bunch size, earliness or the style of wine it tends to produce.

The advantages of clonal selection are clear:

  • Health: certified material is free of the main viruses, which means more vigorous, longer-lived plants. In some cases heat therapy or tissue culture is used to clean up a valuable plant.
  • Predictability: the grower knows in advance how the plant will behave.
  • Choice according to goals: clones can be chosen that are more or less productive, earlier or later ripening, or suited to a particular style.

The risks of uniformity

The widespread use of a few clones also had negative consequences. In the early decades of clonal selection the most productive clones were often favoured, which in some cases led to more dilute wines. Moreover, planting an entire vineyard with a single clone reduces genetic diversity: every vine reacts in the same way to weather or a pest, and the wine may turn out more uniform.

Today many producers choose to plant mixtures of several clones to recover some of that diversity, while others are returning to massal selection from their best old vines, first having the chosen vines tested for disease. Some nurseries also offer controlled massal selections, a middle way between the two approaches.

Conservatories and diversity

To prevent the loss of diversity, various institutions have created conservatories or collections in which hundreds of different types of the same variety, gathered from old vineyards, are maintained. These collections make it possible to study the internal variability of grapes, to select new clones with characteristics suited to today's challenges, and to preserve a genetic heritage that might otherwise disappear as old vines are uprooted.

Climate change has given this work new urgency. In regions where ripening has moved earlier, growers are looking within traditional varieties for later-ripening types, looser bunches or greater drought resistance, without having to change grape.

A choice with lasting consequences

The decision between clones and massal selection is not a matter of good or bad, but of objectives. Clonal selection offers health and predictability; massal selection offers diversity and continuity with the past. Many growers combine both depending on the plot. Because a vineyard can live for many decades, the choice of plant material is one of the most lasting decisions made in the vineyard, and its imprint will be felt in the wines for generations.
