Food pairing

Cooking with wine

Wine is a classic kitchen ingredient: it brings acidity, aroma and depth to stews, sauces, marinades and desserts. This article explains what happens when it is cooked, how to choose it and how much truth there is in the idea that the alcohol evaporates completely.

Wine does not merely accompany food: it is also part of it. For centuries, the cuisines of wine-producing countries have used it as an ingredient, and many emblematic dishes would be unthinkable without it. Boeuf bourguignon, coq au vin, risotto, kidneys in sherry, pears poached in wine and Italian zabaione are just a few examples. Understanding what wine contributes to cooking and how it is transformed helps to use it more skilfully.

What wine brings to a dish

Wine contains water, alcohol, acids, sugars in varying amounts, aromatic compounds and, in reds, tannins and pigments. Each of these components plays a role in the kitchen:

  • Acidity brightens flavours, balances fat and, in marinades, helps to flavour the surface of meat.
  • Alcohol dissolves aromatic compounds that are soluble in neither water nor fat, which helps to release the aromas of herbs, spices and other ingredients.
  • The wine's own aromas, although transformed by heat, add depth and complexity to sauces and stews.
  • In reds, colour and tannins give body and hue to sauces.
  • In sweet and fortified wines, sugar allows glossy, caramelised reductions.

The main techniques

The simplest use is deglazing: after browning meat or fish in a pan, a little wine is poured in to dissolve the caramelised juices stuck to the bottom, which become the base of a sauce. In marinades, wine, alone or with oil, herbs and spices, flavours meat before cooking, as in many game recipes. In stews and braises, wine forms part of the cooking liquid for hours, and its acidity and aroma merge with the meat and vegetables. Reductions concentrate wine over a high heat until a thick sauce is obtained. Finally, wine is used for poaching fish or fruit, as in the classic pears cooked in red wine with cinnamon.

How to choose a wine for cooking

An often-repeated piece of advice is never to cook with a wine one would not drink. This does not mean using expensive wines, but avoiding those that are faulty, oxidised or vinegary, because their defects will pass into the dish. Products sold as “cooking wine” deserve caution, as they often contain added salt and can upset the seasoning. When choosing, it is worth thinking about what happens when wine is concentrated:

  • Very tannic reds can become bitter and astringent when reduced; for reduced sauces, reds with soft tannins and generous fruit are preferable.
  • Heavily oaked wines can contribute bitter or resinous notes; a young, unoaked white usually gives cleaner sauces.
  • Acidity becomes concentrated; a very acidic wine should be balanced with fat, such as butter or cream, or with sweet ingredients.
  • Sweetness also becomes concentrated; sweet wines should be used sparingly in savoury dishes.

A good practical rule is to cook with a wine similar in style to the one that will be served at the table, so that dish and glass share the same character.

Fortified wines in the kitchen

Fortified wines are highly prized ingredients for their intensity and stability. In Andalusia, fino, amontillado and oloroso give character to many meat, fish and mushroom stews. Dense, dark Pedro Ximénez reductions accompany meats, foie gras and desserts. In France, Madeira sauce is a classic of traditional cooking, and in Italy Marsala from Sicily is indispensable in zabaione and in escalopes alla Marsala.

Does all the alcohol evaporate?

A widespread belief holds that alcohol disappears completely during cooking. This is not quite true. Although alcohol boils at a lower temperature than water, in a mixture it evaporates gradually, and the amount that remains depends on the cooking time, the temperature, the size of the pan and whether it is covered. A stew simmered for hours will retain much less alcohol than a sauce to which the wine is added at the end, or a flambéed dessert, but it is difficult for the alcohol to disappear entirely. Those who must avoid alcohol completely, for health, religious or other reasons, should therefore bear this in mind, and it is equally worth remembering when cooking for children.

Some practical tips

For wine to blend in well, it is usually added at the beginning or halfway through cooking and left to cook long enough to remove the raw taste of alcohol. If it is added at the end, it should be in small amounts. Wine left over in a bottle can be kept for cooking for a few days in the refrigerator, tightly closed, or frozen in small portions. A wine that has begun to oxidise can still serve for a robust stew, but one that has turned to vinegar or shows clear faults is best discarded.

Kitchen and glass

Cooking with wine is, in the end, another form of pairing. A stew made with a red Burgundy is naturally accompanied by a Pinot Noir from the same region, and a butter and white wine sauce, such as the beurre blanc of the Loire, finds its partner in a dry, fresh white from that area. The wine in the dish and the wine in the glass reinforce each other.
