History & culture

The history of port

From the English merchants of the seventeenth century to Pombal's demarcation and today's styles: how the fortified wine of the Douro valley was born and evolved.

Port is one of the best-known fortified wines in the world and one of the clearest examples of how international trade can shape a wine. It is made from grapes grown in the Douro valley in northern Portugal, but takes its name from the city of Porto, from where it was shipped. Its history brings together Portuguese growers, British merchants, political decisions of European reach and an exceptional landscape carved into terraces.

Before port

Vines were grown in the Douro valley long before modern times, and the region produced red wines that were drunk locally or sent downriver. During the seventeenth century, frequent wars and tensions between England and France led English merchants to look for wine elsewhere. Portugal, England's traditional ally, became a natural source of supply.

The merchants settled first on the coast, around Viana do Castelo, and later in Porto. They soon found that the robust reds of the interior, from the Upper Douro, were appreciated in the English market, although the long sea voyage tested their stability.

The role of brandy

To protect the wine during transport, grape spirit began to be added. Over time the practice evolved: instead of adding spirit to finished wine, it came to be added during fermentation. This technique, fortification, stops the yeasts working and leaves part of the grape's natural sugar in the wine. The result is a sweet, powerful and stable wine. The process was not a sudden invention but a gradual evolution that became established over the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries.

The Methuen Treaty

In 1703, England and Portugal signed the Methuen Treaty, a commercial agreement under which Portuguese wines would pay lower customs duties in England than French wines, in exchange for easier access for English textiles in Portugal. The treaty gave Portuguese wine exports a lasting advantage and strengthened the presence of British trading houses in Porto and in Vila Nova de Gaia, on the opposite bank of the Douro, where the ageing cellars, known as lodges, were established.

Pombal and the first demarcation

Commercial success also had downsides: by the mid-eighteenth century there was overproduction, falling prices and complaints of adulteration. The response came from the Marquis of Pombal, the powerful minister of King José I. In 1756 the Companhia Geral da Agricultura das Vinhas do Alto Douro was created, a company with wide privileges to regulate production and trade.

At the same time, the area authorised to produce wine for export was delimited, its boundaries marked with granite posts, some of which still survive. This demarcation is regarded as one of the first delimited and regulated wine regions in the world. Vineyards were also classified according to the quality of their wine. Pombal's measures caused friction with British merchants and with some local producers, but they laid the foundations of a system of origin control that, with many changes, has lasted to this day.

The nineteenth century: splendour and crisis

The nineteenth century was a golden age for port in the British market, where it became a drink associated with the social life of the well-to-do. Merchants and travellers left accounts of the region; among them was the Briton Joseph James Forrester, who drew detailed maps of the Douro and defended the quality of the wine against what he saw as bad practices.

The region also suffered severe crises. Powdery mildew arrived in the middle of the century and, from the 1860s, phylloxera devastated much of the vineyard. Replanting, with vines grafted onto American rootstocks, shifted part of the cultivation towards the eastern reaches of the valley, such as the Douro Superior.

The Douro landscape

Douro vineyards cover very steep slopes of schist soils, in a climate of extremely hot summers. To farm them, generations of growers built terraces held up by dry-stone walls, and later other forms of banks. For centuries, young wine was carried downriver in rabelo boats, flat-bottomed vessels, to the lodges in Gaia. Railways, roads and the dams built in the twentieth century ended that traffic, although the rabelos remain a symbol of the city. In 2001 UNESCO declared the Alto Douro Wine Region a World Heritage cultural landscape.

Varieties and styles

Port is made from numerous authorised varieties, notably Touriga Nacional, Touriga Franca and Tinta Roriz, the local name for Tempranillo, along with others such as Tinta Barroca and Tinto Cão. Traditionally the grapes were trodden in stone troughs called lagares, a practice some houses still keep.

The styles fall into two broad families:

  • Bottle-aged or fruit-driven ports, such as ruby, Late Bottled Vintage and Vintage, the last coming from a single exceptional year declared by each house and intended for long development in bottle.
  • Wood-aged, oxidative ports, such as tawny, which mature in casks or vats and take on amber hues and nutty aromas; they may state an average age or, in the case of colheita, a single harvest.

There are also white ports and, more recently, rosé ports.

Port today

Production is regulated by the Instituto dos Vinhos do Douro e do Porto. Alongside the great historic houses, many of British origin, the quintas, estates in the valley itself that make and bottle their own wines, have gained prominence. At the same time, the Douro has become a leading region for unfortified table wines, red and white, adding a new chapter to a story that began more than three centuries ago.
