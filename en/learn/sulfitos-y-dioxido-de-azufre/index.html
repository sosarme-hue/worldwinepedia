Sulphites and sulphur dioxide

Sulphur dioxide is the most widely used preservative in winemaking, protecting wine from oxidation and microorganisms. What it is, how it works, why labels mention sulphites and what a wine with no added sulphites means.

Few substances have been as important in the history of winemaking as sulphur dioxide, often known in the cellar simply as SO2 or sulphur. Its use to sanitise containers, by burning sulphur inside them, has been known for centuries, and its direct application to wine became widespread with the development of modern oenology. Today it is the most widely used additive in winemaking throughout the world. The terms sulphites and sulphur dioxide are often used interchangeably: strictly speaking, sulphites are the salts and chemical forms in which sulphur dioxide is present in wine, and that is the word that appears on labels.

What it is used for

Sulphur dioxide performs several functions at once, which explains why it is so difficult to replace.

  • Antioxidant: it reacts with oxygen and with compounds formed during oxidation, such as acetaldehyde, and helps preserve the fresh aromas and colour of the wine.
  • Anti-enzymatic: it inhibits the grape enzymes responsible for browning the juice, which are especially active in damaged or rot-affected grapes.
  • Antimicrobial: it checks the growth of unwanted bacteria and yeasts, such as acetic bacteria or yeasts of the genus Brettanomyces, which can spoil the wine. The usual fermenting yeasts tolerate it better, so a moderate dose in the juice favours a clean fermentation.

In addition, sulphur dioxide binds with acetaldehyde, thereby masking the bruised-apple aromas that this compound gives to oxidised wines.

Free and bound

Once added to wine, sulphur dioxide does not remain in a single form. Part of it binds to other molecules, such as acetaldehyde, certain sugars or some acids: this is bound sulphur dioxide, which has little protective activity. Another part remains free, and this is the fraction that really does the work. The sum of the two is called total sulphur dioxide, and it is this figure that legal limits regulate.

Of the free sulphur dioxide, only a small fraction is present in molecular form, the most effective against microorganisms. That fraction depends largely on pH: the lower the pH, that is, the more acidic the wine, the greater the proportion of molecular sulphur dioxide. A high-acid wine therefore needs less sulphur dioxide to be equally well protected than a wine with a high pH. This relationship is one of the reasons why acidity matters so much for the keeping qualities of wine.

How and when it is added

Sulphur dioxide can be added as a gas, in aqueous solution or in the form of salts such as potassium metabisulphite, which releases sulphur dioxide when dissolved in the acidic medium of wine. Sulphur wicks or tablets are still burned inside empty barrels to disinfect and preserve them.

Additions are usually made at several stages: on the grapes or juice on arrival at the winery, after malolactic fermentation in wines that undergo it, during ageing and before bottling. Each winery adjusts the doses according to the health of the grapes, the pH, the type of wine and the style sought. White and rosé wines, which are more sensitive to oxidation, and sweet wines, which are at risk of refermentation, generally need more than reds, which are protected by their polyphenols.

There is no completely sulphite-free wine

Yeasts naturally produce small amounts of sulphites during fermentation. As a result, even a wine to which no sulphur dioxide has been added normally contains some. The correct expression is therefore wine with no added sulphites. Making wine in this way requires very healthy grapes, rigorous hygiene and close attention throughout the process, and the resulting wines may be more vulnerable to oxidation and microbial spoilage if they are not stored in good conditions.

Legal limits and labelling

The legislation of many countries sets maximum levels of total sulphur dioxide, which vary according to the type of wine. In the European Union, for example, the limits for dry wines are lower for reds than for whites and rosés, and considerably higher for certain sweet wines. Organic production regulations impose lower limits than for conventional wines, and some private certifications are stricter still.

In the European Union, the United States and other countries, wines containing more than 10 milligrams per litre of sulphites must state this on the label with the words contains sulphites or an equivalent. Practically all wines exceed that threshold, so the statement appears on almost every bottle, including many organic wines.

Sulphites and health

The requirement to declare sulphites exists because they can cause reactions in a particularly sensitive part of the population, notably in some people with asthma. For this reason they are among the allergens that must be indicated on food labels. For most people, the amounts present in wine are not considered problematic, and some foods, such as certain dried fruits, may contain markedly higher concentrations.

Sulphites are often blamed for the headaches that some people associate with wine, but this link has not been demonstrated, and other factors, starting with alcohol itself, may play a part. Anyone with doubts about their sensitivity to these compounds should consult a healthcare professional.

Alternatives and trends

Oenological research has been seeking to reduce the use of sulphur dioxide for decades. Approaches explored include protecting the juice with inert gases, working at low temperatures, and using lysozyme against lactic bacteria, chitosan against certain yeasts, or tannins and mannoproteins with antioxidant properties. None of them alone provides all the functions of sulphur dioxide, but combined with good hygiene they allow doses to be reduced significantly. That has been the general trend in recent decades: wines with ever lower sulphur dioxide levels, without sacrificing stability.
