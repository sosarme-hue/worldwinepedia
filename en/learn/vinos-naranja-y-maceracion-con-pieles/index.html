Oenology & science

Orange wines and skin contact

Orange wines are made from white grapes vinified as if they were red, fermenting on their skins. The result is an amber colour, a tannic texture and a distinctive aromatic profile.

The term orange wine has spread in recent decades to describe a type of wine that is in fact very old: one made from white grapes fermented in contact with their skins, in the same way as a red wine. The name has nothing to do with the fruit; it refers to the colour these wines usually take on, ranging from deep gold to amber or copper. In some places people prefer to speak of amber wines or skin-contact white wines, and certain regulations use the latter term.

A red winemaking technique applied to white grapes

In ordinary white winemaking, the juice is separated from the skins before fermentation to produce a clean, fresh wine without tannins. For orange wines, by contrast, the white grapes are wholly or partly destemmed, crushed and fermented together with their skins, sometimes with some of the stems as well. Contact may last from a few days to several months, depending on the style sought.

During this maceration, alcohol and time extract from the skins compounds that are scarcely present in a conventional white: polyphenols, including tannins, yellow and brown pigments, and numerous aroma precursors. White grape skins do not contain the anthocyanins that colour red wines, but they do contain other phenolic compounds which, as they oxidise to a greater or lesser degree, produce the characteristic orange hues.

Differences from other practices

Orange wines should be distinguished from other techniques involving skin contact. The brief skin maceration used for some whites, lasting a few hours before fermentation, aims to extract aroma without adding tannin, and yields wines that remain white in style. Nor should orange wine be confused with oxidised wine: although many are made with some exposure to oxygen, the colour comes mainly from extraction from the skins, and there are very fresh orange wines made with protection against oxidation.

Nor are they the same as rosé, which is made from red grapes with brief skin contact, or as wines made from a mixture of white and red grapes.

Historical roots

Fermenting white grapes with their skins is a traditional practice in Georgia, where it has been carried out for centuries in large buried clay vessels called qvevri. There, particularly in the region of Kakheti, white wines from varieties such as Rkatsiteli are fermented and macerated for long periods with their skins, and even their stems, producing amber wines of remarkable structure.

In western Europe the technique enjoyed a revival from the late twentieth century, above all on the border between Italy and Slovenia, in the Friulian Collio and in Goriška Brda, where some producers revived long macerations for local varieties such as Ribolla Gialla, known in Slovenia as Rebula. Friuli also has a tradition of copper-tinted Pinot Grigio made with brief skin contact, known as ramato. From there the style has spread to many wine-producing countries.

What an orange wine is like

Orange wines combine features of whites and reds. Their colour can range from golden yellow to deep amber. On the nose they often show notes of ripe or dried fruit, such as dried apricot or orange peel, together with aromas of tea, spices, nuts, dried flowers, honey or herbs. On the palate they stand out for their texture: the tannins give a grip and dryness unusual in white wines, often accompanied by lively acidity and a pleasant bitterness.

The intensity of these features depends on the variety, the length of maceration, the vessel and exposure to oxygen. A maceration of a few days gives lighter-coloured wines with a delicate structure; one of several months produces deeply amber, tannic wines. Aromatic varieties such as Malvasia or the Muscats express themselves very differently from neutral ones.

Vessels and practices

Orange wines can be made in any vessel: stainless steel, wood, concrete or clay containers such as amphorae, Spanish tinajas or qvevri. Many producers of this style identify with a philosophy of minimal intervention, using native yeasts, little or no added sulphur dioxide and no filtration, but this is not a requirement: skin maceration is a technique, not a production system.

One effect of maceration is that the extracted polyphenols give the wine some protection against oxidation, much as in red wines. For this reason some producers consider that it allows them to use less sulphur dioxide.

At the table

Because of their tannic structure, orange wines behave differently at the table from conventional whites. They tend to pair well with richly flavoured dishes, spiced cuisines, roasted vegetables, mushrooms, mature cheeses or white meats in sauces. They are generally served at a slightly higher temperature than light whites, so that their aromas and texture can fully express themselves.

A style with its own identity

What for a time might have seemed a curiosity has become an established category, present on wine lists and in the ranges of producers all over the world. Orange wines are a reminder that the line between white and red is more a matter of technique than of grape, and they have drawn attention back to ancient practices that for a long time survived only in a few local traditions.
