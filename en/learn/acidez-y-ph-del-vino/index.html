Acidity and pH in wine

Acids give wine freshness and longevity, while pH affects its colour, stability and resistance to spoilage. Which acids grapes contain, how they are measured and how growers and winemakers manage them.

Acidity is one of the pillars of wine. On the palate it is perceived as freshness, liveliness and tension; it makes the mouth water and balances the sensations of sweetness, alcohol and bitterness. A wine with too little acidity tastes flat, heavy or flabby; one with too much tastes hard and sharp. But acidity is not just a matter of taste: it also largely determines how a wine develops, how long it can be kept and how it responds to cellar practices. To understand it, two related but distinct concepts must be distinguished: total acidity and pH.

The acids in wine

Grapes contain mainly two organic acids: tartaric and malic. Tartaric acid is relatively characteristic of the vine and is the most important acid in finished wine; it remains stable during ripening and resists the action of microorganisms. Malic acid, also found in apples and other fruit, is abundant in green grapes and decreases during ripening, especially in warm climates, because the plant consumes it through respiration. Citric acid is present in small quantities.

Other acids form during winemaking. Alcoholic fermentation produces succinic acid, which has a somewhat salty, bitter taste. Malolactic fermentation converts malic acid into lactic acid, whose acidity is softer. And acetic acid, the main component of what is known as volatile acidity, is always present in small amounts.

Total acidity and volatile acidity

Total acidity measures the overall quantity of acids present in a wine. In Spain and much of the world it is expressed in grams per litre of tartaric acid; in France the equivalent in sulphuric acid has traditionally been used, so the figures are not directly comparable. Total acidity correlates fairly well with the intensity of sourness on the palate, although this also depends on the type of acid, the sugar and the alcohol.

Volatile acidity refers to the acids that can be separated by distillation, chiefly acetic acid. In small doses it is a normal part of wine and may contribute to its complexity; in excess it smells of vinegar or nail varnish and is considered a fault. For this reason the law sets maximum limits, and winemakers monitor it closely during ageing.

What pH is

pH measures the concentration of free hydrogen ions and therefore expresses the strength of acidity rather than its quantity. The scale is logarithmic: in this sense, a wine with a pH of 3.0 is ten times more acidic than one with a pH of 4.0. Most wines have a pH roughly between 3 and 4; whites from cool climates tend to lie at the lower end of that range, and many reds from warm areas at the upper end.

Two wines with the same total acidity can have different pH values. This is mainly due to potassium, a mineral that the vine absorbs from the soil and accumulates in the grapes. Potassium partly neutralises acids and raises pH. Varieties, soils and farming practices that encourage potassium accumulation tend to produce wines with a high pH.

Why pH matters

Although it is not perceived on the palate as clearly as total acidity, pH influences almost every aspect of wine:

  • Microbiological stability: a low pH hinders the growth of unwanted bacteria and yeasts, while a high pH encourages them.
  • Effectiveness of sulphur dioxide: the active fraction of sulphur dioxide increases as pH falls, so low-pH wines need smaller doses to be protected.
  • Colour: in red wines, anthocyanins show redder, brighter hues at low pH and duller, purplish or bluish tones at high pH.
  • Development: low-pH wines tend to oxidise more slowly and generally age better.

Acidity in the vineyard

Climate is the most decisive factor. In cool regions grapes retain more malic acid and reach ripeness with high acidity; in warm regions acidity falls rapidly at the end of ripening. This is why growers in warm areas keep a close eye on the harvest date, seek out high-altitude or cooler-facing plots and adapt canopy management to protect bunches from excessive sun. Climate change has made retaining acidity a growing concern in many regions.

Varieties also differ. Some, such as Riesling, Albariño, Chenin Blanc and Barbera, are known for keeping high acidity even when fully ripe; others tend to lose it more easily.

Acidity in the winery

When grapes arrive with insufficient acidity, the legislation of many warm regions permits acidification, normally with tartaric acid, within certain limits. In cool regions, where acidity can be excessive, deacidification is authorised instead, for example with carbonates. Malolactic fermentation itself is, in practice, a natural way of reducing acidity.

Tartaric acid also poses a stability problem. Combined with potassium, it forms potassium bitartrate, which is poorly soluble in the presence of alcohol and at low temperatures. If the wine is not stabilised, small crystals can appear in the bottle; they are harmless but often mistaken for fragments of glass. Cold stabilisation and other treatments prevent this.

Acidity and balance

Acidity is never perceived in isolation. Sugar counterbalances it, so a sweet wine can tolerate, and even need, very high acidity in order not to be cloying; the great sweet Rieslings of Germany and the wines of Tokaj are good examples. Alcohol and a rich texture soften it, while tannins reinforce it. The final result depends on this interplay, which is why tasters judge acidity not by its absolute amount but by the harmony it achieves with the other components.
