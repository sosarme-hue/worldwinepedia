History & culture

The 1855 Bordeaux classification

The origins, criteria and enduring relevance of the ranking of châteaux in the Médoc, Graves and Sauternes drawn up for the 1855 Paris Universal Exposition.

Few lists have been as influential in the world of wine as the so-called 1855 classification. Drawn up in a matter of weeks for an international exhibition, it has survived for more than a century and a half almost unchanged and still shapes the reputation of a group of Bordeaux estates. Its story helps explain both how the Bordeaux trade worked and the limits of any official hierarchy.

The setting: the Paris Universal Exposition

In 1855, Napoleon III's Second Empire staged a Universal Exposition in Paris to show the world the wealth of French industry and agriculture, following the example of the great London exhibition of 1851. The wines of the Gironde were to have a prominent place, and the Bordeaux Chamber of Commerce was asked to present a selection along with a classification to guide visitors.

The Chamber handed the task to the union of brokers (courtiers), the intermediaries who brought estate owners together with merchants. Nobody knew better than they did the prices each wine fetched on the market. In April 1855 they delivered their list.

A commercial yardstick

The classification was not based on tastings held for the occasion or on a study of soils. Its foundation was essentially economic: the prices at which each estate's wines had sold over many years, which in turn reflected their reputation among buyers. To a large extent the brokers simply gave official form to a hierarchy the Bordeaux trade had already been applying since the eighteenth century.

This explains some of its features. Only estates with an established commercial track record were included, and the geographical scope was limited to the areas whose wine was sold through the traditional channels of Bordeaux brokers and merchants.

The reds: five tiers

For red wines, the list divided the estates into five tiers, from premiers crus to cinquièmes crus, that is, from first to fifth growths. With one exception, all the classified reds came from the Médoc, the peninsula north of the city on the left bank of the Garonne and the Gironde estuary, with communes such as Margaux, Saint-Julien, Pauillac and Saint-Estèphe.

At the top stood four first growths: Château Lafite, Château Latour, Château Margaux and Château Haut-Brion. The last is the geographical exception: it lies in Pessac, in the Graves area south of the city, and its fame was so great that it could not be left out. Today it belongs to the Pessac-Léognan appellation.

Médoc reds are based on varieties such as Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Cabernet Franc and Petit Verdot, with the first frequently dominant at the great estates of this bank.

The sweet whites of Sauternes and Barsac

The classification also covered the sweet white wines of Sauternes and Barsac, made mainly from Sémillon and Sauvignon Blanc affected by noble rot. Here first and second growths were established, and above them all stood Château d'Yquem, in a category of its own as premier cru supérieur, unique in the list.

An almost frozen list

One of the most striking traits of the classification is its stability. Shortly after publication, Château Cantemerle was added to the fifth growths. The only significant change came in 1973, when Château Mouton Rothschild, listed as a second growth, was promoted to first growth after years of lobbying by its owner, Baron Philippe de Rothschild.

Over time, some estates were split up, changed their names or altered their vineyards, so the number of châteaux on the list has varied slightly. But in essence the hierarchy remains that of 1855. An important detail is that the classification applies to the estate, to the château's brand, not to specific plots: an estate can buy or sell vineyards without losing its rank.

What was left out

The 1855 list omitted areas that are now world-famous. Saint-Émilion and Pomerol, on the right bank, were not included, partly because their wines were sold through other channels. Nor did most Graves wines feature, apart from Haut-Brion.

These gaps led to other, independent classifications:

  • Graves established its own classification in the mid-twentieth century, for reds and whites.
  • Saint-Émilion created a classification in the 1950s which, unlike that of 1855, is revised periodically, a process that has caused no small amount of controversy.
  • Pomerol has no official classification, despite the prestige of many of its estates.
  • In the Médoc, the crus bourgeois category groups many estates not classified in 1855, under a system of its own that has changed several times.

Debate and relevance

The 1855 classification has often been criticised. Critics say it reflects the market of the mid-nineteenth century rather than present-day quality, that it ignores how many estates have changed and that it has frozen a hierarchy the trade had built flexibly. Some fifth growths have gained prestige, while others ranked higher have gone through difficult periods.

Its defenders reply that the list still broadly reflects the ability of certain Médoc terroirs to produce top-level wines for ageing, and that its stability is part of its historical value. Either way, the 1855 classification has become a cultural reference in its own right and an early example of how a wine region organised its prestige to present itself to the world.
