Viticulture

Native grape varieties and their revival

Why so many local grapes came close to disappearing, how they are identified and rescued today with the help of ampelography and DNA, and what they bring to the diversity of wine.

There are thought to be several thousand varieties of the grapevine species Vitis vinifera, although a small fraction of them occupies most of the world's vineyard area. During the twentieth century, many local grapes were sidelined or came close to disappearing. In recent decades, however, growers, researchers and institutions have made a considerable effort to identify them, study them and bring them back into cultivation.

What a native variety is

The term native, or autochthonous, refers to a variety belonging to a particular territory, cultivated there for a long time and bound up with its history. Strictly speaking, it would be a grape that originated in that place, but the origin of many varieties is uncertain or lies in neighbouring areas, so people often speak of traditional or local varieties as well. What matters is their roots in the place: generations of growers have selected them and adapted them to a specific climate and soils.

Why they were lost

The loss of diversity had several causes:

  • Phylloxera: when replanting on rootstocks after the plague, many growers chose the most productive or best-known varieties, and many minor grapes were never replanted.
  • The pursuit of yield: for much of the twentieth century, grapes that produced more or suited large-scale winemaking were favoured.
  • Internationalisation: the spread of a small group of world-famous varieties, such as Cabernet Sauvignon and Chardonnay, displaced local grapes in some places.
  • Rural abandonment: with depopulation, many old mixed vineyards where rare varieties survived were no longer worked.

How a variety is recovered

Recovery usually begins with prospecting: old vineyards, field margins and family plots are searched for vines that do not match known varieties. Information from older growers, who remember local names and uses, often proves decisive.

Next comes identification. Ampelography, the discipline that describes vines by the shape of their leaves, bunches, berries and shoots, was for centuries the main tool. Since the late twentieth century it has been complemented by DNA analysis, which can establish with great precision whether two vines belong to the same variety and what their family relationships are. These analyses have shown, for example, that Californian Zinfandel and Italian Primitivo are the same grape, of Croatian origin, and that the Carménère grown in Chile had been mistaken for Merlot for decades until it was identified in the 1990s. The parents of famous varieties have also been discovered, such as Cabernet Sauvignon, born of a spontaneous cross between Cabernet Franc and Sauvignon Blanc.

The identified vines are preserved in germplasm banks or ampelographic collections, such as El Encín near Madrid or Vassal in the south of France, one of the largest in the world. There, their agronomic behaviour, health and winemaking potential are studied, often through experimental micro-vinifications.

Finally, for a variety to be grown and sold legally, it usually has to be registered in official catalogues and, where relevant, included among the grapes authorised by an appellation.

Examples of revival

There are many success stories. In Galicia, red grapes such as Merenzao, Brancellao, Caíño and Sousón, which had been pushed aside, are regaining a place in areas such as Ribeira Sacra and Ribeiro. In the west of the Spanish meseta, Juan García in Arribes and Prieto Picudo in the province of León have become emblems of their areas.

In Italy, a country of enormous varietal wealth, Fiano in Campania, which had declined sharply, was rescued in the second half of the twentieth century and now makes some of the most admired whites of the south. In Piedmont, the white Timorasso went from a nearly forgotten rarity to one of the region's most highly regarded white grapes. In Greece, Malagousia was saved when hardly any vines remained, and other ancient grapes are following the same path.

Why they matter

The revival of native varieties has several dimensions:

  • Diversity and originality: they offer different aromas, textures and styles, enriching the world of wine and allowing each region to express an identity of its own.
  • Climate adaptation: some local grapes, selected over centuries in difficult conditions, show resistance to drought or heat, or ripen later, qualities that are valuable in the face of climate change.
  • Cultural heritage: they are linked to traditions, names, recipes and landscapes, and their disappearance would mean the loss of part of rural memory.
  • Genetic resources: they form a reserve for the future improvement of the vine.

Limits and challenges

Not every recovered variety is of winemaking interest: some were abandoned for good reasons, such as poor quality, susceptibility to disease or irregular production. Research serves precisely to distinguish those that deserve a second chance. The work also demands time, funding and patience: many years can pass between locating a vine and its wine reaching the market.

Even so, interest in these grapes keeps growing. What a few decades ago was considered a curiosity has become one of the liveliest currents in the world of wine, one in which rescuing the past is also seen as a way of preparing for the future.
