Practical guides

Types of wine glasses

The parts of a glass, the materials and the main shapes for reds, whites, sparkling and fortified wines, and why a good all-purpose glass covers most situations.

The glass is the tool with which wine is observed, smelled and drunk, and its shape influences what is perceived. There is no need to own a different glass for each grape variety, as some manufacturers suggest, but it is worth knowing which features make a glass work well and what the most common shapes are for.

The parts of a glass

A wine glass has four parts:

  • The bowl, which holds the wine. Its size and shape determine how much air sits above the liquid and how the aromas gather.
  • The rim, the part that touches the lips. The thinner it is, the less it comes between the wine and the mouth.
  • The stem, which lets you hold the glass without warming the wine with your hand or leaving fingerprints on the bowl.
  • The foot, the base that keeps it stable.

A good tasting glass has three features: it is clear and colourless, so colour and clarity can be assessed; its bowl is wide at the base and narrower towards the rim, in a tulip shape, so the wine can be swirled and the aromas retained; and its rim is thin and smooth.

Materials

Glasses are made from ordinary glass or crystal. Traditional crystal contained lead oxide, which gave it brilliance and allowed very thin walls. Today, so-called lead-free crystal is widely used, replacing lead with other oxides and offering similar clarity and strength. Hand-blown glasses are usually lighter and more delicate; machine-made ones are sturdier and better suited to everyday use and the dishwasher. Decorated, cut or coloured glasses make it harder to see the wine and are best kept for ornamental use.

The main shapes

Glasses for red wine

These are usually the largest. The Bordeaux glass is tall, with a broad bowl whose sides curve gently inwards towards the rim; it is associated with structured, tannic reds, such as those made from cabernet sauvignon or tempranillo. The Burgundy glass has a wider, more rounded, balloon-like bowl that offers a large surface in contact with air; it is associated with aromatic, delicate reds, such as those made from pinot noir or nebbiolo.

Glasses for white and rosé wine

These are generally somewhat smaller than red-wine glasses, which helps keep the wine cool by serving smaller amounts and topping up more often. Light, aromatic whites are well served in narrower bowls; fuller, oak-aged whites, such as some chardonnays, can benefit from slightly broader glasses.

Glasses for sparkling wine

The flute, tall and narrow, became popular because it shows off the stream of bubbles and preserves the gas well. Its narrow opening, however, limits the perception of aromas, so many professionals now prefer a tulip glass, or even a white-wine glass, for complex, long-aged sparkling wines. The wide, shallow coupe, fashionable in earlier times, quickly disperses gas and aromas and is used today mainly for its decorative appeal.

Glasses for fortified and sweet wines

Fortified and sweet wines are served in small quantities and have intense aromas, so smaller glasses are used. In Jerez the traditional glass is the catavino, a tall, narrow tulip-shaped glass that concentrates the aromas of fino, manzanilla or oloroso. Small glasses of similar shape are used for port and other fortified wines.

The standard tasting glass

For professional tastings there is a standardised glass defined by the international standard ISO 3591. It is a small, tulip-shaped glass of colourless glass, designed so that all wines are assessed under the same conditions. It is not the most striking glass on a table, but it is a useful reference, and many schools and competitions use it.

The all-purpose glass

In practice, a good all-purpose glass, medium-sized, with a tulip-shaped bowl and a thin rim, works for almost every wine: reds, whites, rosés and sparkling wines too. For a beginner, a set of glasses of this type is more useful than many specific shapes. Later, a large glass for aromatic reds and a small one for fortified wines can be added.

Using and caring for glasses

A few simple habits improve the experience:

  • Hold the glass by the stem or the foot, so as not to warm the wine or smudge the bowl.
  • Fill it only to about a third, so the wine can be swirled and its aromas appreciated.
  • Before pouring, check that it does not smell of cupboard, cardboard or detergent: such odours interfere with the wine's.
  • Wash fine glasses in hot water with little detergent, rinse thoroughly and dry with a lint-free cloth. Store them upright rather than upside down if the cupboard may pass on odours.

What matters

The influence of glass shape is real but has limits: a suitable glass lets a wine show what it has, without turning it into something else. More than the brand or the specific design, what matters is that the glass is clean, clear, generous enough in size and with a rim that narrows slightly. Under those conditions, any wine can be appreciated properly.
