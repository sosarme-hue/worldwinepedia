Phylloxera: the pest that changed the vineyard

How a tiny insect from America destroyed much of Europe's vineyards in the nineteenth century and why almost all vines today are grafted.

In the second half of the nineteenth century, Europe's vineyards suffered the worst crisis in their history. An almost invisible insect, phylloxera, attacked the roots of the vines and killed them, region after region, over several decades. The response to the pest changed viticulture for ever: since then, the vast majority of the world's vines have grown grafted onto the roots of American species.

An American insect

Phylloxera, whose current scientific name is Daktulosphaira vitifoliae, is a small insect related to aphids, native to North America. It has a complex life cycle, with forms that live on the leaves, where they cause galls, and others that feed on the roots. On American vines, which evolved alongside it, phylloxera causes limited damage: these species form tissues that heal the wounds. The European vine, Vitis vinifera, lacks that defence. Feeding on the roots causes swellings and lesions through which fungi and bacteria enter; the roots rot and the plant dies within a few years.

Arrival in Europe

In the mid-nineteenth century, steamships shortened transatlantic voyages and exchanges of plants between America and Europe multiplied, including American vines imported for botanical purposes or to test their resistance to powdery mildew, another pest of American origin that had arrived a little earlier. Phylloxera travelled with them, unnoticed.

The first damage was observed in France in the 1860s, in the Rhône valley. In 1868 a group of scientists, prominent among them the botanist Jules-Émile Planchon, identified the insect on the roots of diseased vines. For years it was debated whether it was the cause of the disease or a consequence of it, while the pest advanced unchecked through almost every French wine region.

The spread of the pest

From France, phylloxera spread across Europe over the following decades: Portugal, Spain, Italy, Germany, Austria, Switzerland and the Balkans. It also reached other parts of the world, such as Australia, South Africa and California, where it had arrived with European plant material.

In Spain, the pest was detected in the province of Málaga at the end of the 1870s and, at almost the same time, in Empordà, in Catalonia, near the French border. From these foci it advanced slowly across the peninsula over several decades. Meanwhile, the destruction of French vineyards had a paradoxical effect in some Spanish regions: demand for wine from France boosted areas such as Rioja, where French merchants and technicians arrived and helped modernise winemaking and barrel ageing. When phylloxera reached Rioja too, at the end of the nineteenth century, that period of prosperity came to an end.

The search for a remedy

For years numerous treatments were tried. Flooding vineyards in winter was effective where water and flat land were available. Carbon disulphide, a toxic and expensive product, was injected into the soil; it slowed the pest but did not eradicate it. It was also observed that phylloxera does not thrive in very sandy soils, which favoured planting in some coastal areas.

Another path was direct producer hybrids, crosses between European and American vines that resisted the pest. They were widely planted, but many gave wines considered of lower quality, with flavours foreign to the European tradition; over time, many regulations restricted their use for quality wine.

The solution: grafting

The definitive answer was grafting: joining the aerial part of a European variety, which produces the grapes, to a rootstock of a resistant American species, which provides the roots. Species such as Vitis riparia, Vitis rupestris and Vitis berlandieri, and their crosses, became the basis of modern rootstocks. These had to be adapted to very different soils, especially limestone, where some American species suffered from chlorosis. French and American scientists collaborated on this task, and the exchange of knowledge was decisive.

Replanting was slow and costly. Many estates disappeared, many growers emigrated and the wine map changed: some areas were never replanted, others were converted, and the number of varieties grown was reduced in many regions. The crisis also encouraged fraud, with adulterated wines or wines made from raisins, which contributed to the appearance of the first laws protecting designations of origin in the early decades of the twentieth century.

Places that escaped

Phylloxera did not reach everywhere. Chile, isolated by the Andes, the desert and the ocean, retains much of its vineyard area ungrafted. The same is true of some parts of Australia, protected by strict quarantines, and of the Canary Islands, where the pest never became established. In sandy soils, such as those of Colares in Portugal, or in some isolated plots in Europe, old vines planted on their own roots survive and are highly valued today as witnesses to the pre-phylloxera vineyard.

A threat that persists

Phylloxera has not disappeared: it is still present in the soils of almost every wine region, kept in check by rootstocks. In the 1980s in California, a rootstock that had proved insufficiently resistant suffered severe attacks from a biotype of the pest, and many vineyards had to be replanted. The story of phylloxera is a reminder of the importance of plant health, control of planting material and research in modern viticulture.
