Food pairing

Wine with Mexican and Latin American cuisine

Fresh and dried chillies, moles, citrus, maize and grilled meats give the cuisines of Mexico and Latin America a very distinctive personality; this article explains how to pair them with wine, with particular attention to the region's own wines.

The cuisines of Mexico and Latin America are the result of centuries of blending between indigenous traditions, the European heritage, above all Spanish and Portuguese, and African and other influences. American ingredients such as maize, chilli, tomato, avocado, cacao and potato are combined with techniques and products that arrived from elsewhere. The result is a vast and diverse gastronomic universe. At the same time, Latin America is a major wine-producing region, with countries such as Argentina, Chile, Uruguay, Brazil, Peru and Mexico, which makes it possible to explore local pairings that were long overlooked.

Chilli: heat, fruit and smoke

Chilli is the ingredient that most affects wine in Mexican cooking, but not all chillies are alike. Fresh chillies, such as jalapeño or serrano, bring heat and green notes; dried chillies, such as ancho, guajillo or chipotle, also bring flavours of dried fruit, toast and smoke, often with more moderate heat. As in any spicy cuisine, high alcohol and firm tannins intensify the heat. Wines of moderate strength with generous fruit and soft tannins work better, and, with very spicy dishes, aromatic whites or fresh rosés. Dried and smoked chillies, on the other hand, can take fruity reds, whose ripe fruit notes converse with them.

Moles

Mole is one of the most complex preparations in Mexican cooking: a thick sauce that may combine several dried chillies, spices, nuts, seeds, bread or tortilla and, in mole poblano, chocolate. Its combination of sweetness, bitterness, gentle heat and spice makes very tannic or heavily oaked reds seem harsh. Ripe reds with abundant fruit and rounded tannins, such as a garnacha, a Zinfandel or a fruit-forward Malbec, get on better with it. Lighter moles, such as mole verde, made with pumpkin seeds, herbs and fresh chillies, prefer full-bodied whites or rosés.

Tacos, antojitos and guacamole

Tacos are as varied as their fillings. Tacos al pastor, made with pork marinated in chillies and often served with pineapple, suit fruity rosés or aromatic whites with a touch of sweetness. Grilled beef tacos call for juicy, low-tannin reds. Fish tacos, battered or grilled, with cabbage, lime and creamy sauces, work with fresh whites with lively acidity. Guacamole, rich and made with lime, coriander and chilli, goes well with a Sauvignon Blanc, whose herbal and citrus notes complement it. Cochinita pibil from Yucatán, pork marinated with achiote and bitter orange, suits light, fruity reds or full-bodied rosés.

Ceviches and Pacific cooking

Ceviche, the emblematic dish of Peru, found along much of the Pacific coast, is raw fish or shellfish “cooked” in lime or lemon juice, with onion, chilli and coriander. Its acidity is very high, so it calls for equally acidic, unoaked wines: cool-climate Sauvignon Blanc, such as those from Chile's Casablanca Valley, Albariño or dry Torrontés. In Peru, Creole cooking, with dishes such as ají de gallina or lomo saltado, suits medium-bodied reds with ripe fruit.

Argentina, Chile and Uruguay

In the Southern Cone, meat grilled over embers is the centre of the table, and local reds such as Argentine Malbec or Uruguayan Tannat are its natural partners. But the cooking of these countries is broader. Empanadas, found in countless versions, go well with young reds or, in north-western Argentina, with Torrontés from Salta, a highly aromatic white with floral notes. In Chile, pastel de choclo, with its mixture of meat, maize and a sweet touch, finds a good partner in Carmenère, with its red fruit and spicy notes, while Pacific fish and shellfish call for the whites of the coastal valleys.

Brazil

Brazil, whose Serra Gaúcha region is known for its sparkling wines, offers hearty dishes such as feijoada, a stew of black beans with various cuts of pork. Its richness and fat are balanced by reds with good acidity or by dry sparkling wines, whose bubbles and freshness cleanse the palate. Brazilian sparkling wines are also frequently drunk as an aperitif with fried snacks and traditional savoury bites.

The wines of Mexico

Mexico has an old winemaking history on the American continent, and today its best-known region is the Valle de Guadalupe in Baja California, where varieties such as nebbiolo, tempranillo, cabernet sauvignon, syrah and chenin blanc, among others, are grown. Its reds and whites allow local pairings with the cooking of northern Mexico and with the fish and seafood of the Baja California peninsula.

Ideas to remember

With Latin American cuisines, it is best to prefer fruity wines with moderate tannins and good acidity, to keep structured reds for grilled meats, and to turn to aromatic whites and rosés when chilli or citrus dominate. Exploring the region's own wines is, moreover, a natural way of discovering combinations that kitchen and vineyard have shaped together.
