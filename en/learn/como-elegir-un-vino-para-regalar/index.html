How to choose a wine as a gift

Thinking about the person, the occasion and when the wine will be drunk: practical criteria for choosing the right bottle as a gift without spending more than is reasonable.

Giving wine is a widespread custom in many cultures: people take a bottle to a dinner, offer one at a celebration or choose it as a present for someone who enjoys good food. Getting it right depends less on spending a lot than on thinking a little about who will receive it, in what context and when it is likely to be opened. Here are some practical guidelines.

Think about the person

The first criterion is the taste of the recipient. If you know they prefer fresh whites, smooth reds or sparkling wines, the sensible thing is to respect that preference rather than impose your own. If they are a knowledgeable enthusiast, they may appreciate a wine from a little-known region or variety, or a bottle from a small producer. If they hardly drink wine, an approachable, easy-to-enjoy style such as a sparkling wine or a fruity, low-tannin red is usually a better choice than a complex or very structured wine.

It is also worth bearing in mind that some people do not drink alcohol for health, religious or personal reasons. In those cases wine is not a good gift, and there are many alternatives.

Think about the occasion

Taking a bottle to dinner with friends is not the same as giving a present for an anniversary or a birth.

  • Dinner at someone's home: the host may decide to open the bottle that evening or keep it. It is a courtesy not to expect it to be served. A versatile wine that goes well with different dishes is a good choice.
  • Celebrations: sparkling wines, such as cava, champagne or another traditional-method wine, are naturally associated with toasts.
  • A gift to keep: if the intention is for the person to keep the wine for years, choose one with genuine ageing potential and, if possible, give them guidance on how to store it.
  • Desserts or after dinner: a sweet or fortified wine, such as a Pedro Ximénez, a Moscatel, a port or an oloroso, is an original gift that usually lasts longer once opened.

Think about when it will be drunk

One of the most common mistakes is to give a wine that needs years in bottle to someone who will open it that same week or, conversely, to give a young white meant for early drinking as a wine for keeping. A very young, tannic red from a great appellation can seem hard if opened immediately. If you want it to be enjoyed soon, wines that are already at their best are preferable, such as a reserva with a few years of age or a fresh white from the latest vintage.

A classic idea is to give a wine from a child's birth year or from the year of a wedding, to be opened in the future. For this to make sense, the wine must be able to age for decades: some great reds, vintage ports, concentrated sweet wines or certain fortified wines are the usual options. It is worth researching carefully, because vintage quality varies and not all wines last that long.

Origin and story

A wine with a story to tell usually makes the gift more personal: a wine from the recipient's home region, from a place they have visited, from a revived grape variety or from a family winery. A few written lines about why you chose that bottle, where it comes from or what dishes to serve it with add value to the gesture.

Price

You do not need to spend a lot to give well. There are excellent wines at every price level, and a bottle chosen with thought says more than an expensive one chosen only for its fame. Lesser-known appellations or emerging regions often offer good value for money. On the other hand, be wary of very flashy packaging or advertising messages that do not match the quality of the contents.

Practical tips

  • Asking for advice at a specialist shop, explaining the person's taste, the occasion and your budget, is one of the most reliable ways to get it right.
  • Check the condition of the bottle: the wine level should be normal and the cork should not be pushed out or show traces of seeped wine, a sign that it may have suffered heat.
  • Protect the bottle from heat and light between purchase and delivery; do not leave it in a car in the sun.
  • If the wine is to be served chilled, such as a sparkling wine or a white, you can bring it already cold in case the host wishes to open it.
  • Large formats such as the magnum look impressive at celebrations and, for wines meant to age, are considered to favour slower development.
  • A simple box or bag is enough; what matters is the wine.

Giving responsibly

Wine is a product to be enjoyed in moderation, in company and with food. It should never be given to minors. A good gift of wine is, above all, an invitation to share: to open the bottle at a special moment, to discover a place or a grape variety and to celebrate without excess.
