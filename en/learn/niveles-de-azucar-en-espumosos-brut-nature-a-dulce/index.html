Sugar levels in sparkling wine: from brut nature to sweet

What the terms brut nature, extra brut, brut, extra dry, dry, demi-sec and sweet mean, where the sugar in a sparkling wine comes from and how it is perceived in the glass.

Almost every sparkling wine label carries a word indicating its sugar content: brut, extra brut, demi-sec, sweet... These terms are regulated in the European Union and correspond to precise ranges, expressed in grams of sugar per litre. Knowing them tells you in advance whether a sparkling wine will be dry and taut or soft and sweet, and helps you choose it according to the occasion and the food.

Where the sugar comes from

In sparkling wines made by the traditional method, such as champagne, cava or franciacorta, the base wine undergoes a second fermentation in the bottle, during which yeasts consume added sugar and produce carbon dioxide. After ageing on the lees, the sediment is removed at disgorgement, and the small volume lost is then replaced with the liqueur d'expédition, a mixture of wine and, usually, sugar. The amount of sugar this liqueur contributes is called the dosage, and it largely determines the final label term.

In the Charmat or tank method, used for example for much prosecco, the second fermentation takes place in a sealed tank and the sugar level is adjusted before bottling, sometimes by stopping fermentation. In some sweet sparkling wines, such as Asti, the sugar is simply grape sugar that has not fermented. Whatever the method, the label term refers to the total sugar present in the finished wine.

The official terms

European rules set the following ranges of residual sugar, from lowest to highest:

  • Brut nature: less than 3 grams per litre, with no sugar added after the second fermentation. The terms pas dosé and dosage zéro are also used, and in Italy dosaggio zero.
  • Extra brut: between 0 and 6 grams per litre.
  • Brut: less than 12 grams per litre. This is the most widespread term.
  • Extra dry (extra sec, extra seco): between 12 and 17 grams per litre.
  • Dry (sec, seco, secco): between 17 and 32 grams per litre.
  • Demi-sec (semiseco, abboccato): between 32 and 50 grams per litre.
  • Sweet (doux, dulce, dolce): more than 50 grams per litre.

The ranges for brut nature, extra brut and brut overlap: a wine with 2 grams per litre and no dosage could be labelled with any of the three, and the producer chooses the term.

A paradox of vocabulary

The words on the scale can be misleading. “Dry” and “extra dry”, which for a still wine would indicate no perceptible sweetness, designate sparkling wines with noticeable sweetness, sweeter than a brut. The explanation is historical: in the nineteenth century many sparkling wines, champagne in particular, were drunk much sweeter than today, and terms such as “dry” or “extra dry” then signalled versions less sweet than usual. Over time taste moved towards ever drier wines, brut became popular and, more recently, extra brut and brut nature, but the old words kept their relative meaning.

How sugar is perceived

The sensation of sweetness does not depend only on grams of sugar. Sparkling wines generally have high acidity, and carbon dioxide heightens the sense of freshness; both factors balance the sugar and make a brut with several grams per litre taste dry. A wine with high acidity and little dosage can seem sharp; the same dosage in a riper wine may go unnoticed.

Dosage also affects texture and the way the wine develops. A small amount of sugar can round off acidity, soften the feel of the bubbles and contribute to aromatic complexity as the wine ages in bottle. That is why producers adjust the dosage of each wine according to grape ripeness, vintage and the length of lees ageing. Sparkling wines with long ageing and well-ripened grapes lend themselves better to zero-dosage versions, while young wines from cool climates often need some sugar to find their balance.

Which style to choose

As a general guide:

  • Brut nature and extra brut: taut, austere wines that highlight acidity, minerality and aromas from ageing. They go well with shellfish, oysters, raw fish or cured fish.
  • Brut: the most versatile style, suitable as an aperitif and with a wide range of dishes, from fried food to rice dishes.
  • Extra dry and dry: with a discreet sweetness that can work with mildly spiced or sweet-and-sour dishes and with some Asian cuisines.
  • Demi-sec and sweet: suited to desserts that are not too sweet, fruit or cream-based pastries; as a general rule, the wine should be at least as sweet as the dessert.

Reading the label

Besides the sweetness term, some producers state on the back label the exact dosage in grams per litre and the disgorgement date, which gives a clearer idea of the wine's style. Both pieces of information are useful for comparing sparkling wines from the same house or from different regions, and they help in choosing more precisely the right wine for each occasion.
