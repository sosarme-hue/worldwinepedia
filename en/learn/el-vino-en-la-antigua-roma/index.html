History & culture

Wine in ancient Rome

How the Romans grew, made, stored and drank wine, and why its spread across the Empire laid the foundations of many of today's European wine regions.

For the Romans, wine was at once an everyday food, a major commodity, a symbol of civilisation and part of religious worship. Rome did not invent viticulture: it inherited it from the Greeks, the Etruscans and the peoples of the eastern Mediterranean. But it organised it on a scale never seen before and, through its legions, settlers and merchants, carried it across much of western Europe.

From family farm to large estate

In the early centuries of the Republic, the vine was just one crop among others on small peasant holdings. With territorial expansion in the third and second centuries BC, the larger agricultural villa, worked largely by enslaved labour and geared towards the market, spread across Italy. Together with the olive, the vine became one of the most profitable crops.

We know this agriculture well thanks to the treatises of the Latin agronomists. Cato the Elder, in the second century BC, gave practical instructions for running an estate with vineyards. Varro wrote a treatise on farming in the first century BC. In the first century AD, Columella, born in Gades, today's Cádiz, devoted much of his work to the vine: choosing the site, preparing the soil, planting, pruning and yields. Shortly afterwards, Pliny the Elder compiled in his Natural History a long account of grape varieties and wines from across the known world.

How vines were grown and wine was made

The Romans knew several training systems: low, unsupported bush vines, vines held up by stakes or trellises and, in parts of Italy, vines climbing trees such as elms or poplars, a practice that survived for centuries. The agronomists debated the aspect of plots, the depth of planting trenches and the balance between quantity and quality.

After the harvest, grapes were trodden and then pressed, often with beam presses. The must fermented and was stored in large earthenware jars, the dolia, frequently half-buried and lined inside with pitch to make them watertight. For transport, wine went into amphorae, elongated ceramic vessels sealed with stoppers of cork or clay. Towards the end of the Empire, the wooden barrel, a technique of Gaulish origin, gradually gained ground.

Wines with a name

The Romans clearly distinguished wines by their origin. The most celebrated was Falernian, produced in northern Campania, whose fame is echoed by poets such as Horace. Caecuban, from southern Latium, and other wines from Italy and the Greek islands also enjoyed great prestige. Some years lived on in memory: wine from the vintage of the consulship of Opimius, in 121 BC, was cited as the very model of a legendary vintage. This notion that origin and year shape quality anticipates ideas that seem familiar to us today.

Roman taste, however, differed from ours. Wine was normally drunk mixed with water, sometimes hot, and could be flavoured with resins, spices or herbs. Mulsum was wine blended with honey. Defrutum and sapa, boiled and concentrated musts, were used to sweeten and preserve. Passum was a wine made from dried grapes, which Columella describes in detail. At the other end of the social scale, posca, a mixture of water and vinegar, was the usual drink of soldiers and the poor.

Trade on an imperial scale

Wine was one of the great commodities of the Roman world. Amphorae, made by the million and often bearing stamps or inscriptions, now allow archaeologists to reconstruct trade routes. At first it was Italian wines that were exported in huge quantities to Gaul, where local elites prized them highly. Later the provinces became producers and exporters in their own right: wines from Tarraconensis and Baetica travelled to Rome and to the army camps on the frontiers.

The cities around Vesuvius offer an exceptional picture of this economy. In Pompeii, buried by the eruption of AD 79, archaeologists have identified vineyards within the city walls, taverns, storerooms and many inscriptions related to wine.

The vine conquers the provinces

The expansion of the Empire took the vine to lands where it had been unknown or marginal. In Gaul, cultivation first took root in Narbonensis and the Rhône valley, then moved west and north. Pliny and Columella mention a vine called biturica, associated with the Bituriges Vivisci, the people of the area around Burdigala, today's Bordeaux. In the fourth century, the poet Ausonius described in his poem Mosella the slopes covered with vines along the Moselle. In Hispania, viticulture, already present before Rome, reached a remarkable commercial development.

According to the historian Suetonius, the emperor Domitian ordered at the end of the first century that the planting of new vineyards in the provinces be restricted, perhaps to encourage grain growing or to protect Italian production. How far the measure was enforced is unclear, and tradition holds that it was repealed in the third century.

Wine, religion and society

Wine was linked to the cult of Bacchus, the Greek Dionysus, identified with the old Italic god Liber. In 186 BC the Senate harshly suppressed the Bacchanalia, nocturnal rites it regarded as a threat to public order. Wine also played a part in sacrifices and offerings.

In social life, the banquet or convivium was the great stage for wine. Latin texts stress mixing it with water and criticise excess, which was considered unbecoming of a respectable citizen.

A lasting legacy

When the Western Empire broke apart in the fifth century, viticulture did not disappear. Many areas planted in Roman times went on producing wine, and the Church and the monasteries preserved part of that knowledge. Regions such as the Rhône valley, Bordeaux, the Moselle and much of the Iberian Peninsula have roots reaching back to that era, and the very vocabulary of wine in the Romance languages derives largely from Latin.
