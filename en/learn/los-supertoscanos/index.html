History & culture

The Super Tuscans

How a group of Tuscan producers, working outside the rules of the appellations, created wines from the 1960s and 1970s onwards that transformed the image of Italian wine.

The term Super Tuscan informally refers to a series of wines from Tuscany, mostly red, that emerged from the late 1960s outside the rules of the designations of origin of the time. They were made from international varieties such as Cabernet Sauvignon or Merlot, from pure Sangiovese or from blends not provided for by the regulations, and were often aged in small French oak barrels. Although legally sold in the lowest category, table wine, some quickly achieved extraordinary international prestige. Their story is one of rebellion against rules that many producers saw as an obstacle to quality.

The background: Chianti and its rules

For much of the twentieth century, Tuscany's best-known wine was Chianti. Its traditional make-up went back to a formula attributed to Baron Bettino Ricasoli in the nineteenth century, which combined Sangiovese with other red grapes and a share of white grapes such as Malvasia and Trebbiano. When Italy created its system of controlled designations of origin (DOC) in 1963, the Chianti rules adopted that idea and made a certain proportion of white grapes compulsory in a red wine.

Over time, many producers felt that this requirement, together with high yields and an image associated with simple wines, sometimes sold in the famous straw-covered flask, held back the quality and prestige of Tuscan wines.

Sassicaia, the pioneer

The first wine usually cited as the forerunner of the Super Tuscans was born far from Chianti, on the coast, in the Bolgheri area. There, the Marchese Mario Incisa della Rocchetta planted Cabernet on his Tenuta San Guido estate in the 1940s, initially for family consumption. A lover of Bordeaux, he wanted to make a Bordeaux-style red. For years the wine never left the family, but its quality improved over time, and the first vintage to be sold commercially was 1968, under the name Sassicaia.

The distribution of this wine involved the Antinori family, related to the Incisas, and the oenologist Giacomo Tachis, who would play a central role in developing several of the great wines of this generation.

Tignanello and revolution in Chianti

In the heart of Chianti Classico, the house of Antinori launched Tignanello in the early 1970s, a red based on Sangiovese, without white grapes and with a share of Bordeaux varieties, aged in barrel. Since it did not respect the blend required for Chianti, it had to be sold as table wine. Its success showed that a wine outside the designation could be appreciated and command higher prices than most DOC wines.

Other producers followed its example, creating their own flagship wines. Some opted for blends of Sangiovese with Cabernet or Merlot; others, such as Montevertine with Le Pergole Torte or Fontodi with Flaccianello, for Sangiovese on its own, without the white grapes Chianti required. In Bolgheri new estates appeared, such as Ornellaia, focused on Bordeaux varieties.

A name coined by the press

The term Super Tuscan became popular in the English-language press in the 1980s to describe the phenomenon. It was not a legal category but a journalistic and commercial label. What these wines had in common was ambition: lower yields, rigorous grape selection, ageing in new or nearly new barrels and a style designed to compete with the great international wines.

The legal paradox

The situation was paradoxical: some of Italy's most expensive and prestigious wines sat in the table-wine category, alongside the most ordinary ones. This anomaly exposed the limits of the designation system and contributed to its reform.

In 1992 a new Italian wine law created the category of Indicazione Geografica Tipica (IGT), between table wine and DOC, which allowed origin and varieties to be stated under more flexible rules. Many Super Tuscans began to be sold as IGT Toscana. At the same time the Bolgheri DOC was created, taking in the area's Bordeaux-style reds, and later Sassicaia obtained a designation of its own, Bolgheri Sassicaia, an exceptional case of a DOC dedicated to a single estate.

Changes in Chianti Classico

The success of the Super Tuscans also drove changes in the designations themselves. The Chianti Classico rules were revised to allow wines with a higher proportion of Sangiovese or even pure Sangiovese, and to authorise a percentage of other red varieties, including international ones. In time, white grapes were no longer permitted in Chianti Classico. In this way, part of what had begun as a transgression was absorbed into the system.

Debate and legacy

The Super Tuscan phenomenon had its critics. Some regretted the spread of international varieties and new oak at the expense of Tuscan identity, and pointed to the risk of a uniform style designed for the international market. In recent decades many producers have shifted the emphasis back to Sangiovese and local expression, and moderated their use of barriques.

Even so, the Super Tuscans changed how the world saw Italian wine. They showed that Tuscany could produce wines of the highest international standing, attracted investment and turned areas such as Bolgheri, once little known, into benchmarks. Their story is an example of how the initiative of a few producers can force a regulatory system to evolve.
