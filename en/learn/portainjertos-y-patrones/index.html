Rootstocks: the roots of the modern vineyard

Why almost every vine in the world grows on American roots, how a rootstock is chosen and what role it plays in vigour, resistance and grape quality.

When you look at a Tempranillo, Chardonnay or Nebbiolo vine, you are seeing only part of the plant. In the great majority of the world's vineyards, the part above ground —trunk, arms, leaves and bunches— belongs to the European species Vitis vinifera, but the roots come from a different plant altogether: a rootstock, usually derived from American vine species. This union of two plants in one is one of the quiet foundations of modern viticulture.

An origin tied to phylloxera

The widespread use of rootstocks began as a response to phylloxera, the aphid-like insect from North America that devastated European vineyards in the second half of the nineteenth century. The insect feeds on vine roots, and those of Vitis vinifera have no defence against it: the wounds it causes become infected, the root rots and the plant dies within a few years. American vines, by contrast, evolved alongside the insect, and their roots tolerate its feeding, healing the lesions without serious damage.

After trying countless remedies without success, growers realised that the most effective solution was to graft European varieties onto American roots. This preserved the quality of vinifera grapes while producing a plant able to survive in infested soils. Ever since, most of the world's vineyards have been planted with grafted vines.

The parent species

Three American species are used most often in breeding rootstocks:

  • Vitis riparia, a plant of damp riverbanks with shallow roots, which tends to advance the growing cycle and give vines of moderate vigour.
  • Vitis rupestris, from stony ground, with deep roots and great vigour.
  • Vitis berlandieri, native to the limestone soils of Texas, prized for its tolerance of lime, although difficult to root on its own.

Since none of them combines all the desirable qualities, breeders in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries crossed them with each other, and sometimes with vinifera, to create hybrids suited to each situation. Many of today's rootstocks bear the name of their breeder followed by a number: 110 Richter, 1103 Paulsen and 140 Ruggeri (crosses of berlandieri and rupestris), SO4 and 161-49 Couderc (from berlandieri and riparia), 3309 Couderc (from riparia and rupestris), or 41 B, which combines berlandieri with the European variety Chasselas.

How a rootstock is chosen

There is no universal rootstock. The choice depends above all on soil and climate, and also on the variety and the style of wine to be made. The main criteria are:

  • Phylloxera resistance: the basic requirement. Hybrids with a large share of vinifera parentage may be vulnerable.
  • Tolerance of active lime: on very calcareous soils, some vines cannot absorb iron properly and suffer from chlorosis, with yellowing leaves and loss of vigour. Rootstocks with berlandieri in their background cope better.
  • Drought resistance: deep-rooted rootstocks, such as those derived from rupestris and berlandieri, perform better in hot, dry-farmed vineyards.
  • Vigour conferred: a vigorous rootstock produces more wood and leaves, which can help on poor soils but is counterproductive on fertile ones, where it encourages high yields and uneven ripening.
  • Effect on the cycle: some rootstocks advance ripening and others delay it, an important factor in cool climates or in areas at risk of autumn rain.
  • Other factors: tolerance of salinity, of waterlogged soils or of nematodes, tiny worms that damage roots and can transmit viruses.

Grafting in practice

There are two main ways to produce a grafted vine. In bench grafting, carried out in a nursery, a length of rootstock cane is joined to a bud of the fruiting variety with a cut that locks the two pieces together, such as the omega graft; the union is then encouraged to callus in warm, humid rooms, and the young plant spends a year in the nursery before going to the vineyard. In field grafting, the grower first plants the rootstock and, once it has rooted, grafts the chosen variety onto it in the vineyard itself, using either a scion or a single bud. This more laborious method is still used in many areas and allows the variety to be chosen at leisure.

The quality of the union is essential: a poor graft restricts the flow of sap and shortens the life of the vine.

A lesson in caution

The history of rootstocks also offers warnings. In California, a rootstock called AXR1, a cross of vinifera and rupestris, was planted for decades because of its vigour and productivity. Its resistance to phylloxera proved inadequate, and at the end of the twentieth century a fresh wave of the insect forced growers to replant much of the Napa Valley and other areas. The episode was a reminder that a rootstock's resistance must be robust and lasting.

Ungrafted vineyards

Some vineyards are still planted on their own roots, with vinifera growing ungrafted. They are usually found on very sandy soils, where phylloxera cannot move about or survive, or in isolated territories the insect never reached. The Canary Islands, much of Chile and some sandy plots in Portugal, Australia and other countries still have ungrafted vines, some of them very old.

It is often debated whether wine from ungrafted vines is different or better. Some producers claim it expresses the character of the variety more purely, but it is hard to separate that effect from the age of the vines, the soil or the climate. What is certain is that the rootstock influences vigour, yield, the balance of the plant and the timing of ripening, and therefore, indirectly, the wine.

Current challenges

Climate change has revived interest in rootstocks. Research is looking for rootstocks that are more resistant to drought and salinity, able to delay ripening in warming climates or to cope better with new soil-borne health problems. Choosing the right rootstock, a decision that will stay with the vineyard for decades, remains one of the most important steps in planting a vineyard.
