Alcoholic fermentation and yeasts

How yeasts turn grape sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide, which species are involved and why fermentation shapes so much of a wine's character.

Without fermentation there is no wine. Grape must is a sweet, cloudy liquid that only becomes wine when microorganisms called yeasts consume its sugars and convert them into ethyl alcohol and carbon dioxide. This process, alcoholic fermentation, lies at the heart of winemaking. It determines the alcohol level, a large part of the aroma and texture and, to a great extent, the stability of the finished wine.

What happens in the tank

Ripe grapes contain mainly two sugars, glucose and fructose, in roughly similar proportions. Yeasts take them into their cells and, through a chain of reactions known as glycolysis, break them down into pyruvate. In the absence of oxygen, pyruvate is converted into acetaldehyde and then into ethanol, releasing carbon dioxide. Put very simply, each six-carbon sugar molecule yields two molecules of ethanol and two of carbon dioxide. For the yeast this pathway is a way of obtaining energy; for us, the result is wine.

The reaction gives off heat, so the must warms up by itself. Temperature control is therefore one of the winemaker's main jobs. White and rosé wines are usually fermented cool to preserve delicate aromas, while reds ferment warmer, which helps extract colour and tannins from the skins. If the temperature climbs too high, the yeasts become stressed and fermentation may stop.

As a rule of thumb, roughly 16 to 18 grams of sugar per litre are needed to produce one degree of alcohol by volume; the exact figure varies with the yeast strain and conditions. A very sugar-rich must will therefore give a more alcoholic wine, unless fermentation stops and leaves residual sugar, as happens in many sweet wines.

Much more than alcohol

Alongside ethanol, yeasts produce a long list of secondary compounds that have a decisive influence on the wine. Glycerol adds a certain smoothness. Esters, formed from alcohols and acids, are responsible for many fruity and floral notes, especially in young wines. Higher alcohols add complexity in small amounts but become heavy in excess. Small quantities of acetic acid, succinic acid and acetaldehyde are also formed.

Yeasts also release aromas that were present in the grape as odourless precursors. This is the case with certain varietal thiols typical of Sauvignon Blanc and the bound terpenes of the Muscat family. That is why fermentation is often said to reveal the grape variety.

Saccharomyces and the others

The main protagonist is Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the same species used in baking and brewing, although with strains adapted to wine. Its advantage is its tolerance of alcohol and acidic conditions: it can finish fermentation when other species can no longer survive.

At the start, however, the must hosts a varied community of so-called non-Saccharomyces yeasts that come from the grape skins and the winery, including genera such as Hanseniaspora, Metschnikowia, Torulaspora and Pichia. They tend to dominate the early stages and gradually disappear as alcohol rises. Their role can be positive, adding aromatic complexity, or negative, if they produce too much volatile acidity. Some of them are now sold commercially for controlled use alongside Saccharomyces.

Native or selected yeasts

Winemakers can let fermentation start spontaneously, with the yeasts naturally present on the grapes and in the cellar, or they can inoculate selected yeasts, usually in the form of active dry yeast. Spontaneous fermentation is sometimes associated with a stronger sense of place and greater complexity, but it carries a higher risk of stuck or deviant fermentations. Inoculation provides a quick, predictable start and allows strains to be chosen for specific traits: good alcohol tolerance, low production of sulphur compounds or the ability to release particular aromas. Both options are legitimate and are used in wineries at every level.

When fermentation goes wrong

Yeasts need more than sugar. They require assimilable nitrogen, vitamins, minerals and, in the early stages, a little oxygen to build healthy membranes. A nitrogen-poor must can lead to sluggish fermentations or to the production of hydrogen sulphide, with its smell of rotten eggs. Extreme temperatures, very high potential alcohol or residues of vineyard treatments can also slow the process. A stuck fermentation, with unconsumed residual sugar, is a serious risk because it leaves the wine exposed to spoilage bacteria and yeasts.

To prevent this, winemakers measure the density of the must every day, control temperature, add nutrients when needed and, for reds, manage the cap of skins with pump-overs or punch-downs, which also lightly aerate the must.

A little history

Although humans have made wine for thousands of years, the nature of fermentation remained a mystery until the nineteenth century. Louis Pasteur showed that it was a process carried out by living organisms and studied wine spoilage in depth. At the end of that century, Eduard Buchner demonstrated that a cell-free yeast extract could ferment sugar, opening the way to the biochemistry of enzymes.

Special fermentations

Alcoholic fermentation takes particular forms in some styles. In traditional-method sparkling wines, a second fermentation is triggered in the bottle itself to trap the carbon dioxide. In biologically aged wines such as fino and manzanilla, certain yeast strains form a film on the surface of the wine called flor, which protects it from oxygen and transforms its aromatic profile. In naturally sweet and fortified wines, fermentation stops because of the sheer richness in sugar or because grape spirit is added.

Understanding fermentation helps explain why two wines from the same grape can be so different: yeast strain, temperature and nutrition are cellar decisions every bit as important as the choice of vineyard.
