Primary, secondary and tertiary aromas

The classic classification of wine aromas by origin —the grape, winemaking and ageing— and how to use it to describe and understand what you perceive in the glass.

A wine may contain hundreds of volatile compounds, of which only some can be perceived by smell. To bring order to this complexity, tasters usually group aromas into three families according to their origin: primary aromas, which come from the grape; secondary aromas, linked to winemaking; and tertiary aromas, which appear with ageing. The classification is a simplification, but it is very useful for describing a wine and working out how it was made.

A classification with nuances

It is worth knowing that not all authors divide aromas in the same way. In the French and Spanish winemaking tradition, secondary aromas are mainly those born during fermentation, while oak ageing and development in bottle form the tertiary group, also known as the bouquet. Other tasting systems, widely used in international wine education, include the aromas of alcoholic fermentation among the primary ones and reserve the secondary group for later techniques such as malolactic fermentation, lees contact or oak. What matters is not the label but understanding where each note comes from.

Primary aromas: the imprint of the grape

Primary or varietal aromas derive from compounds present in the grape. Some can be detected simply by biting a berry, but many exist as odourless precursors that fermentation releases. They are what allow many varieties to be recognised with practice:

  • Terpenes, responsible for the floral, fresh-grape notes of the Muscats, of Gewürztraminer and, to a lesser extent, of Riesling.
  • Methoxypyrazines, which give green notes of green pepper or freshly cut grass, typical of Sauvignon Blanc and the Cabernet family when the fruit is not fully ripe.
  • Volatile thiols, associated with aromas of grapefruit, passion fruit or boxwood in some whites, such as Sauvignon Blanc from certain regions.
  • Rotundone, the black pepper molecule, found in some cool-climate Syrah and in other varieties.

To these are added more general fruit and floral notes: citrus, stone or tropical fruit in whites; red or black fruit in reds; violet, rose or white flowers. The ripeness of the grape changes their character considerably: the same variety can smell of fresh, crisp fruit in a cool climate or of ripe, almost stewed fruit in a warm one.

Secondary aromas: the imprint of winemaking

During fermentation, yeasts do not merely turn sugar into alcohol: they also produce a wide range of aromatic compounds. Esters provide fruity notes, such as the banana or pear found in many young wines; higher alcohols, in small amounts, contribute to complexity. Fermentation temperature and yeast strain both influence this profile.

Other techniques also leave their mark:

  • Malolactic fermentation can generate diacetyl, with its characteristic aroma of butter or cream.
  • Lees contact, with the dead yeast cells, adds notes of bread, dough, brioche or nuts, very evident in traditional-method sparkling wines.
  • Carbonic maceration gives highly expressive red fruit aromas, sometimes with hints of banana or bubblegum.
  • Biological ageing under a veil of flor yeast, as in fino sherry, produces acetaldehyde and pungent notes of almond and dough.

Oak deserves a separate mention. Depending on the classification system, its contributions —vanilla, coconut, toast, sweet spices, smoke— are considered secondary or tertiary. Either way, these are aromas linked to winemaking rather than to the grape, and their intensity depends on the type of oak, the level of toast and the proportion of new barrels.

Tertiary aromas: the imprint of time

With ageing, the fruity aromas of youth slowly change. Some esters break down, other compounds form, and the wine gains in complexity what it loses in exuberance. Two main paths can be distinguished:

  • Reductive development, in bottle and with hardly any oxygen, which brings notes of forest floor, leather, tobacco, truffle, mushroom or damp earth in reds, and of honey, wax, nuts or, in Riesling, a characteristic hint of petrol in whites.
  • Oxidative development, typical of wines aged in contact with air, such as oloroso, Madeira or tawny Port, which show aromas of walnut, caramel, coffee, toffee or dried fruit.

In well-kept wines, these aromas blend with the fruit rather than replacing it entirely. When the fruit has disappeared completely and dull or flat notes dominate, the wine has usually passed its best.

How to use the classification when tasting

When smelling a wine, it can help to ask three questions in order. What does the grape contribute? What traces has the winery left? Are there signs of the passage of time? Answering them helps to organise one's impressions and to form hypotheses about the variety, style, region or age of the wine.

Bear in mind that descriptors are analogies: saying that a wine smells of raspberry does not mean it contains raspberry, but that it shares certain compounds or a similar impression with it. Olfactory memory is trained by smelling fruits, flowers, spices and other everyday products attentively, and by comparing wines from different origins. With practice, the three-family classification stops being a theory and becomes a map for reading the story of each wine.
