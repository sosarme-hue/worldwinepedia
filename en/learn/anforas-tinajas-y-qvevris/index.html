Amphorae, tinajas and qvevri

Clay vessels have accompanied wine since its beginnings. How they affect fermentation and ageing, which traditions have survived in Georgia, Spain and Portugal, and why they have returned to many modern wineries.

Long before wooden barrels or steel tanks existed, wine was made and stored in clay vessels. Archaeological finds from the Caucasus, the Near East and the Mediterranean show that pottery was for millennia the essential material of wine, used to ferment, store and transport it. Although wood, concrete and steel displaced it in most of the world, some traditions never abandoned it, and in recent decades many winemakers have taken a renewed interest in these vessels. Under different names, such as amphorae, tinajas, qvevri or talhas, they share a single principle: wine in contact with clay.

What clay contributes

Fired clay has properties that set it apart from other materials. If it is unlined, it is slightly porous and allows a very slow exchange of oxygen with the outside, comparable in some ways to that of a used barrel, but without imparting oak aromas or tannins. For this reason many winemakers regard it as an aromatically neutral vessel that respects the character of the fruit while allowing a certain development.

Clay also has good thermal inertia: its thick walls cushion changes in temperature, especially when the vessels are fully or partly buried. And the shape of many of them, with a broad belly tapering towards the base, encourages natural circulation of the wine during fermentation and the collection of solids at the bottom.

Porosity also creates difficulties. An unlined vessel can absorb wine and lead to losses through evaporation, and cleaning it is laborious. That is why many traditions have coated the interior with beeswax, resins or pitch, and some modern vessels are made from denser clays or with treatments that reduce porosity.

The Georgian qvevri

The most famous example of this continuity is the Georgian qvevri, a large egg-shaped vessel buried up to its neck in the floor of the cellar. Its interior is traditionally waterproofed with beeswax. Traditional qvevri winemaking has been inscribed by UNESCO on its list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

In the traditional Georgian method, especially in Kakheti, crushed grapes are placed in the qvevri with their skins and often some of their stems. After fermentation, the vessel is sealed and the wine remains in contact with the solids for months. The solids settle in the pointed base, and the wine is later racked into another vessel. White grapes such as Rkatsiteli produce amber, tannic wines in this way; red grapes such as Saperavi, deeply coloured wines. In the western region of Imereti, tradition favours shorter skin contact.

Spanish tinajas

In Spain, the clay tinaja was for centuries the usual vessel in large areas of the centre and south. In La Mancha and Valdepeñas, traditional wineries kept rows of large tinajas, sometimes partly buried, in which wine from varieties such as Airén or Tempranillo, known locally as Cencibel, was fermented and stored. Some towns in La Mancha and around Madrid were pottery centres specialising in making them.

In Montilla-Moriles, tinajas, first of clay and later also of concrete, have traditionally been used to ferment Pedro Ximénez must before the wine moves on to ageing. During the second half of the twentieth century many wineries replaced clay tinajas with concrete or steel tanks, which are easier to clean and control, but in recent years several producers have revived their use.

Portuguese vinho de talha

In the Alentejo, in southern Portugal, the tradition of making wine in large clay vessels called talhas has continued uninterrupted in some villages. The inside of the talhas is usually coated with a layer of pitch or resin. The grapes ferment in them with their skins, which form a cap that is pushed down by hand, and the wine is drawn off through a hole near the base, passing through the settled skins, which act as a natural filter. The Alentejo denomination of origin recognises the term vinho de talha for these wines.

From antiquity to the present

The Greeks and Romans used large clay vessels, such as the Roman dolium, to ferment and store wine, and smaller amphorae to transport it around the Mediterranean. The word amphora is used today generically for any clay vessel used in winemaking, although strictly speaking it referred to a two-handled transport jar.

Since the late twentieth century interest in clay vessels has revived in many countries. Some producers in Friuli and Slovenia travelled to Georgia and adopted qvevri; others have commissioned amphorae from Italian, Spanish or Portuguese potters. Alongside traditional terracotta, vessels are now made of stoneware, denser ceramics or other mineral materials, and egg-shaped concrete tanks share part of their philosophy.

Wines made in clay

There is no single style of amphora wine. The vessels can be used for fermenting with or without skins, for ageing wines that have already fermented, or for both, and with white or red grapes. In general, winemakers who use them are looking for a pure expression of fruit, a particular texture and slow development without the aromatic imprint of oak. Many are associated with minimal-intervention practices, but the vessel itself does not determine the winemaking philosophy. What all these traditions share is the continuity of a very ancient gesture: entrusting wine to fired earth.
