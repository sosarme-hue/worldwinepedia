Alcohol-free and dealcoholised wines

What dealcoholised and partially dealcoholised wines are, how they are made, what European rules say about their labelling and how they differ from conventional wine.

Alcohol-free and reduced-alcohol wines have gone from being a marginal product to occupying a space of their own in shops and restaurants. They meet the demand of people who wish to enjoy the ritual and flavours associated with wine without drinking alcohol, or while drinking less: drivers, people who do not drink for personal, religious or health reasons, or simply those who prefer to moderate their intake on certain occasions.

What a dealcoholised wine is

A dealcoholised wine is, first and foremost, a wine: it is made by fermenting grape must like any other, and only afterwards is the alcohol wholly or partly removed. This distinguishes it from grape juice, which has never fermented, and from flavoured drinks that imitate the taste of wine using other ingredients.

European rules

For a long time, European legislation did not allow an alcohol-free product to be called “wine”. A reform of the common market organisation approved in 2021 introduced two new categories:

  • Dealcoholised wine: when the actual alcoholic strength by volume does not exceed 0.5%.
  • Partially dealcoholised wine: when the strength is above 0.5% but below the minimum required for the original wine category.

The relevant term must appear on the label alongside the product's name. The rules also regulate the permitted processes and prohibit, for example, increasing the sugar content of the must in order to offset the later removal of alcohol. Some national laws, within and outside the European Union, also reserve expressions such as “alcohol-free” or “0.0” for products with an even lower alcohol content, so it is always worth reading the label to know the exact strength.

How the alcohol is removed

The technical challenge lies in separating the alcohol from the wine's other components, especially the aromas, which are volatile like alcohol itself. The main techniques are:

  • Vacuum distillation: by lowering the pressure, alcohol evaporates at a much lower temperature than normal, limiting heat damage to the aromas.
  • Spinning cone column: a type of vacuum distillation in which the wine is spread in very thin layers over rotating cones, while a stream of vapour first strips off the aromas, which are collected separately, and then the alcohol. The aromas are added back at the end.
  • Reverse osmosis and other membrane techniques: the wine is forced under pressure through membranes that separate a fraction of water and alcohol; the alcohol is removed from that fraction and the water returned to the wine.

These processes require expensive equipment, so many wineries use specialist companies to dealcoholise their wines.

Differences in the glass

Alcohol is not simply a neutral component of wine. It contributes body, a sensation of warmth and some sweetness, carries the aromas and balances acidity and tannins. When it is removed, the wine tends to seem lighter and thinner, acidity is more pronounced and the tannins of reds can feel drier and harsher. Some aromas are also lost during the process.

To compensate, some producers add grape must or sugar after dealcoholisation, within the limits the rules allow, which gives many of these wines a perceptible sweetness. Others add flavourings, carbon dioxide or other authorised ingredients, which must be listed on the label. For this reason, the styles that best withstand the removal of alcohol are usually sparkling wines, where the bubbles bring freshness and texture, and aromatic whites and rosés. Structured reds are the hardest to bring into balance.

Storage and service

Alcohol contributes to a wine's stability, so dealcoholised wines are more susceptible to microbial spoilage once opened. They usually carry a best-before date and should be kept in the refrigerator after opening and consumed within a few days, following the producer's instructions. They are generally served chilled, in the same type of glass as their alcoholic counterpart, and can accompany the same dishes, bearing in mind their lighter body and possible sweetness.

Points to bear in mind

A dealcoholised wine at 0.5% contains a very small, but not zero, amount of ethanol. People who must avoid alcohol entirely, for example for medical reasons, during pregnancy or because they are taking certain medicines, should check the label and, if in doubt, consult a health professional. Nor are these products intended for minors: they reproduce the ritual of wine drinking, and in many places their sale is subject to specific rules.

Partially dealcoholised wines, for their part, still contain a significant proportion of alcohol, though less than usual, and should be treated in every respect as alcoholic drinks, including where driving is concerned.

A category in evolution

The quality of these wines has improved as techniques have been refined and experienced wineries have become involved, and some regions are studying the possibility of producing dealcoholised or partially dealcoholised versions within their designations of origin. It is still a young category, whose rules and styles continue to evolve, and it offers an alternative to those who wish to share the table without drinking alcohol.
