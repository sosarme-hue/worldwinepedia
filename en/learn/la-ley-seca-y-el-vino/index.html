Prohibition and wine

What the ban on alcohol in the United States from 1920 to 1933 meant for growers and wineries, how some survived and what traces it left, along with other prohibitionist episodes.

Between 1920 and 1933 the United States banned the manufacture, sale and transport of alcoholic beverages throughout its territory. This period, known as Prohibition, is remembered mainly for smuggling and organised crime, but it also had deep and lasting consequences for American wine, which was only just beginning to establish itself. The American case was not unique: other countries applied similar measures in the twentieth century, with equally notable effects on their vineyards.

The roots of the movement

The temperance movement in the United States had roots in the nineteenth century. Religious and civic associations, in which women played a prominent role, denounced the effects of alcohol on health, the family and social order. By the early twentieth century many states had already adopted their own dry laws. The climate of the First World War, with demands to save grain and some suspicion of the German-origin brewing industry, eased the way to nationwide prohibition.

The Eighteenth Amendment to the Constitution was ratified in 1919 and came into force in January 1920. The law implementing it, known as the Volstead Act, defined what counted as an intoxicating beverage and set out the exceptions.

A blow to a young industry

By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, California had become the centre of a growing wine industry, with wineries in areas such as Napa, Sonoma and the Central Valley, many founded by European immigrants. There were also wineries in the East, for example in New York State, in the Finger Lakes region. Prohibition forced most of them to close. Many vineyards were uprooted or converted to other crops, and knowledge, equipment and a generation of professionals were lost.

The exceptions and how some survived

The law provided for some exceptions that allowed part of the industry to carry on:

  • Sacramental wine: producing wine for religious use was permitted. Some wineries, such as Beaulieu Vineyard in Napa, survived by supplying wine for Mass and religious ceremonies.
  • Medicinal use: certain alcoholic products could be obtained on a doctor's prescription, a route that led to widespread abuse.
  • Home production: the law allowed heads of household to make a limited quantity of fruit juice at home for their own use. This ambiguous provision was in practice interpreted as a way to make wine at home.

The third exception had a paradoxical effect: demand for grapes for home winemaking soared. Californian growers shipped grapes by rail to cities in the East and Midwest, where Italian, Jewish and other immigrant communities bought them to make their wine. Thick-skinned, deeply coloured varieties that travelled well and yielded plenty of wine were preferred, such as Alicante Bouschet, even if they were not the finest. Concentrates and blocks of dehydrated grape must were also sold, whose labels warned, almost ironically, that they might ferment if dissolved in water.

Consequences for quality

Paradoxically, in some years of Prohibition the vineyard area in California actually grew, but with varieties chosen for shipping and volume. Many plantings of fine varieties were replaced. When the law was repealed, the industry lacked equipped wineries, trained winemakers and a public used to quality table wines.

Repeal and its legacy

The Twenty-first Amendment, ratified in December 1933, repealed national Prohibition but left the regulation of alcohol to each state. This had consequences that endure:

  • Many states established a three-tier distribution system separating producers, distributors and retailers.
  • Some states kept public sales monopolies, and there are still counties where the sale of alcohol is restricted or banned.
  • Rules on direct interstate shipping have been the subject of litigation for decades.

In the decades after repeal, the American wine market was dominated by cheap sweet and fortified wines. The recovery of quality was slow and gathered pace only from the 1960s and 1970s, with new wineries, research at university centres such as Davis and, later, international recognition for Californian wines.

Other prohibitionist episodes

In the early decades of the twentieth century, several countries imposed severe restrictions on alcohol. The Russian Empire banned the sale of vodka from 1914. Finland maintained general prohibition from 1919 to 1932. Norway and Iceland restricted stronger drinks for years, and Iceland did not allow full-strength beer again until 1989. In Canada, several provinces adopted dry laws during and after the First World War, though most soon abandoned them.

A late case of great relevance to wine was the anti-alcohol campaign launched in the Soviet Union in 1985. Its measures included uprooting vineyards in wine-producing republics such as Moldova, Georgia and Ukraine, including Crimea. Some varieties and old vineyards disappeared, and these regions took years to recover.

A historical lesson

Prohibitions responded to real concerns about the harm caused by excessive drinking, which remains a public health issue. Yet the American experience is often cited as an example of the difficulties of a total ban: drinking did not disappear, it moved underground and fed criminal networks. For wine, the episode was a rupture that took half a century to overcome.
