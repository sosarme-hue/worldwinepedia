Sweet wines: production methods

Late harvest, drying, freezing the grapes, noble rot and fortification: the different ways of concentrating sugar or keeping it in the wine, and the great styles that result from each.

For a wine to be naturally sweet, it must retain some of the grape's sugar without the yeasts turning it into alcohol. With grapes of normal ripeness, fermentation consumes almost all the sugar and produces a dry wine. Sweet winemaking therefore relies on two strategies, which are often combined: concentrating the sugar in the grapes to levels the yeasts cannot exhaust, or stopping fermentation before they have finished their work. These approaches give rise to very different styles, from light and aromatic to dense and long-lived.

Late harvest

The simplest method is to delay the harvest and leave the grapes on the vine beyond their usual ripeness. As the weeks go by, the berries continue to accumulate sugar and begin to lose water, especially if the autumn is dry and sunny. The result is richer juice, which yields wines with higher alcohol, more residual sugar or both.

Late-harvest wines are traditional in regions such as Alsace, where the designation Vendanges Tardives refers to wines from overripe grapes, and Germany, whose Prädikat categories reflect different levels of ripeness at harvest. Riesling, Gewürztraminer, Chenin Blanc and the Muscats are especially well suited to this method because of their acidity or aromatic richness. The risk is climatic: autumn rain can bring rot or dilute the grapes, and birds and other animals help themselves to the ripe bunches.

Drying the grapes

Drying aims to concentrate sugar by dehydrating the grapes. It can take place on the vine, for example by twisting the stalk of the bunch to cut off the flow of sap, or after harvest. In the latter case there are many different traditions:

  • In the sun: in southern Spain, Pedro Ximénez and Muscat grapes are spread out on mats or cloths in the open air, a process called soleo, until they have practically turned into raisins. This is how the sweet wines of Montilla-Moriles, Jerez and Málaga are made.
  • In the shade or in ventilated rooms: in Italy, appassimento takes place in lofts or airy rooms, with bunches hung up or laid on racks or in crates for weeks or months. It is the basis of wines such as Recioto della Valpolicella, made mainly from Corvina, and Tuscan Vin Santo. On Pantelleria, highly regarded wines are made from dried Muscat grapes.
  • On straw: vin de paille, traditional in the Jura and some parts of the Rhône, takes its name from the beds of straw on which the grapes were dried.

On the Greek island of Santorini, Vinsanto is made from sun-dried grapes, mainly Assyrtiko. Wines from dried grapes are usually very dense, with aromas of raisins, figs, dates, honey or nuts, and some are aged for years in barrel.

Ice wine

Ice wine, Eiswein in German and icewine in Canada, is made from grapes left on the vine until a hard frost freezes them naturally. They are picked at sub-zero temperatures, often at night, and pressed while still frozen. Because the water stays in the berry as ice crystals, only a small amount of juice flows out, highly concentrated in sugar and acidity.

The regulations of producing countries set a maximum temperature for harvesting and prohibit artificial freezing for wines bearing this name. Germany and Austria have a long tradition, and Canada, especially the Niagara Peninsula in Ontario, has become one of the leading producers, using varieties such as Riesling and the hybrid Vidal. Ice wines stand out for their pure fruit and very lively acidity, which balances their sweetness. In other regions cryoextraction is practised, freezing the grapes in cold rooms, but such wines may not be called ice wine.

Noble rot

Under certain weather conditions, the fungus Botrytis cinerea attacks ripe grapes without destroying them, dehydrating them while altering their composition and aromas. This noble rot lies behind wines such as Sauternes, Tokaji Aszú and the German and Austrian Beerenauslese and Trockenbeerenauslese. Their production requires harvesting in successive passes and very specific climatic conditions, which is why these wines are scarce.

Fortification

The second major strategy is to stop fermentation by adding grape spirit. When the alcohol reaches a level at which yeasts cannot survive, fermentation stops and the sugar not yet converted remains in the wine. The earlier the spirit is added, the sweeter the wine.

This is the method used for Port, which is fortified a few days after fermentation begins, following an intense maceration on the skins to extract colour and tannin. In southern France, vins doux naturels, such as those made from Muscat or the Grenache-based wines of Roussillon, are made in the same way; there the process is called mutage. Madeira and many Málaga wines are also fortified. For certain Andalusian Pedro Ximénez wines, the juice of dried grapes is fortified almost before it has fermented, producing extraordinarily sweet wines.

Other methods

For the simplest sweet wines, fermentation is stopped by chilling, adding sulphur dioxide and filtering, with no need to concentrate the grapes. Where the law allows, concentrated or unfermented grape must may also be added to a dry wine. These methods produce medium-sweet or sweet wines in a light style, intended for drinking young.

The balance of sweet wines

Whatever the method, the quality of a sweet wine depends on its balance. Too much sugar without enough acidity produces heavy, cloying wines, which is why varieties with good natural acidity are so prized for these styles. Great sweet wines combine concentration, freshness and aromatic complexity, and many of them rank among the longest-lived wines in existence. Like any wine, they should be enjoyed in moderation; at the table they are usually served in small quantities, with desserts or cheese, or simply on their own.
