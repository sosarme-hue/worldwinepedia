Barrel ageing and types of oak

Why wine is aged in oak barrels, what the wood contributes depending on its species, origin, toast and age, and how vessel size and length of ageing make a difference.

For centuries the wooden barrel was above all a transport container: sturdy, relatively light and easy to roll. In time it became clear that wine which spent a period in it changed, and in many cases for the better. Today barrel ageing is one of the most powerful tools available to the winemaker. It is not simply a matter of adding woody flavour: the barrel allows a slow, controlled ingress of oxygen, gives the wine aromatic compounds and tannins of its own, and encourages natural clarification and stabilisation. Oak is the dominant wood because of a combination of properties: it is strong, can be bent when heated, is relatively watertight and is rich in pleasant aromatic compounds.

What happens in the barrel

A barrel is not an airtight vessel. Through the wood, the joints between the staves and the bung hole, the wine continuously receives small amounts of oxygen. This natural micro-oxygenation promotes the polymerisation of tannins, which become softer, and stabilises the colour of red wines as tannins and anthocyanins combine. At the same time some of the liquid evaporates through the wood, so the barrels must be topped up periodically to prevent air from filling the empty space and oxidising the wine.

The wood also releases a range of substances into the wine. Among the best known are vanillin, responsible for vanilla notes; oak lactones, whose aromas recall coconut; eugenol, which smells of cloves; and compounds produced by toasting, such as furfural, with notes of caramel or toasted almond, and guaiacol, with a smoky character. Oak also contributes ellagitannins, wood tannins that add structure and help protect against oxidation.

During ageing the wine is racked, that is, transferred from one barrel to another clean one in order to separate it from the sediment that has settled at the bottom. This operation also provides a brief aeration. In white wines aged on their lees, by contrast, racking is usually avoided in order to benefit from contact with the yeast.

Oak species

Three species are mainly used in winemaking. The two European species, Quercus petraea and Quercus robur, grow in France and across much of central and eastern Europe. American oak, Quercus alba, comes chiefly from the eastern United States.

American oak tends to give more intense and obvious aromas of vanilla and coconut, owing to its higher lactone content, and less tannin. French oak tends to offer more subtle, spicy and toasty notes and a finer tannic structure. These differences are general and depend greatly on how the wood is treated, but they explain some historical styles: in Rioja, for example, American oak was traditionally used and shaped the profile of many classic reds, while in Bordeaux and Burgundy French oak has predominated.

Within France, coopers distinguish wood by its forest of origin, such as Allier, Tronçais, Nevers or the Vosges, and above all by the tightness of the grain, that is, the width of the growth rings. Tight grain, typical of slow-growing trees, releases its compounds more gradually. Oak from central and eastern Europe, such as Hungarian oak, is also used and valued for its intermediate character.

From tree to barrel

The quality of a barrel depends largely on the cooper's work. European oak must be split along the grain so that the staves are watertight, which wastes a great deal of wood; American oak, because of its internal structure, can be sawn, which makes better use of the timber. The staves are then seasoned in the open air, usually for two years or more. This natural drying, exposed to rain, sun and the action of fungi, reduces the harshest tannins and bitter compounds.

The staves are assembled and bent with the help of fire and water. The inside of the barrel is then toasted to a varying degree, generally classified as light, medium or heavy. A light toast preserves more of the character of the wood and its tannins; a heavier one creates more toasty, coffee or smoky aromas and reduces the tannin contribution. Some coopers also toast the barrel heads.

Size, age and duration

The size of the vessel is decisive. The Bordeaux barrique holds 225 litres, and the slightly broader Burgundian barrel about 228. The smaller the vessel, the greater the surface area of wood in contact with each litre of wine and the stronger its influence. Large casks and foudres, holding thousands of litres, impart very little oak flavour and are used for more neutral maturation, traditional in many regions of Italy, such as Piedmont for Nebbiolo, as well as in Germany and Alsace.

The age of the barrel is equally important. A new barrel gives off a great deal of aroma and tannin; after several uses its contribution gradually declines until it becomes almost neutral, although it still allows oxygen exchange. Many winemakers combine new and used barrels in different proportions to fine-tune the effect of the oak.

The length of ageing varies widely according to style. Some wines spend only a few months in barrel; others, several years. In Spain, traditional terms such as crianza, reserva and gran reserva are linked to minimum ageing periods, partly in barrel, set by national regulations and by each denomination of origin.

White wines and the barrel

Although oak is associated above all with red wines, it plays an important role for some whites. Barrel fermentation followed by ageing on the lees is traditional in Burgundy for Chardonnay and is used in many other regions. In these cases the lees protect the wine from oxidation and soften the influence of the oak, which integrates more delicately.

Alternatives and balance

For economic reasons, some wineries use alternatives such as oak chips or staves placed in steel tanks, practices authorised in the European Union within certain limits. They impart oak aromas quickly but do not reproduce the barrel's oxygen exchange in the same way.

In recent decades many winemakers have reduced their use of new oak in search of wines that express fruit and origin more clearly. The barrel, in short, is an instrument to be used with restraint: used well, it brings complexity, structure and longevity; used to excess, it can mask the character of the grape and of the place it comes from.
