Residual sugar and sweetness levels

The sugar left in a wine after fermentation determines whether it is dry, medium or sweet. How it is retained, how it is perceived and what label terms mean for still and sparkling wines.

Residual sugar is the sugar that remains in a wine once fermentation is complete. In principle, yeasts convert almost all the sugar in the must into alcohol, and the result is a dry wine. But for natural reasons or by the winemaker's choice, some of that sugar may remain unfermented. Its quantity, usually expressed in grams per litre, is the main criterion for classifying wines by sweetness, although the impression of sweetness perceived by a taster also depends on other factors.

The sugars in grapes

The sugars in ripe grapes are almost exclusively glucose and fructose, in roughly equal proportions. Yeasts consume both but tend to prefer glucose, so in wines with residual sugar fructose, which tastes sweeter, often predominates. Even in wines considered dry, there is almost always a small amount of sugar that yeasts cannot ferment, generally one or two grams per litre.

How sugar is left in the wine

There are several ways in which a wine can retain residual sugar:

  • Natural arrest of fermentation: when the must is very rich in sugar, as with grapes affected by noble rot or with dried grapes, the alcohol produced and the sugar concentration itself eventually inhibit the yeasts, which stop working before the sugar is used up.
  • Deliberate arrest: the winemaker can halt fermentation by chilling, adding sulphur dioxide and filtering to remove the yeasts. This is the usual method for many medium-dry and medium-sweet wines.
  • Fortification: adding grape spirit during fermentation, as in Port or certain vins doux naturels of southern France, raises the alcohol to a level at which yeasts cannot survive, and the remaining sugar stays in the wine.
  • Later addition: in some cases grape must or sugar is added to the finished wine, as permitted by law. In traditional-method sparkling wines, the liqueur d'expédition added after disgorgement sets the final sweetness level.

Perceiving sweetness

The amount of sugar alone is not enough to predict how a wine will taste. Acidity counteracts sweetness: a German Riesling with a fair amount of sugar may seem less sweet than it is thanks to its high acidity, while a wine with the same amount of sugar and little acidity may be cloying. Alcohol and glycerol give a sensation of smoothness and a certain sweetness even in dry wines. Tannins and bitterness, on the other hand, diminish it. The carbon dioxide in sparkling wines also changes perception, which is why a brut sparkling wine with several grams of sugar tastes dry.

Aromas play a part too. Notes of very ripe fruit, honey or vanilla from oak are mentally associated with sweetness and can make a dry wine seem slightly sweet. Tasters therefore distinguish between actual sweetness, measurable in the laboratory, and the impression of sweetness.

Categories for still wines

European Union regulations define several terms for still wines, according to residual sugar and, in some cases, total acidity:

  • Dry: up to 4 grams per litre, or up to 9 grams if total acidity is sufficiently high in relation to sugar.
  • Medium dry: above the limit for dry and up to 12 grams per litre, or up to 18 grams under the same acidity condition.
  • Medium sweet: above the limit for medium dry and up to 45 grams per litre.
  • Sweet: at least 45 grams per litre.

Some countries also use their own terms. In Germany, for example, trocken and halbtrocken correspond to dry and medium dry, and the word feinherb is used for wines with a slight sweetness. It is worth remembering that the German Prädikat categories, such as Kabinett, Spätlese or Auslese, refer to the ripeness of the grapes at harvest and not to the sweetness of the finished wine: a Spätlese may be dry or sweet.

Categories for sparkling wines

Sparkling wines have a different scale, more detailed at the dry end:

  • Brut nature: less than 3 grams per litre, with no sugar added after the second fermentation.
  • Extra brut: between 0 and 6 grams per litre.
  • Brut: less than 12 grams per litre.
  • Extra dry: between 12 and 17 grams per litre.
  • Dry: between 17 and 32 grams per litre.
  • Demi-sec: between 32 and 50 grams per litre.
  • Doux: more than 50 grams per litre.

This scale can be confusing, because a sparkling wine labelled dry or extra dry is in fact sweeter than a brut. The reason is historical: in the nineteenth century many sparkling wines were made with very high doses of sugar, and the terms were set in comparison with those styles.

Stability of wines with sugar

A wine with residual sugar is more vulnerable than a dry one, because the sugar can feed any yeasts or bacteria that have survived. A refermentation in the bottle would produce gas, cloudiness and unpleasant aromas. To prevent this, such wines are carefully filtered to remove microorganisms, bottled under strict hygienic conditions and usually given somewhat higher doses of sulphur dioxide. Fortified wines, by contrast, are protected by their alcohol content.

A question of balance

Residual sugar is in itself neither a virtue nor a fault. Some of the world's most admired wines are sweet, and their quality depends on the harmony between sugar, acidity and aroma. In other styles, a few grams of sugar serve to soften very pronounced acidity without making the wine taste sweet. Understanding the role of sugar helps in reading labels and choosing the right wine for each occasion, especially when pairing food, where a slight sweetness can go well with spicy or salty dishes.
