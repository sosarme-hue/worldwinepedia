Viticulture

Organic and biodynamic viticulture and climate change

What organic and biodynamic viticulture require, what is known about their effects and how vineyards are adapting to a warmer, more erratic climate.

Viticulture in recent decades has faced two major, related questions: how to grow grapes with less environmental impact, and how to adapt to a rapidly changing climate. The answers range from certified methods such as organic and biodynamic viticulture to profound changes in varieties, planting sites and growing practices.

Organic viticulture

Organic viticulture prohibits synthetic herbicides, fertilisers and pesticides. Soil fertility is maintained with organic matter such as compost or green manures, and weeds are controlled by tillage, mowing or grazing. Fungal diseases are fought mainly with mineral-based products, sulphur against powdery mildew and copper against downy mildew, together with plant extracts and preventive strategies such as good air flow around the bunches.

In the European Union, organic production is governed by common rules with inspection and certification by approved bodies. Since 2012 EU rules have also covered the making of organic wine, not just the growing of the grapes: they restrict the additives and practices allowed and set lower maximum sulphur dioxide levels than for conventional wine. Before then one could only speak of wine made from organically grown grapes.

The method is not without debate. Copper is a metal that accumulates in the soil and can affect its microbial life, so regulations limit the amounts that may be used and research is looking for alternatives. In very wet years, protecting an organic vineyard requires frequent interventions and can lead to significant crop losses.

Biodynamics

Biodynamics stems from the lectures on agriculture given by the Austrian philosopher Rudolf Steiner in 1924. It sees the farm as a living, self-sufficient organism in which crops, animals and biodiversity coexist. It meets the requirements of organic farming and adds its own practices: the use of preparations made from manure, silica or medicinal plants, applied in very small doses to the soil, plants or compost, and the scheduling of work according to a calendar that takes into account the positions of the Moon and planets. The best-known preparations are 500, cow manure fermented in a horn buried over winter, and 501, ground silica.

There are specific private certifications, such as Demeter and Biodyvin, and many prestigious wineries in France, Spain, Italy and elsewhere practise it. From a scientific point of view, however, the picture is nuanced: various studies have observed positive effects on soil life and biodiversity that largely coincide with those of organic farming, but there is no solid evidence that the preparations themselves or the lunar calendar have the effects attributed to them. Many growers stress, in any case, that biodynamics forces them to observe their vineyards more closely.

Other approaches

Between conventional and certified viticulture there are intermediate approaches. Integrated production, or sustainable viticulture, reduces treatments and intervenes only when the risk justifies it, though it allows synthetic products. Regenerative viticulture emphasises soil health, organic carbon and permanent cover crops. Fungus-resistant varieties, often called PIWI, obtained by cross-breeding, allow treatments to be greatly reduced and are beginning to be authorised in some European appellations, although their acceptance is still uneven.

So-called natural wines do not refer to a legally defined growing method but to a movement that, starting from grapes usually grown organically or biodynamically, seeks minimal intervention in the cellar, with native yeasts and little or no added sulphur.

Climate change in the vineyard

The vine is highly sensitive to climate, and the effects of warming can already be seen in many regions. The best documented is the earlier timing of the growth stages, especially harvest, which in many European areas now takes place considerably earlier than in the mid-twentieth century. Ripening during a hotter period tends to produce musts with more sugar, and therefore wines with more alcohol, lower acidity and sometimes a mismatch between sugar ripeness and the ripeness of aromas and tannins.

Added to this are extreme events, more frequent in many areas: heatwaves that scorch bunches, prolonged droughts, hailstorms, torrential rain and, paradoxically, a greater risk of spring frost when budbreak comes earlier. Pests and diseases are also shifting.

Adaptation strategies

Growers are responding on different timescales. In the short term they adjust growing practices: keeping more leaves to shade the grapes, pruning later to delay budbreak, using cover crops to protect the soil, installing shade netting, or harvesting earlier and in several passes. Where it is permitted and water is available, regulated deficit irrigation helps avoid extreme stress.

In the medium and long term they reconsider plant material: more drought-tolerant rootstocks, slower-ripening clones and heat-adapted varieties. In Spain and other countries, minor varieties that were once abandoned and that retain acidity well are being revived. Growers also look for new sites: higher altitude, north-facing slopes or areas closer to the sea. Meanwhile, regions once considered marginal because of their coolness, such as southern England, now produce wines, especially sparkling ones, that have found a place in the market.

Reducing the impact

The industry is also working on mitigation: reducing the weight of glass bottles, which account for a large part of wine's carbon footprint, improving energy efficiency in wineries, promoting carbon storage in the soil and protecting biodiversity. Organic, biodynamic or conventional, the viticulture of the future will have to reconcile quality, profitability and respect for the environment in a climate less predictable than that of the past.
