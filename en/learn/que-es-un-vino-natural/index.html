What is natural wine?

The principles of the natural wine movement, how it differs from organic and biodynamic wine, why it has no general legal definition and what debates it raises.

The term natural wine has become common in bars, shops and restaurant lists, but its meaning is not always clear. Unlike designations of origin or organic farming, in most countries there is no legal definition of natural wine. It is rather a movement and a winemaking philosophy based on the idea of intervening as little as possible, both in the vineyard and in the cellar.

General principles

Although each producer and each association has its own criteria, most agree on some basic principles:

  • Organically or biodynamically grown grapes, without synthetic herbicides, insecticides or fungicides.
  • Hand harvesting, which allows the grapes to be sorted and brought whole to the winery.
  • Fermentation with native yeasts, naturally present on the grapes and in the cellar, without added cultured yeasts.
  • No additives, or as few as possible: no acid adjustment, no added sugar, no enzymes, no tannins or oak chips.
  • No or very low added sulphites. Sulphur dioxide has been used in winemaking for centuries as an antioxidant and antimicrobial; many natural wine makers do without it entirely, while others accept a small dose at bottling.
  • No fining or filtration, or only very light, so the wine may be hazy or have sediment.
  • No aggressive physical techniques, such as reverse osmosis, cryoextraction or flash détente.

It is worth remembering that all wines contain a small amount of sulphites, because yeasts produce them during fermentation. A wine with “no added sulphites” is therefore not necessarily sulphite-free.

How it differs from organic and biodynamic wine

Organic wine is regulated in the European Union: it requires certified organic grapes and limits additives and sulphite levels, though it allows more cellar practices than natural wine does. Biodynamic wine also follows the precepts of biodynamics, with specific preparations and its own farming calendar, and is certified by private organisations. A natural wine usually starts from organic or biodynamic grapes but adds restrictions in the cellar. Conversely, a certified organic wine need not be natural.

Origins of the movement

The idea of making wine without additives is as old as wine itself, but the modern movement is usually traced to France in the second half of the twentieth century. In Beaujolais, the chemist and négociant Jules Chauvet researched fermentation and winemaking without sulphur and influenced a group of local growers, among them Marcel Lapierre in Morgon. From there the trend spread to the Loire, the Jura, the Languedoc and other French regions, and later to Italy, Spain, Georgia, Austria, Australia and many other countries.

The movement has also revived traditional practices, such as fermentation in clay vessels, notably Georgian qvevri, or white wines with prolonged skin maceration, known as orange wines. Another associated style is pét-nat (pétillant naturel), a sparkling wine made by the ancestral method and bottled before the first fermentation has finished.

Attempts at regulation

The lack of an official definition has led various producer associations to draw up their own charters. In France, a winemakers' union created the term “Vin Méthode Nature”, with a specification that requires, among other things, certified organic grapes, hand harvesting, native yeasts and no corrective techniques, and that uses different logos to distinguish wines with no added sulphites from those given a small dose before bottling. Outside such cases, the word “natural” on a label is usually restricted or unregulated, depending on each country's legislation, and many producers prefer not to use it.

Characteristics and debates

There is no single natural wine style: they can be white, red, rosé, orange or sparkling, light or structured. Many share, however, traits such as very direct fruit, moderate alcohol, some haziness and a texture that is sometimes less polished. Supporters emphasise the expression of place and vintage, the absence of oenological make-up and respect for the soil.

Critics point out that the lack of protection against oxygen and microorganisms increases the risk of faults: high volatile acidity, Brettanomyces aromas, the flavour known as “mousiness”, reminiscent of cereal husks or animal fur and noticed mainly on the aftertaste, or refermentation in bottle. They also stress that some wines are unstable and sensitive to heat during transport and storage. Between the two positions, many winemakers advocate minimal but not zero intervention, and numerous conventional wineries have adopted some of these ideas, such as native yeasts or reduced sulphites.

Practical tips

Anyone wishing to explore these wines will find it useful to ask in specialist shops and restaurants, which usually know the producers well. Because of their sensitivity, they are best kept somewhere cool and stable and served at a moderate temperature. A slight haze or some sediment is not in itself a fault, but vinegary or unpleasant aromas are, whatever philosophy the wine was made under.
