Wine faults: cork taint, oxidation, Brett, reduction and more

How to recognise the most common wine faults, what causes them and which are irreversible and which may improve with air.

A faulty wine is one that shows abnormal smells or flavours due to chemical or microbiological spoilage or to contamination. Knowing how to recognise faults is useful both for enthusiasts, who will be able to send back a bottle in poor condition in a restaurant, and for professionals. One must distinguish, however, between a fault and a feature of style: some compounds regarded as faults in excess are, in small doses, part of the complexity of certain wines. The boundary sometimes depends on perception thresholds and individual taste.

Cork taint: TCA

The best-known fault is cork taint, caused mainly by 2,4,6-trichloroanisole, or TCA. It forms when certain fungi convert chlorinated compounds, originating for example from cleaning products or wood treatments, in the cork itself or in materials in the winery. It smells musty, of cellars, wet cardboard or old newspaper, and it also flattens the wine's fruit. It can be perceived at extremely low concentrations, in the order of nanograms per litre, and some people are much more sensitive than others.

Although it is called cork taint, not every case comes from the stopper: a contaminated winery can affect wines with any type of closure. Related compounds, such as tribromoanisole, produce similar smells. The cork industry has developed cleaning and control methods in recent decades to reduce the problem. TCA is irreversible: aerating the wine does not fix it.

Oxidation

An oxidised wine has been exposed to too much oxygen, through a faulty closure, poor storage or careless winemaking. It loses its fresh fruit and acquires smells of bruised apple, walnut or sherry, largely due to acetaldehyde. The colour changes: whites darken towards gold or brown, and reds lose brightness and turn brick-coloured. This fault should not be confused with the deliberate oxidative ageing of wines such as oloroso, amontillado, tawny port or certain Jura wines, in which those notes are sought and form part of the style.

Volatile acidity

Every wine contains a small amount of volatile acids, mainly acetic acid. When the level is high, the wine smells of vinegar and often of glue or nail-polish remover, due to the ethyl acetate that accompanies it. It is usually caused by acetic acid bacteria acting in the presence of air, although some yeasts and lactic bacteria produce it too. Regulations set maximum limits for volatile acidity. In small amounts, some tasters regard it as something that lifts the aromatic expression of certain reds.

Brettanomyces

Brettanomyces, often shortened to Brett, is a yeast that can grow in wine, especially during barrel ageing and in high-pH wines with little sulphur dioxide. It produces volatile phenols, notably 4-ethylphenol, which smells of stables, horse sweat, leather or sticking plaster, and 4-ethylguaiacol, smokier and spicier. At low levels some enthusiasts enjoy these notes as a rustic touch; at high levels they mask the fruit and leave a metallic finish. Brett is hard to eradicate from a winery once established, so hygiene and sulphur management are essential.

Reduction

Reduction is in a sense the opposite of oxidation: it appears in wines that have been heavily protected from oxygen. It is caused by volatile sulphur compounds. Hydrogen sulphide smells of rotten eggs; mercaptans of cooked cabbage, garlic, onion or burnt rubber. They usually originate from yeasts stressed by a lack of nutrients during fermentation. When reduction is mild and caused by hydrogen sulphide, it often dissipates with aeration or decanting; in more severe cases the compounds can turn into more stable forms that are harder to remove.

It should be distinguished from certain flinty or struck-match notes that some producers seek in reductive-style whites and which, in small doses, are considered a virtue.

Other faults

  • Excess sulphur dioxide: a pungent smell of a freshly struck match that irritates the nose. It can soften with aeration.
  • Light strike: white, sparkling and rosé wines in clear bottles exposed to light can develop cabbage or onion smells through reactions involving riboflavin and sulphur-containing amino acids. That is why many wines are bottled in dark glass.
  • Cooked wine: a wine exposed to high temperatures during transport or storage takes on stewed-fruit or jammy notes and loses freshness. Sometimes the cork is pushed out or the wine has seeped.
  • Refermentation in bottle: a still wine with unexpected fizz and haze may have undergone a fermentation of residual sugar or a malolactic fermentation in the bottle.
  • Geranium: a smell of geranium leaves caused by the bacterial breakdown of sorbic acid, a preservative, in some sweet wines.
  • Mousiness: a microbial fault, more common in wines with very little sulphur, which is not perceived on the nose but on the finish, recalling a rodent cage or stale popcorn.

What is not a fault

Tartrate crystals, small clear crystals that appear at the bottom of the bottle or on the cork after chilling, are natural salts of tartaric acid and do not affect taste. The sediment in old reds is not a fault either, but the result of the evolution of polyphenols. A young wine that is a little closed and opens up after a few minutes in the glass is not faulty.

What to do with a faulty wine

In a restaurant, the sommelier or waiter usually pours a small amount so the wine can be checked. That moment is for detecting faults, not for deciding whether you like the wine. If you suspect a fault, the right thing is to say so politely. At home, if in doubt, leave the wine for a few minutes in the glass or a decanter: whatever disappears with air was probably mild reduction; whatever persists or gets worse, such as TCA or oxidation, cannot be remedied.
