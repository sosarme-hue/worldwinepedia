Blind tasting

What blind tasting is, why professionals and enthusiasts use it, how deductive reasoning works and how to organise a blind tasting at home.

Blind tasting means tasting a wine without knowing its identity: not the label, not the producer, not the region, and sometimes not even the colour. To the general public it may look like a guessing game, but for professionals and enthusiasts it is a serious tool, both for assessing wines impartially and for training perception and memory.

Why taste without seeing the label

Prior information strongly conditions what we perceive. Knowing that a wine comes from a prestigious region, that it is expensive or that it is made by an admired producer creates expectations that influence judgement, even if the taster is not aware of it. Various experiments have demonstrated this: in a well-known study carried out in Bordeaux early this century, oenology students described a white wine to which a tasteless red dye had been added using terms typical of red wines. The example shows how far sight and expectation steer the sense of smell.

Blind tasting tries to neutralise these biases. That is why it is the norm in wine competitions, in many appellation assessment panels and in comparisons of vintages or producers. One of its most famous episodes was the tasting held in Paris in 1976, at which a panel of French tasters, unaware of the wines' origins, placed a red and a white from California ahead of highly renowned French wines. The result had an enormous impact and helped bring New World wines into the spotlight.

Types of blind tasting

There are several degrees of “blindness”:

  • Single-blind tasting: the taster knows something about the line-up —for example, that all the wines are from the same variety or region, or from the same vintage— but not the identity of each sample.
  • Double-blind tasting: no information about the wines is given.
  • Black-glass tasting: opaque glasses are used so that not even the colour guides the taster, an exercise that often reveals how hard it is to tell some whites from light reds by nose and palate alone when served at the same temperature.

Deductive reasoning

In the training of sommeliers and other professionals, blind tasting is approached with a deductive method. The aim is not to “guess” but to reach a reasoned conclusion from what is perceived, following a set order:

  • Sight: the depth and hue of the colour suggest the variety, the age or possible oxidative ageing.
  • Nose: primary aromas point towards certain grapes; notes of oak, lees or malolactic fermentation speak of the winemaking; tertiary aromas, of development.
  • Palate: structure is often the most reliable clue. High acidity and moderate alcohol suggest a cool climate; high alcohol and very ripe fruit, a warm one. The amount and texture of the tannins help distinguish varieties.
  • Conclusion: the clues are combined to propose a variety or blend, a region, an age range and an assessment of quality.

The key is that every conclusion rests on concrete observations. A good taster may get a wine's exact identity wrong and still have tasted superbly if the description and reasoning are consistent.

Limits and humility

Blind tasting is a demanding and often humbling discipline. Even the most experienced professionals are frequently wrong, because many wines do not fit the stereotypes: some producers work in unusual styles, some vintages are atypical and some regions resemble one another. The taster's condition matters too: tiredness, a cold, food eaten beforehand or the order of the samples can all alter perception.

It also has limits as an assessment tool. When many wines are tasted in a row, the most concentrated or aromatic tend to stand out against subtler ones, and a young wine may not show its full potential at that moment. That is why serious panels repeat samples, control serving temperature and order, and limit the number of wines per session.

How to organise a blind tasting at home

You do not need to be a professional to enjoy this exercise. A few simple guidelines:

  • Wrap the bottles in paper bags or opaque sleeves and number them; ideally the person pouring should not take part, or someone else should shuffle the order.
  • Serve all the wines at the right temperature and in identical glasses.
  • Start with simple comparisons: two very different varieties, the same wine from two vintages, or the same grape from two different climates.
  • Write down impressions before discussing them aloud, so that participants do not influence one another.
  • Keep water and some bread to hand, and use spittoons if many wines are being tasted.

At the end, unveiling the bottles is usually the most instructive moment: it lets you compare your impressions with reality and fix in your memory features that will be easier to recognise next time.

A school of attention

More than a test of skill, blind tasting is a school of attention and honesty. It forces the taster to listen to what the wine is really saying rather than what the label promises, and it is a reminder that pleasure and quality do not always coincide with fame or price.
