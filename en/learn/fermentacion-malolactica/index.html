Oenology & science

Malolactic fermentation

The second transformation many wines undergo: bacteria convert malic acid into lactic acid, softening acidity and bringing stability and new nuances.

After alcoholic fermentation, many wines go through a second, quieter but very important transformation: malolactic fermentation. Despite its name, it is not a fermentation in the strict sense, nor is it carried out by yeasts, but by lactic acid bacteria. Its main effect is the conversion of sharp-tasting malic acid into softer lactic acid. Almost all quality red wines undergo it, while in whites and sparkling wines it is a stylistic choice.

The chemistry of the conversion

Grapes contain two main organic acids: tartaric and malic. Malic acid, the same acid that makes green apples tart, is abundant in under-ripe grapes and in those from cool climates, and it declines as grapes ripen in warm conditions. During malolactic fermentation, bacteria convert each molecule of malic acid into one of lactic acid and one of carbon dioxide. Because malic acid has two acid groups and lactic acid only one, the result is a fall in total acidity and a slight rise in pH. On the palate the wine feels rounder and less sharp.

The release of carbon dioxide is visible: in the cellar a slow, fine bubbling can be seen in tanks or barrels, which growers long recognised as the wine starting to stir again in spring, when cellar temperatures rise.

The bacteria involved

The most important species is Oenococcus oeni, a lactic acid bacterium particularly well adapted to the hostile environment of wine: it tolerates alcohol, acidity and moderate levels of sulphur dioxide better than other bacteria. Other genera, such as Lactobacillus and Pediococcus, can also grow, especially in high-pH wines, but they are more often associated with faults: unpleasant lactic smells, oily or ropy wines, or the production of biogenic amines such as histamine.

As with yeasts, the winemaker can wait for the bacteria naturally present in the cellar to start malolactic fermentation or can inoculate selected cultures of Oenococcus oeni. Inoculation allows faster, more controlled progress and reduces the risk of undesirable bacteria taking hold.

What it brings to the wine

Besides softening acidity, malolactic fermentation changes aroma and texture. The best-known compound it produces is diacetyl, responsible for the buttery or creamy notes found in some Chardonnay. Its concentration depends greatly on the bacterial strain, on contact with the lees and on when the wine is separated from them; at high levels it becomes heavy. In reds, malolactic fermentation tends to tone down green notes and integrate the fruit, and it is usually perceived as added roundness.

The other great advantage is microbiological stability. A wine that keeps its malic acid may start malolactic fermentation spontaneously once bottled, producing gas, haze and odd aromas. Completing it in the cellar removes a substrate that bacteria could otherwise exploit later.

When it is sought and when it is avoided

In reds, malolactic fermentation is practically systematic. It takes place in tank or in barrel; in the latter case it is said to help the wine and oak integrate earlier, although the exact effect is debated among winemakers.

In whites, the decision depends on style. Many barrel-aged Chardonnays, including numerous white Burgundies, go through it to gain volume and complexity. In aromatic, fresh whites, by contrast, such as many Albariño, Verdejo, Riesling or Sauvignon Blanc wines, it is common to block it to preserve acid tension and fruit purity, although some producers allow partial or full malolactic in part of their wines. In sparkling wines it is also a house decision: some Champagne houses always carry it out and others avoid it to keep acidity.

To prevent it, winemakers combine low temperatures, sulphur additions after alcoholic fermentation, filtration or prompt removal from the lees. To encourage it, they keep the wine at a mild temperature, limit sulphur dioxide and, if necessary, add bacteria.

Conditioning factors

Malolactic fermentation is harder in wines with very low pH, high alcohol, a lot of sulphur dioxide or cold temperatures. In cool regions it may therefore be delayed until the spring after harvest, whereas in warm climates, with higher-pH grapes, it happens easily, sometimes almost at the same time as alcoholic fermentation. Some winemakers practise co-inoculation, adding bacteria shortly after the yeasts so that both fermentations overlap; others prefer sequential inoculation once the sugar is finished. Both strategies have pros and cons in terms of speed, risk of volatile acidity and aromatic profile.

A delicate balance

Well managed, malolactic fermentation is an ally. Poorly controlled, it can raise volatile acidity, generate biogenic amines or leave inappropriate lactic aromas. In warm climates, where grapes already arrive with little acidity, some winemakers even question whether it should be carried out in full, and combine its management with other techniques to preserve freshness.

In short, malolactic fermentation shows clearly how modern winemaking brings together microbiology and style: the same reaction can be essential for a red built to age and carefully avoided in a white that seeks to express the vivacity of its grape.
