Italian designations: DOC and DOCG

How Italy's system of DOCG, DOC and IGT is organised, what terms such as Classico, Superiore or Riserva mean, and why some great wines were born outside the designations.

Italy has one of the oldest and most varied wine cultures in the world, with hundreds of native grape varieties and vineyards in every region. To bring order to this diversity, from the 1960s the country developed a system of designations that today fits within the European PDO and PGI framework but keeps its own abbreviations: DOCG, DOC and IGT.

Origins of the system

The first modern designation law, passed in 1963, created the categories of Denominazione di Origine Controllata and Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita. The first DOCs were approved in the following years, Vernaccia di San Gimignano among the pioneers. The first DOCGs arrived in 1980 and went to four long-established reds: Barolo, Barbaresco, Brunello di Montalcino and Vino Nobile di Montepulciano. In 1992 a new law introduced the IGT category and reinforced the idea of a quality pyramid. The rules were later brought together in a consolidated act on vines and wine.

The current pyramid

DOCG

Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita is the most demanding level. As well as meeting all the rules of a DOC, the wines undergo chemical analysis and tasting by panels before release. Bottles carry a numbered strip, or fascetta, issued under public control, which usually wraps around the neck or closure. As a rule, an area can only apply for DOCG after being a DOC for a number of years.

DOC

Denominazione di Origine Controllata is the core of the system. Each DOC has a disciplinare di produzione (production rules) setting the area, the varieties and their proportions, maximum yields, minimum alcohol, winemaking techniques and ageing periods. Some are very large, covering almost an entire region; others are tiny, limited to a handful of villages.

IGT

Indicazione Geografica Tipica corresponds to the European PGI. It covers broad areas, such as Toscana or Sicilia, and allows much more freedom over varieties and methods. Below it are wines without a geographical indication, labelled simply as vino, the successors to the former vino da tavola.

Additional terms

Within a DOC or DOCG, the rules may provide for additional terms with specific requirements:

  • Classico: identifies the historic, original zone of a designation that was later enlarged, as in Chianti Classico, Soave Classico or Valpolicella Classico.
  • Superiore: indicates stricter requirements, usually a higher minimum alcohol level or lower yields, and sometimes longer ageing.
  • Riserva: reserved for wines with a minimum ageing period longer than that of the basic version, set by each set of rules.
  • Vigna: followed by a proper name, shows that the wine comes from a specific registered vineyard.

Some designations have created their own categories. In Chianti Classico, for instance, Gran Selezione sits above Riserva and requires estate-grown grapes, and the designation has also defined additional geographical units that allow the village or area to be named. In Barolo and Barbaresco, the menzioni geografiche aggiuntive officially delimit the vineyards and places whose names may appear on the label, a form of site classification close to the idea of a cru.

The Super Tuscan paradox

In its early decades the system had an unexpected effect. The rules of some DOCs, such as Chianti at the time, required white grapes in the red blend or prevented the use of international varieties. Several Tuscan producers decided to make wines outside those rules, with cabernet sauvignon, merlot or sangiovese alone and ageing in barriques, which had to be sold as mere table wines. Some of them achieved great international renown and became known as Super Tuscans. The creation of IGT and the reform of several DOCs were in part a response: today Chianti Classico may be made from sangiovese alone, and Bolgheri, birthplace of some of these wines, has its own DOC.

Regional diversity

Each Italian region has its own map of designations. Piemonte has a notable number of DOCGs centred on nebbiolo, barbera and moscato; the Veneto includes designations as different as Prosecco, Soave and Amarone della Valpolicella; in the south, areas such as Taurasi, with aglianico, or Etna, with nerello mascalese, stand out. Some designations bear a place name and others combine grape and place, such as Montepulciano d'Abruzzo or Barbera d'Asti, which can cause confusion: Vino Nobile di Montepulciano is a Tuscan red based on sangiovese, whereas Montepulciano d'Abruzzo names a grape grown in another region.

How to read the label

On an Italian bottle, it is worth looking, in this order, for the designation and its category, any additional terms, the producer and the vintage. The fascetta indicates a DOCG; the word Classico points to the historic zone; Riserva signals longer ageing. As in other countries, the category alone does not guarantee a particular style or quality, but it helps place the wine within its territory and tradition.
