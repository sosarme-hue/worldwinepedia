Food pairing

Wine and cheese

Why white wine often goes better with cheese than people think, and which wines suit fresh, soft-ripened, hard, blue and goat's cheeses.

Wine and cheese have a great deal in common: both are born of fermentation, both express a place and both can evolve over months or years. Their union at the table is a classic, but not as simple as is often thought. The idea that any cheese goes with any red is, in fact, the source of many disappointing combinations. With a few principles it is easy to do much better.

Why red does not always work

Cheese is rich in fat and salt and, when aged, in intense flavour compounds, umami and sometimes bitterness. The tannins of a powerful red can clash with some cheeses: fat does not always soften astringency, and with creamy or very salty cheeses the red becomes harsh or metallic. White wines, by contrast, provide acidity to balance fat and have no tannins to cause conflict. Many sommeliers believe that if a single wine must be chosen for a varied cheese board, an aromatic, full-bodied white or a fortified wine is usually more versatile than a tannic red.

Fresh cheeses

Fresh, mild cheeses such as ricotta, mozzarella, Spanish queso de Burgos or fresh goat's cheese have delicate, milky, slightly tangy flavours. They call for light, fresh white wines without oak, or dry rosés. An Albariño, a Verdejo, a Sauvignon Blanc or a dry sparkling wine accompanies them well without dominating.

Goat's cheeses

Goat's cheese, especially when young, has marked acidity and herbaceous notes. Its most frequently cited partner is Sauvignon Blanc: in the Loire, the wines of Sancerre and local goat's cheeses such as crottin de Chavignol form a famous regional pairing. The acidity of the wine matches that of the cheese and their green aromas complement each other. More mature goat's cheeses can take fuller whites and even light reds.

Soft, bloomy-rind cheeses

White-rind cheeses such as Brie or Camembert are creamy and rich, with mushroomy notes. Sparkling wines work particularly well: bubbles and acidity cleanse the palate and contrast with the creaminess. Whites with some body, such as a Chardonnay without too much oak, or light, fruity reds such as a young Pinot Noir, are also good options. Tannic reds tend to turn bitter with them.

Soft washed-rind cheeses, such as Époisses or Taleggio, have a powerful smell. They suit aromatic, slightly unctuous whites such as Gewürztraminer, or some light reds. In Burgundy, Époisses is commonly served with the region's own wines.

Semi-hard and hard cheeses

Pressed cheeses such as Manchego, Idiazábal, Comté, Gruyère or Parmigiano Reggiano gain intensity and concentration as they mature. These are the cheeses that best accommodate red wines, because their richness in protein and fat softens tannins.

  • A semi-cured Manchego gets on well with young or crianza Tempranillo reds, such as those from La Mancha, Valdepeñas or Ribera del Duero, and also with full-bodied whites.
  • A well-aged Manchego or a long-matured Parmesan, salty and with tyrosine crystals, pairs well with aged reds and, very successfully, with fortified wines such as amontillado or oloroso, whose nutty notes find an echo in the cheese.
  • Idiazábal, made from Latxa sheep's milk and often smoked, goes well with medium-bodied reds and some oak-aged whites; in the Basque Country it is also enjoyed with txakoli.
  • Comté and other mountain cheeses have a classic regional partner in the vin jaune of the Jura, thanks to their shared walnut notes.

Blue cheeses

Blue cheeses such as Cabrales, Roquefort, Gorgonzola or Stilton are intense, salty and sometimes piquant. The most celebrated pairing is by contrast with sweet wines: Roquefort with Sauternes, Stilton with port and, in Spain, Cabrales with a Pedro Ximénez or with sweet Moscatel wines. The sweetness of the wine balances the salt and intensity of the cheese, and its acidity and concentration stand up to the flavour. A dry, tannic red, by contrast, usually tastes bitter next to a blue.

Practical tips

  • Temperature: cheese is more enjoyable when taken out of the fridge in advance, so that its aromas and texture can show.
  • Order: on a board, go from milder to more intense cheeses and, if several wines are served, from light whites to reds or sweet wines.
  • Accompaniments: nuts, quince paste, fruit or honey change the pairing; a sweet touch on the board favours wines with some sugar.
  • Shared origin: as with other pairings, cheeses and wines from the same area tend to get along, because they grew up together.

A field for experiment

The enormous variety of cheeses and wines makes a complete list of pairings impossible. The rules above are a starting point, but the best combinations are often discovered by trying. A tasting of two or three cheeses with two or three different wines, one white, one red and one fortified or sweet, is a very instructive way to understand how fat, salt, acidity, sweetness and tannins interact.
