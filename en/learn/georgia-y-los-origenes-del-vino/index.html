Georgia and the origins of wine

What archaeology reveals about Neolithic wine in the South Caucasus and how Georgia has kept winemaking in buried clay vessels, the qvevri, alive for millennia.

Few nations tie their identity to wine as strongly as Georgia, the South Caucasus country lying between the Black Sea and the Caspian. Georgians often present their land as the cradle of wine, and archaeology has provided evidence that places some of the oldest known traces of grape winemaking in this region. That antiquity is matched by a remarkable continuity: the traditional method of fermenting and storing wine in large buried clay vessels, the qvevri, has been handed down for millennia.

The archaeological evidence

In southern Georgia, at Neolithic sites in the Kvemo Kartli area such as Gadachrili Gora and Shulaveris Gora, fragments of pottery vessels were found in which chemical analysis detected residues consistent with grape wine, in particular tartaric acid and other associated compounds. The studies, published in 2017 by an international team, dated those remains to roughly the sixth millennium BC, around 6000 BC. Grapevine pollen was also found around the sites.

These finds are among the oldest chemical evidence of grape wine identified so far. They do not necessarily mean that wine was invented in a single place: across a wide area covering the Caucasus, eastern Anatolia and the Zagros mountains there are early traces, such as those from the site of Hajji Firuz in present-day Iran, or the later winery in the Areni cave in Armenia. What they do show is that the South Caucasus was one of the earliest centres of the practice.

From wild vine to cultivated vine

The Eurasian wild vine, Vitis vinifera subsp. sylvestris, grows naturally in this region. Domestication, which produced hermaphrodite plants with higher yields and larger berries, was a long process whose exact timing and geography are still being researched. Georgia today retains a remarkable genetic heritage, with hundreds of native varieties described, though only some are grown commercially.

The qvevri

The qvevri is a large, egg-shaped earthenware vessel without handles, traditionally lined inside with beeswax. It is buried up to its neck in the floor of the cellar, the marani, which keeps it at a cool, stable temperature. The traditional method, especially associated with the eastern region of Kakheti, involves putting the must into the qvevri together with skins, pips and sometimes stems, known in Georgian as chacha. After fermentation the qvevri is sealed and the wine stays in contact with the solids for months.

Applied to white grapes, this method produces amber-coloured wines with tannins and a structure unusual in a white: these are what are now known internationally as orange or amber wines. In the west of the country, in regions such as Imereti, a smaller proportion of skins is traditionally used.

In 2013 UNESCO inscribed the ancient Georgian traditional qvevri wine-making method on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. Making the vessels themselves is a specialised craft, passed down in a few families of potters.

Wine, faith and culture

Wine holds a central place in Georgian culture. According to tradition, Saint Nino, who preached Christianity in the Caucasian kingdom of Iberia in the fourth century, carried a cross made of vine stems bound with her own hair; that cross, with its slightly drooping arms, is today one of the symbols of the Georgian Orthodox Church. Medieval churches and monasteries frequently display carvings of grape clusters and vine leaves.

The supra, the traditional feast, revolves around wine and toasts. It is led by the tamada, a toastmaster who proposes a sequence of toasts to family, ancestors, homeland or friendship. It is a ceremony of hospitality and of speech as much as of food.

Emblematic varieties

Two Georgian varieties stand out:

  • Saperavi: a red grape with coloured flesh, giving deeply coloured wines with good acidity, capable of ageing.
  • Rkatsiteli: a widely planted white grape with marked acidity, used both for European-style wines and for amber wines made in qvevri.

Others, such as Mtsvane Kakhuri, Kisi, Tsolikouri and Chinuri, play an important regional role.

The twentieth century and the Soviet era

Georgia was part of the Russian Empire from the nineteenth century and later of the Soviet Union. In Soviet times, vineyards were organised into large state farms and production was geared to volume, with semi-sweet wines for the Soviet market carrying particular weight. The anti-alcohol campaign of the mid-1980s also led to vineyards being uprooted in several republics, Georgia included. During those decades the qvevri method survived mainly in homes and villages.

A new beginning

After independence in 1991, the wine sector went through difficult years. The Russian embargo on Georgian wine imposed in 2006 forced producers to seek new markets and to focus on quality. Since then many wineries, large and small, have returned to qvevri winemaking, and Georgian amber wines have attracted international interest. Georgia also has a system of protected designations of origin, many of them in Kakheti.

Thus a country that preserves some of the oldest traces of wine is today also a place where that heritage is being vigorously reinterpreted.
