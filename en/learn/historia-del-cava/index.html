History & culture

The history of cava

How Spain's traditional-method sparkling wine was born in Penedès, from the first bottles of the nineteenth century to its designation of origin and today's debates.

Cava is the Spanish sparkling wine made by the traditional method, the same one used in Champagne: a second fermentation in the bottle itself produces the carbon dioxide, and the wine then rests on its lees for a minimum period before disgorgement. Although it is now made in several regions of Spain, its history is closely tied to one Catalan district, Penedès, and to one town, Sant Sadurní d'Anoia.

Penedès before sparkling wine

Penedès, lying between Barcelona and Tarragona, had been a wine-growing area for centuries. In the nineteenth century it produced mainly still red and white wines, as well as spirits, for the Catalan market and for export. Barcelona, in the midst of industrialisation, was a nearby and dynamic market, and its bourgeoisie appreciated prestigious products from France, champagne among them.

The first bottles

Around the mid-nineteenth century there were several attempts in Catalonia to make sparkling wines in imitation of champagne. The name most often associated with their establishment is that of Josep Raventós, of the family that owned Codorníu in Sant Sadurní d'Anoia. According to the account handed down by the house itself and widely accepted, in 1872 he made his first bottles of traditional-method sparkling wine after studying the Champagne technique.

A decisive feature of that venture was the use of local varieties instead of the grapes of Champagne. Catalan producers adapted the method to what grew in their vineyards, and over time the classic trio of white cava varieties became established: Macabeo, Xarel·lo and Parellada.

Phylloxera and the transformation of the vineyard

At the end of the nineteenth century, phylloxera reached Catalonia and destroyed much of the Penedès vineyard. Replanting, with vines grafted onto American rootstocks, was done largely with white varieties, better suited to the sparkling wine that was gaining ground. The vineyard crisis thus helped steer the district towards producing base wines for sparkling wine.

In the early decades of the twentieth century the number of producers grew, and extensive underground cellars were dug in Sant Sadurní d'Anoia for ageing in bottle. The Codorníu cellars, designed by the modernista architect Josep Puig i Cadafalch, are one of the most remarkable examples of wine architecture of the period. Freixenet and other family houses also contributed to the sector's growth.

From champaña to cava

For a long time these wines were sold in Spain under names such as champaña or xampany. As international protection of the Champagne name was strengthened, Spanish producers had to find another name. They adopted the term cava, which refers to the underground cellars where the wine rests in bottle, and it became established in the second half of the twentieth century.

Regulation of cava as a wine with a protected name was consolidated in the 1980s, in the context of Spain's entry into the European Community. A particular feature of this designation is that it is not confined to a single district: the authorised area includes municipalities in several autonomous communities, among them parts of Catalonia, Aragon, La Rioja, Navarre, the Basque Country, the Valencian Community and Extremadura, although the vast majority of production remains concentrated in Penedès.

Growth and popularity

In the second half of the twentieth century, cava became a staple of Spanish celebrations, especially at Christmas, and developed a major export market. Mechanisation of processes such as riddling, using gyropalettes, made it possible to increase production. Alongside the big houses, many medium-sized and small family wineries continued to operate.

The range of styles widened, from young cavas to long-aged ones, and other authorised varieties were added, such as Chardonnay for whites and Garnacha, Monastrell, Trepat and Pinot Noir for rosés.

Twenty-first-century debates

Growth in volume also created tensions. Some producers felt that the image of a sparkling wine associated with low prices did not do justice to the quality of the best cavas, and that the geographical breadth of the designation weakened its link with the land.

These discussions led to several changes. On the one hand, the designation introduced more demanding categories, such as cava de guarda superior and Cava de Paraje Calificado, reserved for wines from specific plots with strict ageing requirements. More recently, an internal zoning was approved that distinguishes large production areas. On the other hand, some producers chose to leave the designation: some joined the Clàssic Penedès category within the Penedès DO, and another group created the collective brand Corpinnat, with its own rules based on vineyards and production in the area.

A sparkling wine with its own identity

Over a century and a half, cava has gone from being a local imitation of champagne to one of the most widely produced traditional-method sparkling wines in the world, with an identity based on its Mediterranean varieties. Its history reflects the adaptability of Penedès and, at the same time, the ongoing debate between volume and quality, and between a broad brand and the expression of place.
