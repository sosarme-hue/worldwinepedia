History & culture

The history of Madeira

From Atlantic port of call to the lodges of Funchal: how tropical voyages, heat and fortification gave rise to one of the longest-lived wines in the world.

Madeira is a fortified wine made on the Portuguese island of the same name, in the Atlantic Ocean off the coast of Morocco. What sets it apart from almost every other wine is its relationship with heat and oxygen, two factors usually considered enemies of wine that here form part of how it is made. That singularity did not arise from a plan but from history: the history of an island at the crossroads of the great sea routes.

An island on the navigators' route

Portuguese navigators reached the archipelago at the beginning of the fifteenth century, as part of the expeditions sponsored by Prince Henry the Navigator, and settlement of the island of Madeira began soon afterwards. The settlers cleared the forests that gave the island its name, since madeira means wood in Portuguese, and built irrigation channels, the levadas, which still run through its mountains.

The first great commercial crop was sugar cane, which brought considerable prosperity in the fifteenth century. When competition from Brazilian and other colonial sugar caused that activity to decline, the vine gradually gained ground. The steep volcanic slopes were farmed in small terraces called poios, often with the vines trained on low pergolas.

The wine of the ships

Funchal, the capital, became a port of call for ships crossing the Atlantic to the Americas, Africa and Asia. Vessels took on water, provisions and wine there. In the seventeenth century, an English law on colonial trade left Madeira wine in an advantageous position for direct export to England's American colonies, and the island became a regular supplier to them. Many British merchants settled in Funchal.

Madeira was highly prized in colonial America and in the early years of the United States. According to an often-repeated tradition, Madeira was used to toast the signing of the Declaration of Independence in 1776, although this is a detail that is hard to verify.

The discovery of heat

To withstand long voyages, a small amount of grape spirit was added to the wine. Merchants noticed something unexpected: wines that had travelled in the holds of ships, crossing the tropics and enduring high temperatures and the rolling of the sea for months, arrived in better condition than when they left. They became more complex, smoother and more robust.

That discovery led to a curious practice: sending wine on round trips through the tropics simply to improve it. Such wines were known as vinho da roda, wine that had made the round trip. Since this was expensive and impractical, in the eighteenth century producers began to reproduce the effect on the island itself, heating the wine in sun-exposed stores or with stoves.

Estufagem and canteiro

Out of that evolution came the two ageing methods still used today:

  • Estufagem: the wine is heated in tanks for a period of several months at a controlled temperature. This is the usual method for younger wines.
  • Canteiro: the wine ages slowly in casks on the upper floors of the lodges, where the natural warmth of the island climate transforms it over the years. This method is reserved for the finest, longest-aged wines.

In both cases the wine is exposed to oxygen and heat. The result is a wine that has already been through what would normally be spoilage, which explains its extraordinary ability to last, even after the bottle has been opened.

The addition of spirit was gradually systematised into today's fortification, carried out during or after fermentation depending on the desired level of sweetness.

The nineteenth-century plagues

In the mid-nineteenth century, Madeira's vineyards suffered two successive blows. First came powdery mildew, in the 1850s, and then phylloxera, in the 1870s. Both devastated production. Many growers gave up the vine or replaced it with other crops, and replanting relied on American and hybrid varieties and, above all, on Tinta Negra, a productive and versatile red grape that is now the most widely planted on the island. Several of the traditional varieties were reduced to very small areas.

The noble varieties and the styles

Tradition distinguishes four main white varieties, associated with increasing levels of sweetness:

  • Sercial: the driest style, with very marked acidity.
  • Verdelho: medium dry.
  • Boal or Bual: medium sweet.
  • Malvasia, known in English as Malmsey: the sweetest style.

To these can be added other historic and now scarce varieties, such as Terrantez and Bastardo. When the label names a variety, the wine must be made predominantly from it. Tinta Negra wines are labelled by their level of sweetness.

Madeiras may state an average age, such as five, ten, fifteen or twenty years and more, or come from a single harvest. Colheita wines are from a specific year with a minimum ageing of several years, and Frasqueira, also called Garrafeira, wines are vintage wines with very prolonged cask ageing of at least twenty years. Some old Madeiras are kept for more than a century.

Madeira today

Production is regulated by a public body of the autonomous region of Madeira. The number of exporting houses is small, and in recent decades the sector has seen renewed appreciation for noble-variety and vintage wines, as well as for their use in gastronomy. Alongside fortified wine, the island also produces table wines under a separate designation.

Madeira remains a unique case: a wine whose style was born of the Atlantic trade routes and the chance of sea voyages, and which still carries in its making the memory of those ships.
