The French appellation system

How AOCs and PDOs, PGIs and Vin de France work, and what the various crus and classifications of Burgundy, Bordeaux, Champagne and Alsace mean.

France was one of the first countries to build a national system to protect the origin of its wines, and its model has inspired much of Europe. The central idea is that of the appellation d'origine: a wine bears the name of a place because it comes from there and because that place, with its soils, climate and traditional practices, gives it a recognisable character.

Origins of the system

In the early twentieth century, after the phylloxera crisis, the French market suffered from frequent fraud: wines sold under prestigious names that did not come from those areas. After several partial laws, the Appellation d'Origine Contrôlée (AOC) was created in 1935, together with the body charged with managing it, today's INAO, now called the Institut national de l'origine et de la qualité. Since then, each appellation has been governed by a specification (cahier des charges) drawn up with producers and approved by the authorities.

The three current categories

With the European reform of the sector, the French system was integrated into the EU categories. Today there are three main levels:

  • AOP / AOC (Appellation d'Origine Protégée / Contrôlée). This is the most demanding level. The specification sets the delimited area, often plot by plot, the varieties, planting density, yields, minimum alcohol and winemaking practices. On French labels the traditional AOC wording remains common.
  • IGP (Indication Géographique Protégée), the successor to the former vins de pays. It covers broader areas, such as Pays d'Oc or Val de Loire, and allows more varieties and greater technical freedom. Many IGP wines state the grape on the label.
  • Vin de France, the category without a geographical indication that replaced the old vin de table. It may blend grapes from several regions and state the variety and vintage.

As in Spain or Italy, the category describes the regulatory framework, not intrinsic quality. Some well-regarded producers sell wines as IGP or Vin de France because they choose varieties or methods their AOC does not allow.

The geographical ladder of AOCs

Within AOCs there is usually a hierarchy running from the general to the particular. A regional appellation, such as Bourgogne or Bordeaux, covers a large territory; subregional or communal appellations, such as Médoc, Pauillac or Gevrey-Chambertin, are confined to specific areas or villages. The smaller the territory, the stricter the rules tend to be and the more precise the character attributed to the wine.

The crus

The word cru literally means “grown” and designates a vineyard or place of production with its own reputation. Its meaning varies greatly from region to region, which is the main source of confusion.

Burgundy: the climats

In Burgundy the classification applies to the land. Above the regional and village appellations stand the premiers crus, delimited plots whose name appears on the label alongside the village name, and the grands crus, which are appellations in their own right, such as Chambertin or Montrachet, and are usually named without the village. These named plots are called climats. Chablis has a similar ladder: Petit Chablis, Chablis, Chablis Premier Cru and Chablis Grand Cru.

Bordeaux: the château classifications

In Bordeaux, by contrast, classifications rank properties, not plots. The most famous is the 1855 classification, drawn up for the Paris Universal Exhibition, which ranked the red wines of the Médoc, plus one château from Graves, into five tiers and also classified the sweet wines of Sauternes and Barsac. It has changed very little; the best-known change is the promotion of Château Mouton Rothschild to first growth in 1973. Other classifications include that of Graves and that of Saint-Émilion, which, unlike the 1855 ranking, is revised periodically. There is also the Cru Bourgeois designation for many Médoc properties.

Champagne, Alsace and other regions

In Champagne the traditional classification applies to villages: some hold grand cru status and others premier cru, according to an old scale once used to set grape prices. In Alsace, above the Alsace AOC, a series of delimited sites make up the Alsace Grand Cru AOC, each with its own name. In Beaujolais, the term crus refers to ten communal appellations, such as Morgon, Fleurie or Moulin-à-Vent, ranking above Beaujolais and Beaujolais-Villages. In the Rhône and elsewhere, the term Villages is used to distinguish certain villages.

Other label terms

Besides the appellation, a French label may include terms such as mis en bouteille au château or au domaine, indicating estate bottling, or the name of a specific place (lieu-dit) where the specification allows it. Sweet and sparkling wines also carry regulated sweetness or style terms.

Finding your way

To read a French wine, it helps to ask first which region it comes from and then which rung of the local ladder it occupies. Knowing that a Burgundy grand cru is a plot and a Bordeaux one a château, or that a Beaujolais cru is an entire village, avoids most misunderstandings. The system is complex because it reflects centuries of local history, but its underlying logic is constant: to tie each wine to a precise place and to a set of shared rules.
