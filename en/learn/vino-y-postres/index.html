Food pairing

Wine and desserts

Why the wine should be at least as sweet as the dessert, what role acidity plays, and which sweet wines, whether late-harvest, botrytised, ice wines or fortified, best accompany each family of desserts.

For many people, the end of a meal is the moment that receives the least attention as far as wine is concerned. It is common to finish with the same red as the main course, or to toast with a dry sparkling wine alongside the cake, and in both cases the result is usually disappointing. Pairing wine with desserts follows a few simple rules which, properly applied, open the door to one of the most fascinating and often least known families of wine: sweet wines.

The golden rule: the wine sweeter than the dessert

The fundamental principle is that the wine should be at least as sweet as the dessert. When the dish is sweeter than the wine, the sugar in the dessert makes the wine seem more acidic, thin and even bitter. This is why a dry wine, or a brut sparkling wine, suffers next to a cake. The same rule also explains why fine sweet wines, which may seem cloying when drunk on their own, become balanced and reveal their complexity alongside a suitable dessert.

Acidity, a necessary counterweight

A good sweet wine is not just sugar. It needs enough acidity not to feel heavy, and it is that acidity which allows it to accompany desserts made with fruit, cream or butter without overwhelming the palate. The world's great sweet wines, from those of Sauternes and Tokaj to Germany's late-harvest Rieslings, owe much of their reputation to this balance between sweetness and freshness. As a rough guide, the more acidic the dessert, for example one based on red berries or lemon, the more acidity the wine should have.

The main types of sweet wine

There are several ways of making a sweet wine, and each gives rise to different styles:

  • Late harvest: the grapes are left on the vine until they concentrate their sugars, sometimes with slight raisining.
  • Noble rot: the fungus Botrytis cinerea, in favourable conditions, dehydrates the berries and contributes aromas of honey, dried apricot and spice. It is the basis of Sauternes, made mainly from semillon, and of Hungarian Tokaji Aszú, made from furmint.
  • Drying: the grapes are dried in the sun or indoors before pressing, as with Andalusian Pedro Ximénez or Tuscan vin santo.
  • Ice wine: the grapes are harvested frozen on the vine and pressed in that state, so that only a highly concentrated must flows. Germany, Austria and Canada, with varieties such as riesling or vidal, are benchmarks.
  • Sweet fortified wines: adding alcohol stops fermentation and preserves part of the natural sugar, as in Port, sweet Madeira or the Moscatels of Málaga and Setúbal.

Fruit desserts

Apple or pear tarts, fruit salads and peach or apricot desserts get on very well with fresh, aromatic wines: a young Moscatel, a late-harvest Riesling or a Moscato d'Asti, light and only gently sparkling. With red berries, which are more acidic, a wine with marked acidity is advisable; some medium-dry or sweet sparkling rosés can work. Citrus desserts are among the most difficult because of their high acidity, and usually call for botrytised wines with plenty of verve.

Creams, custards and dairy desserts

Custards, flan, crema catalana or crème brûlée have a creamy texture and aromas of vanilla, egg and caramel. Botrytised wines, with their honeyed notes and acidity, are traditional partners: a Sauternes or a Tokaji Aszú finds an ideal counterpoint in these desserts. Moscatels with some age, which add notes of candied orange, also work well.

Nuts, caramel and traditional baking

Desserts made with almonds, walnuts, hazelnuts or caramel, such as turrón, nut tarts or syrup-soaked sponges, call for wines with oxidative, nutty aromas. A tawny Port, aged in wood, a sweet malvasia Madeira or a sweet oloroso are very good choices. In Spain there is also a simple and much-loved custom: pouring a little Pedro Ximénez, with its notes of raisin, fig and coffee, over vanilla ice cream.

Chocolate desserts and cheese at the end of the meal

Chocolate deserves separate treatment because of its bitterness, its fat and the intensity of its cocoa, and it usually calls for sweet, fortified reds. In many countries, the end of the meal also includes blue cheeses, which go classically with sweet wines, as with Roquefort and Sauternes or Stilton and Port.

Serving and moderation

Sweet wines are generally served cool, which enhances their acidity and tempers the impression of sweetness, although the older fortified wines can take a little more warmth. Because of their concentration, they are drunk from small glasses and in small quantities: a short glass is enough to accompany dessert. Once opened, a bottle of sweet wine usually keeps for more days than a dry wine, especially if it is fortified.
