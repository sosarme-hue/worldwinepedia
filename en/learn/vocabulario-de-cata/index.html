Wine tasting vocabulary

The most useful words for describing a wine's appearance, aroma and palate, explained clearly and without unnecessary jargon.

The language of wine has a reputation for being convoluted, and sometimes it is. Most of the terms tasters use, however, correspond to specific sensations and have a fairly precise meaning. Knowing them helps us organise what we perceive and communicate it. This article gathers the essential vocabulary following the order of a tasting: appearance, nose, palate and final assessment.

Appearance

  • Clear, bright, hazy: describe transparency. A bright wine reflects light vividly; a hazy one contains particles in suspension.
  • Depth or intensity of colour, especially in reds. It may be pale, medium or deep, depending on whether the bottom of the glass can be seen through the wine.
  • Rim: the outer edge of the wine in a tilted glass, where the hues indicating youth or development can be seen.
  • Hues: in whites, straw, lemon, gold, amber; in reds, violet, purple, ruby, cherry, garnet, brick.
  • Legs or tears: the drops that run down the sides of the glass. They are mainly related to alcohol, not to quality.
  • Mousse and bead: in sparkling wines, the foam that forms on the surface and the size and persistence of the bubbles.

Nose

  • Clean: free from apparent faults.
  • Intensity: low, medium or pronounced; a wine very discreet on the nose may be described as closed, especially if it is expected to open up with air or time.
  • Aroma and bouquet: strictly, aroma refers to smells from the grape and fermentation, and bouquet to those arising from maturation and ageing. In everyday use they are almost synonyms.
  • Primary, secondary, tertiary: a classification of aromas according to whether they come from the grape, fermentation or ageing.
  • Fruity: dominated by fruit aromas. One specifies whether the fruit is fresh, ripe, candied, stewed or dried.
  • Floral, herbaceous, vegetal: aromas of flowers, freshly cut grass or leaves and green pepper.
  • Spicy: pepper, clove, cinnamon, liquorice.
  • Toasty, smoky: notes often coming from barrel oak, although some varieties provide them too.
  • Lactic or dairy: butter, yoghurt or cream, often associated with malolactic fermentation or lees ageing.
  • Autolytic: notes of bread, pastry or yeast contributed by the lees in sparkling wines aged for a long time.
  • Balsamic: notes of eucalyptus, mint, resin or incense.
  • Forest floor: recalling damp earth, mushrooms and fallen leaves, typical of mature reds.
  • Pungent: an intense, sharp aroma, like that of acetaldehyde in fino and manzanilla.

Palate

  • Dry, off-dry, medium-sweet, sweet: describe the perceived level of sugar. Dry does not mean harsh; it simply means no noticeable sweetness.
  • Fresh, lively, crisp: with good acidity. At the opposite end, a wine with little acidity is flat or flabby; with too much, green or sharp.
  • Tannic, astringent: with marked tannins that dry the mouth. Tannins may be described as fine, silky, polished, ripe or, conversely, as coarse, green or drying.
  • Warm: with a heating sensation from alcohol. If excessive, the wine is said to be alcoholic or hot.
  • Body: the sense of weight. A wine can be light-bodied, medium-bodied or full-bodied, also called structured.
  • Unctuous, oily, rich: with a smooth, enveloping texture.
  • Fleshy: with a sense of volume and density, as if the wine could be chewed.
  • Round: without sharp edges, its components well integrated.
  • Balanced: when acidity, alcohol, sweetness and tannins offset each other without any one dominating.
  • Bitter: a taste that is normal to a small degree in some wines, notably certain Italian whites or young reds, and unpleasant in excess.
  • Saline: a slightly salty sensation often described in wines from Atlantic areas or in manzanilla.
  • Spritz or pétillance: a slight prickle of carbon dioxide in a wine that is not sparkling.

Finish

  • Retronasal: aromas perceived while the wine is in the mouth, reaching the nose from the back of the throat.
  • Length, finish or aftertaste: how long the impression of the wine lasts after swallowing. A finish may be short, medium or long.
  • Aftertaste: the flavours that remain at the end; it may be clean or leave an odd note.

Assessment

  • Complex: with many aromatic and flavour nuances that reveal themselves gradually. Its opposite is simple, which is not the same as bad.
  • Elegant, refined: balanced and harmonious, without excessive power. These terms are subjective but widely used.
  • Typicity: the degree to which a wine expresses the features expected of its variety, region or style.
  • Youthful, at its peak, developed, tired: indicate the stage of the wine's life. A tired wine has lost fruit and freshness.
  • Ageing potential: the estimated ability to improve or hold over the years.

Talking about wine naturally

You do not need to use all these terms to enjoy a wine or describe it well. The best tasting notes are clear and concrete: a sentence saying that a white is dry and fresh, with aromas of lemon and herbs and a medium finish, communicates more than a string of far-fetched adjectives. Vocabulary is a tool for sharing sensations, and its value lies in other people being able to understand it.
