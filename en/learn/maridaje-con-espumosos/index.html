Food pairing

Food pairing with sparkling wines

Sparkling wines are far more than a wine for toasts: their acidity and bubbles make them exceptionally versatile at the table. This article explains why, and how to choose according to style, sweetness and ageing.

In many homes, sparkling wine is kept for celebrations: it is opened for a toast and often forgotten as soon as the food arrives. That is a pity, because sparkling wines are among the most versatile wines at the table. Many sommeliers regard them as the safest solution when a varied menu or a difficult dish has to be matched, and in producing regions such as Champagne, Penedès or Franciacorta it is common to drink them throughout the meal.

Why they work so well

The effectiveness of sparkling wines at the table is explained by several features. The first is their high acidity, since the grapes destined for them are usually picked early, with good freshness. Acidity cleanses the palate, balances fat and heightens flavours. The second is the bubbles: carbon dioxide brings a sense of freshness and a scrubbing effect that is especially useful with fried food, creamy sauces and cheese. The third is their generally moderate alcohol and the absence of marked tannins, which avoids clashes with salt, umami, mild chilli heat or fish. Finally, sparkling wines with long ageing on the lees develop aromas of toast, brioche and nuts that give them depth for more complex dishes.

Sweetness: reading the label

Before choosing, it is worth looking at the sweetness indication on the label, which corresponds to the sugar added in the dosage. From driest to sweetest, the usual terms are brut nature or zero dosage, extra brut, brut, extra dry, sec, demi-sec and doux. One peculiarity should be borne in mind: “extra dry” and “sec” do not denote completely dry wines, but ones slightly sweeter than a brut. For most savoury dishes, brut nature, extra brut or brut are chosen; demi-sec and sweet styles are kept for desserts, following the rule that the wine should be at least as sweet as the dish.

Aperitifs, fried food and shellfish

With an aperitif, light, fruity sparkling wines are a natural option: Prosecco, made from glera using the tank method of second fermentation, offers aromas of pear and white flowers and an easy-going character. With fried food, tempura, croquetas or battered fish, a brut cava or a traditional-method sparkling wine cuts through the fat effectively. Oysters and other raw shellfish find a classic partner in very dry sparkling wines, brut nature or extra brut, especially blanc de blancs made from chardonnay, with a tauter, more mineral profile. Caviar and fish roe are traditionally accompanied by fine dry sparkling wines.

Main courses

Sparkling wines with long ageing, whether vintage or simply aged for an extended period, have the body and complexity to accompany main courses. Roast chicken, fish with a butter sauce, mushroom risotto or dishes with truffle find in them a good counterpoint: their toasty, nutty aromas converse with the notes of the cooking. Rosé sparkling wines, which often include pinot noir, contribute red fruit and a little more structure, and suit salmon, tuna, duck, ham or even light red meats. A rosé cava made from garnacha or trepat, or a rosé Champagne, can accompany an entire meal.

Cheese and Asian cuisine

With cheese, the acidity and bubbles of sparkling wines work very well against soft cheeses with bloomy rinds, such as brie or camembert, and against fresh and goat's cheeses. With aged cheeses, a long-aged sparkling wine can work better than many reds. In Asian cuisine, dry sparkling wines are also a highly regarded choice: they cleanse the palate after tempura, accompany sushi and soften the sensation of moderate chilli heat.

Sweet sparkling wines and sparkling reds

Not all sparkling wines are dry or white. Moscato d'Asti, low in alcohol, aromatic and sweet, goes well with fruit desserts, light cakes and simple pastries. Demi-sec or sweet traditional-method sparkling wines can follow a dessert that is not too cloying. Lambrusco from Emilia-Romagna, a dry or slightly off-dry sparkling red, is the traditional partner for the region's cured meats, mortadella and filled pasta.

Serving

Sparkling wines are served cold, but not ice-cold, so that their aromas can express themselves: the youngest and freshest a little colder, the long-aged ones slightly less so. Although the narrow flute preserves the bubbles well, many professionals now prefer medium-sized white wine glasses, which allow the aromatic complexity to be better appreciated, especially in aged sparkling wines. When opening the bottle, it is wise to hold the cork and turn the bottle gently to avoid accidents and the loss of wine.

A choice for the whole menu

Their ability to move from aperitif to fish, from fried food to cheese and, in their sweet versions, to dessert makes sparkling wines a practical choice when a single wine is to be served throughout the meal, always with the moderation appropriate to any wine.
