German wine classifications

From Kabinett to Trockenbeerenauslese, from Qualitätswein to Grosses Gewächs: how ripeness, origin and sweetness combine on a German wine label.

German labels have a reputation for being difficult. The reason is that two different logics coexist on them: the traditional one, based on the ripeness of the grapes at harvest, and a more recent one, based on origin, which draws closer to the Burgundian model. Sweetness terms are added on top. Understanding these layers makes it possible to read with considerable precision what is inside the bottle.

The general framework

The German Wine Act of 1971 established the structure that, with reforms, remains in force. Germany has thirteen quality wine regions, called Anbaugebiete, among them Mosel, Rheingau, Rheinhessen, Pfalz, Nahe, Franken and Baden. Within them there are districts (Bereiche), villages and named vineyards (Lagen).

The basic categories, from least to most demanding, are:

  • Deutscher Wein: wine without a geographical indication, formerly called Tafelwein.
  • Landwein: the equivalent of a PGI, from a broad area.
  • Qualitätswein: quality wine from one of the thirteen regions, corresponding to the European PDO.
  • Prädikatswein: quality wine with a ripeness designation, also a PDO and subject to stricter requirements.

All Qualitätswein and Prädikatswein undergo official analysis and tasting and receive a control number, the Amtliche Prüfungsnummer or A.P.-Nr., shown on the label.

The Prädikate: the ripeness scale

Ripeness is measured by must weight, expressed in degrees Oechsle, which reflect mainly the sugar content of the grapes. Each designation requires a minimum that varies by region and variety. Unlike other categories, Prädikatswein may not be chaptalised, that is, sugar may not be added to the must to raise the alcohol level. The six designations are:

  • Kabinett: ripe grapes from the normal harvest; usually light wines of moderate alcohol.
  • Spätlese: literally “late harvest”; riper grapes.
  • Auslese: selected, very ripe bunches, sometimes partly affected by noble rot.
  • Beerenauslese (BA): individually selected berries, overripe or affected by Botrytis cinerea; sweet wines.
  • Trockenbeerenauslese (TBA): berries shrivelled by botrytis; very sweet, concentrated and rare wines.
  • Eiswein: grapes harvested and pressed while frozen on the vine, with a must weight at least equal to that of a Beerenauslese.

It is important to understand that these designations indicate the ripeness of the grapes, not the sweetness of the finished wine. A Spätlese can be dry or sweet, depending on how fermentation was managed. Only BA, TBA and Eiswein are, in practice, always sweet.

Sweetness terms

To know whether a wine is dry, you need to look for other words on the label:

  • trocken: dry, with low residual sugar, as regulated by European rules.
  • halbtrocken: off-dry.
  • feinherb: a traditional term with no precise legal definition, usually indicating a wine with some perceptible sugar balanced by acidity.
  • lieblich or süß: clearly sweet wines.

When none of these terms appears on a Kabinett, Spätlese or Auslese, it is often a wine with residual sweetness in the traditional style, particularly in the Mosel, where riesling's high acidity balances the sugar.

Classification by origin

From the late twentieth century, many producers, grouped above all in the VDP association (Verband Deutscher Prädikatsweingüter), promoted a classification based on place. Its pyramid has four levels:

  • Gutswein: estate wine of regional character.
  • Ortswein: village wine.
  • Erste Lage: classified first-class vineyard.
  • Grosse Lage: top-class vineyard.

Dry wine from a Grosse Lage is called Grosses Gewächs (GG) and is identified by that term on the label. In the VDP system, the terms Kabinett, Spätlese or Auslese are in principle reserved for wines with sweetness, while top dry wines are identified by their origin.

National legislation has moved in a similar direction. A reform of the Wine Act passed in 2021 reinforced the principle that the smaller and more precise the stated origin, the stricter the rules, with a pyramid running from regional wine to village wine to single-vineyard wine, and with a transition period for its application.

A classic trap: the Grosslage

The 1971 law also created Grosslagen, groupings of many vineyards under one name which, on the label, looks exactly like an individual vineyard: village name followed by site name. For decades this allowed wines from broad areas to pass for single-site wines. The recent reform has sought to limit the problem, and the term should not be confused with the VDP's Grosse Lage, which means the opposite: a single top-ranking vineyard.

How to read a German label

In practice it is worth looking for four elements: the region (Anbaugebiet), the most precise origin (village and vineyard), the ripeness designation if there is one, and the sweetness indication. The grape variety, very often riesling but also spätburgunder (pinot noir), silvaner or müller-thurgau, is usually shown prominently. With these keys you can work out whether the wine will be light or concentrated, dry or sweet, and from a broad area or a single plot.
