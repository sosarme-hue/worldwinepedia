Oenology & science

How rosé wine is made

Rosé is made from red grapes whose juice spends only a short time in contact with the skins; the length of that contact and the method chosen determine its colour, aroma and structure.

Rosé wine occupies a middle ground between white and red, but it is not simply a blend of the two. In the vast majority of cases it is made from red grapes whose juice stays in contact with the skins just long enough to take on a light tint and pick up some aromas, after which it ferments as if it were a white wine. The result keeps the freshness and liveliness of white wines while retaining some of the fruity character of red varieties. The range of colours it can reach, from an almost imperceptible pink to a deep raspberry hue, reflects the decisions made in the winery.

Where the colour comes from

As with red wines, the colour comes from the anthocyanins in the skin of red grapes. The pulp of almost all varieties is colourless, so the juice only becomes coloured when it comes into contact with the skins. The depth of colour depends on several factors: the variety, whose skin may be more or less rich in pigments; the ripeness of the grapes; the temperature, since warmth speeds up extraction; and above all the length of contact. A maceration of a few hours gives pale rosés, while one of a day or two produces deeply coloured rosés approaching light reds.

Direct pressing

The method that yields the palest rosés is direct pressing. The red grapes are pressed whole or after a light crushing, just as white grapes would be, and the juice is separated from the skins straight away. The brief contact that occurs during pressing itself is enough to give it a faint colour. This method, closely associated with Provence, produces delicate wines with subtle aromas and shades ranging from onion skin to salmon. It requires healthy fruit and very gentle pressing, because any excess pressure brings colour and bitterness.

Short maceration

In the maceration method, the grapes are destemmed, crushed and placed in a tank, where the juice remains in contact with the skins for a period that typically ranges from a few hours to a couple of days, usually at low temperature so that fermentation does not begin. Once the desired colour is reached, the juice is drawn off and fermented without the skins. This approach gives rosés with a brighter colour, more pronounced fruit aromas and a little more body. It is the traditional method in many Spanish regions; the Garnacha rosés of Navarra are a well-known example.

Bleeding the tank

The bleeding method, known in French as saignée, involves drawing off part of the juice from a tank intended for red wine, shortly after maceration has begun. This lightly coloured juice is then fermented separately as rosé. The procedure has a twofold purpose: to obtain a rosé and, at the same time, to increase the proportion of skins to liquid in the red wine tank, which gives the red more concentration. Saignée rosés tend to be deeper in colour and structure, although their quality depends on the grapes having been picked with the rosé in mind, and not just the red.

Blending white and red wine

Mixing white wine with red wine is another way of obtaining a pink colour, but in the European Union this practice is prohibited for still rosé wines. It is permitted for certain sparkling wines and is common in rosé Champagne, where a small proportion of local red wine is added to the white base wine before the second fermentation. Outside Europe the rules vary from country to country.

A different category consists of wines made from a mixture of white and red grapes that ferment or macerate together. In some Spanish areas, such as Cigales, the term clarete has traditionally referred to wines of this kind, although its legal definition has changed over time and it should not be confused with rosé in the strict sense.

Fermentation

Once separated from the skins, rosé juice is treated with the same care as white juice. It is settled to remove solid particles and fermented at a controlled, generally low, temperature to preserve aromas of red fruit, citrus and flowers. Rosés are particularly sensitive to oxidation, which can dull their colour and give them premature orange tints, so they are protected with sulphur dioxide and kept away from air.

Most rosés do not undergo malolactic fermentation, so as to keep a lively acidity. They are bottled in the months following the harvest and are meant to be drunk young. There are, however, rosés fermented or aged in barrel, with lees work, which have greater complexity and can develop in bottle for a few years.

Varieties and styles

Almost any red variety can make a rosé, but some are particularly well suited. Garnacha, with its generous fruit and moderate colour, is one of the most widely used in Spain and southern France. Cinsault brings delicacy and freshness to Provençal rosés, while Syrah, Monastrell, Tempranillo and Bobal are also frequently used. Each leaves its own imprint: notes of strawberry, cherry, raspberry, grapefruit or herbs.

Rosé styles have changed over recent decades. Consumers in many markets have shown a preference for pale colours, influenced by the Provençal model, and many wineries have adapted their techniques to produce lighter wines. It is worth remembering, however, that colour is not an indicator of quality: a deeply coloured, well-made rosé can be just as fine and balanced as a pale one, and often has more body to accompany food.

A demanding craft

Although it is sometimes regarded as a simple wine, rosé demands great precision. Within a few hours the winemaker must decide the exact moment to separate the juice from the skins, and an error in either direction changes the wine entirely. Added to this is the need to protect its freshness from oxygen and heat throughout production. That is why the best rosés reflect both the quality of the fruit and the care taken in the cellar.
