Oenology & science

The chemistry of wine aroma

From the terpenes of Muscat to the peppery note of Syrah: which molecules explain the primary, secondary and tertiary aromas of wine.

When a taster talks about lychee, green pepper, butter or vanilla, they are not only using metaphors: many of those associations correspond to specific molecules that chemists have identified in wine. Hundreds of volatile compounds are known in wine, although only some of them, present above their perception threshold, actually contribute to what we smell. Some act at tiny concentrations, in the order of nanograms per litre, which gives an idea of how sensitive our sense of smell is.

A useful classification

For convenience, wine aromas are grouped into three families according to their origin. Primary or varietal aromas come from the grape. Secondary aromas arise during fermentation. Tertiary aromas, also called bouquet, develop during oak ageing and bottle maturation. The division is a teaching tool: in practice, many varietal aromas are only released thanks to yeasts, and ageing transforms both grape-derived and fermentation aromas.

Aromas from the grape

Terpenes such as linalool and geraniol contribute floral and citrus notes. They are abundant in the Muscat family and in Gewürztraminer, and present in smaller amounts in Riesling and Albariño. In the grape some are free and some are bound to sugars in an odourless form; enzymes from the grape and from yeasts can release them.

Methoxypyrazines, especially isobutyl methoxypyrazine, smell of green pepper and grass. They are found in Sauvignon Blanc, Cabernet Sauvignon, Cabernet Franc and Carmenère. Their concentration is higher in under-ripe or heavily shaded grapes and falls as bunches are exposed to light. Canopy management therefore influences how green these wines taste.

Varietal thiols, such as 3-mercaptohexanol and its acetate or 4-mercapto-4-methylpentan-2-one, evoke grapefruit, passion fruit or boxwood. They are highly characteristic of Sauvignon Blanc, although they occur in other varieties too. In the grape they exist as odourless precursors that yeasts convert into aromatic molecules during fermentation.

Norisoprenoids derive from the breakdown of carotenoids. Beta-damascenone recalls baked apple and stewed fruit, and beta-ionone recalls violets. A famous case is TDN, responsible for the so-called kerosene or petrol aroma found in some Riesling with age, particularly from sunny climates.

Rotundone, a sesquiterpene identified early this century, is the molecule behind the black-pepper note of many cool-climate Syrah wines; it is also found in other varieties such as Grüner Veltliner. Curiously, a proportion of people cannot perceive it at all.

Aromas from fermentation

Yeasts produce a large number of volatile compounds. Esters are mainly responsible for the fruity aroma of young wines: isoamyl acetate recalls banana and pear drops, while other ethyl esters evoke apple, strawberry or pineapple. Cool fermentations tend to preserve more of these esters, hence the very fruity profile of many modern whites and rosés. Over time some of them are hydrolysed and the wine loses that exuberant fruit.

Higher alcohols, such as isoamyl alcohol, have heavier smells, reminiscent of solvent or fusel, and in small doses add complexity. Diacetyl, formed mainly during malolactic fermentation, brings buttery notes. Sulphur compounds are another important group: hydrogen sulphide and some mercaptans cause reduction odours of rotten egg or cabbage, while other sulphur molecules, at very low levels, can recall flint or a struck match and are considered part of the style of some whites.

Aromas from ageing

Oak releases many compounds into wine. Vanillin gives vanilla; whisky lactones give coconut notes, more marked in American oak; eugenol recalls clove; guaiacol and other volatile phenols bring smoke and toast, depending on the barrel's toast level. Furfural and other derivatives of wood sugars recall caramel and toasted almond.

Ageing transforms the whole picture. In oxidatively aged wines such as oloroso, compounds like sotolon appear, smelling of walnut, curry or fenugreek; it is also found in some aged botrytised sweet wines. In biological ageing under a film of flor, acetaldehyde provides the pungent, apple-like character typical of fino and manzanilla. In bottle, with little oxygen, reds develop notes of forest floor, leather, tobacco or truffle, and whites develop honey, nuts or toast.

How we perceive aroma

The smell of a wine is not the sum of its molecules. Compounds interact: some mask others, some enhance each other, and alcohol, sugar or polyphenols alter their volatility. We also smell in two ways: orthonasally, when we bring our nose to the glass, and retronasally, when the wine warms in the mouth and volatiles rise to the nose from the throat. Much of what we call flavour is in fact retronasal aroma.

Serving temperature has a direct influence: a wine that is too cold releases fewer volatiles and seems closed; one that is too warm makes the alcohol stand out. Individual sensitivity also varies: detection thresholds differ widely between people, and experience and training help us recognise and name aromas, though not to perceive what our own sense of smell cannot pick up.

A developing science

Aroma chemistry is a lively field. Techniques such as gas chromatography coupled with olfactometry, in which an analyst sniffs compounds as they are separated, have made it possible to identify key molecules present in minute quantities. Each discovery helps growers and winemakers understand how their decisions in the vineyard and the cellar translate into what we find in the glass.
