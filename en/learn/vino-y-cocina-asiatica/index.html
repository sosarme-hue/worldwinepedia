Food pairing

Wine and Asian cuisine

Chilli heat, umami, sweetness and aromatic spices make Asian cuisines a demanding field for wine; this article explains what happens on the palate and which styles tend to work best with Chinese, Japanese, Thai, Indian or Korean food.

Asia does not have one cuisine but many, and the distance between the delicacy of a Japanese dish and the intensity of a South Indian curry is as great as between any two European cuisines. Even so, many of the continent's culinary traditions share certain features that shape wine pairing: the use of chilli heat, an abundance of umami from fermented sauces, a balance of sweet, sour and salty within a single dish, and a generous use of aromatic herbs and spices. None of these cuisines developed with grape wine in mind, so traditional pairings with it are recent; but understanding how these flavours behave makes it possible to find very satisfying matches.

Chilli, alcohol and tannins

The burning sensation of chillies is due mainly to capsaicin, which activates heat-sensitive receptors. Alcohol intensifies that sensation, and tannins make it harsher. This is why powerful, tannic, high-alcohol reds are generally a poor choice for very spicy dishes. Wines of moderate alcohol, with generous fruit and often a light touch of residual sugar, work better: an off-dry Riesling from the Mosel, for example, soothes the heat while its acidity keeps things fresh. Fruity rosés and light, low-tannin reds served slightly cool are reasonable alternatives.

Umami and soy sauce

Soy sauce, fish sauce, miso and dried mushrooms bring a great deal of umami. This taste tends to make red wines seem more bitter and tannic, and heavily oaked whites heavy. A little salt and acidity in the dish partly offsets the effect, but in practice wines with good acidity, clean fruit and soft tannins adapt better. Dry sparkling wines and dry fortified wines such as fino or manzanilla are among the most effective partners for umami-rich dishes.

Japanese cuisine

Traditional Japanese cooking seeks purity of ingredients and subtlety. With sushi and sashimi it is best to avoid aromatically overwhelming wines and to choose dry, precise whites with lively acidity: a Chablis, an Austrian Grüner Veltliner, an Albariño or a brut sparkling wine. Soy sauce and wasabi add salt and heat, so a light, fresh wine is a safer bet than a tannic one. With tempura, sparkling wines are once again excellent for their ability to cleanse the palate of frying. With glazed grilled dishes, such as yakitori chicken with tare sauce, a light Pinot Noir or a Gamay can work well.

Chinese cuisine

Chinese cooking covers very different regional traditions, from the gentler Cantonese style to the cooking of Sichuan, famous for its heat and for the tingling sensation of its peppercorns. With Cantonese dim sum, a sparkling wine or a dry aromatic white suits the range of steamed and fried morsels. Peking duck, with its crisp skin and slightly sweet sauce, is often paired with Pinot Noir, whose acidity and red fruit get on well with the duck's fat. For Sichuan dishes, a Gewürztraminer or a Riesling with some residual sugar is more pleasant than a structured red.

Thai and Southeast Asian cuisine

Thai cooking combines heat, sourness, sweetness and saltiness with fresh herbs such as lemongrass, coriander, Thai basil and kaffir lime leaf. Here aromatic whites find natural ground. A Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc, with its marked acidity and herbal and tropical notes, goes well with salads and dishes featuring lime and coriander. Coconut-milk curries call for whites with some texture and a little fruit, such as an off-dry Loire Chenin Blanc or an Alsace Pinot Gris. With Vietnamese food, fresher and full of raw herbs, light whites and dry rosés are good options.

Indian cuisine

The cuisines of India are characterised by complex spice blends, often creamy sauces and widely varying levels of heat. With spiced but mild dishes, such as a korma or tandoori chicken, aromatic whites such as Gewürztraminer or Viognier, or fruity rosés, work well. With hotter preparations, it is once again wise to choose wines low in alcohol with some sweetness. Light, fruity reds can accompany milder lamb dishes. Many diners nonetheless prefer to alternate wine with water, a sensible habit when the heat is intense.

Korean cuisine

Korean barbecue, with meats marinated in sweet and salty sauces and cooked at the table, and fermented side dishes such as kimchi, present another challenge. Fruity, juicy reds with soft tannins, such as a Beaujolais, suit the meats; with sour, spicy kimchi, a Riesling or a dry sparkling wine usually works better.

Practical rules

As general guidance, it is best to prefer wines with lively acidity and moderate alcohol, to look for fruit and aroma rather than oak and structure, and not to fear a touch of sweetness when the dish is spicy. Dry sparkling wines, thanks to their versatility, are a safe bet when the table brings together many different dishes. And, as with any pairing, personal experience is the best guide.
