Viticulture

Hand harvesting and machine harvesting

How the moment of harvest is decided, what distinguishes picking by hand from picking by machine, and what each method means for the wine.

Harvest is the moment when a whole year's work in the vineyard comes to fruition. For a few weeks, the grapes must be picked at just the right point and reach the winery in the best possible condition. Two main methods coexist today: hand harvesting, practised since antiquity, and machine harvesting, which spread in the second half of the twentieth century. Neither is good or bad in itself; each meets different needs.

Deciding when to pick

Before choosing how to harvest, one must decide when. In the preceding weeks the grower follows the grapes' development through sampling and laboratory analyses that measure sugar, acidity and pH. But the sugar figure is not enough: so-called phenolic ripeness is also assessed, by tasting the berries and observing the colour of the pips, the texture of the skins and how easily the pulp comes away.

The final date is a compromise. Picking early preserves acidity and freshness but can produce green wines or hard tannins; waiting brings riper aromas, at the risk of losing acidity, of alcohol rising too high, or of rain and rot ruining the crop. On many estates each plot, and even each variety, is picked at a different time.

Hand harvesting

When picking by hand, teams of harvesters cut the bunches with secateurs or small knives and place them in buckets or crates. It is hard work that needs many people over a short period, but it offers important advantages:

  • Selection at the vine: pickers can reject rotten, unripe or damaged bunches.
  • Whole bunches: the fruit arrives intact, which is essential for certain winemaking methods such as carbonic maceration or whole-bunch pressing.
  • Access to any terrain: it is the only option on terraces, narrow ledges and very steep slopes, as well as in bush-trained vineyards or those with very old, irregular vines.

To keep the grapes from being crushed or from starting to ferment or oxidise too soon, small stackable crates are often used and the fruit is taken quickly to the winery. Many producers add a sorting table, where leaves, faulty bunches and damaged berries are removed by hand.

Some appellations require hand harvesting. This is the case in Champagne, where the bunches must reach the press whole, and in Beaujolais, whose traditional reds are made with whole bunches. It is also the rule for most noble-rot sweet wines, which are picked in successive passes through the vineyard, choosing the berries or bunches that have reached the desired stage.

Machine harvesting

Modern harvesters are machines that straddle the row of vines. Vibrating rods shake the canopy and dislodge the berries, which fall onto a conveyor belt; fans remove most of the leaves and the fruit is collected in hoppers. The bunch is not picked whole: the stems usually remain on the vine.

The advantages are clear:

  • Speed: a machine can harvest in a few hours what would take a team of pickers days, so each plot can be picked at its optimum moment.
  • Night harvesting: working at night, when the grapes are cool, helps preserve aromas and reduces the risk of oxidation and spontaneous fermentation, which is particularly useful in warm climates.
  • Cost and availability: it reduces dependence on a workforce that is increasingly hard to find in many areas.

It does, however, require suitable vineyards: vines trained on trellises, regular rows, ground with only a moderate slope and enough room for the machine to turn.

Differences in quality

For a long time machine harvesting was considered to give clearly inferior results: early machines broke many berries, collected leaves and other plant debris and encouraged oxidation. Modern harvesters are far gentler, and some include optical sorting or on-board destemming. In healthy, well-managed vineyards, many quality producers use them without any noticeable harm to the wine.

The main differences still lie in selection, which is harder by machine if the fruit is not healthy; in the integrity of the berries, which matters a great deal for delicate whites or when whole bunches are wanted; and in logistics: machine-picked fruit, already partly broken, must reach the winery as soon as possible and be protected from oxidation.

A choice shaped by wine and place

In practice, the choice depends on the style of wine, the terrain, the size of the estate and the availability of labour. Large, flat vineyards producing wines for early drinking often rely on machines, whereas mountain vineyards, such as those of Ribeira Sacra or the Douro, and wines that require whole bunches or meticulous selection depend on the hands of pickers. Many producers combine both methods depending on the plot.

Beyond the technique, harvest retains strong cultural value. In many districts it is still a time of shared work, of festivals and traditions that mark the village calendar, and the end of picking is celebrated as the close of a cycle that will begin again with winter pruning.
