Viticulture

The annual cycle of the vine

From winter dormancy to harvest: the stages the vine goes through every year, the grower's work and the risks of each phase.

The vine is a woody, climbing, deciduous plant that lives for many decades, yet it repeats a very precise cycle every year. Knowing this cycle is essential to understanding why vintage matters so much: a spring frost, a rainy flowering or an overly hot summer all leave their mark on the wine. The dates below refer to the northern hemisphere; in the southern hemisphere the calendar shifts by about six months, so harvest takes place between February and April.

Winter dormancy

After the leaves fall in autumn, the vine enters dormancy. Its visible activity stops and the plant survives on the starch reserves stored in its wood and roots during the previous year. The buds, protected by scales, already contain in miniature the shoots and future bunches of the next season. The European vine tolerates moderate winter cold well, but very severe frosts can damage buds and trunks.

Winter is the time for pruning, the most decisive job of the year. By removing most of the previous year's wood, the grower decides how many buds to leave and thereby controls the crop load, the shape of the plant and its balance. In areas at risk of late frost, some producers prune late in order to delay budbreak as well.

Bleeding and budbreak

At the end of winter, as the soil warms, the roots become active again and push sap towards the aerial parts. Drops of sap ooze from the pruning cuts: this is bleeding, a sign that the plant is waking up. Shortly afterwards, when the mean temperature is around 10 °C, the buds swell and open, showing a cottony down before the first leaves emerge. This is budbreak, which usually happens between March and April depending on variety and location.

Varieties do not all wake up at once. Early-budding varieties such as Chardonnay are more exposed to spring frosts, which can destroy tender shoots in a single night. Growers fight them with wind machines, burners, water sprinklers or by choosing hillside plots, since cold air tends to collect on valley floors.

Shoot growth

During spring, the shoots grow quickly, sometimes several centimetres a day in favourable conditions. Leaves, tendrils and inflorescences, the future bunches, appear. The grower removes unnecessary shoots and begins to train the foliage. This is also when fungal diseases start to be monitored, especially downy mildew, favoured by rain and warmth, and powdery mildew, which develops in mild, humid weather even without rain.

Flowering and fruit set

Towards the end of spring, roughly two months after budbreak, comes flowering. Vine flowers are small, greenish and inconspicuous; in cultivated varieties they are usually hermaphrodite and self-pollinating. The cap covering each flower falls away, the stamens are exposed and fertilisation takes place.

Fertilised flowers turn into berries: this is fruit set. If the weather is cold, rainy or very windy at this stage, fruit set can be poor. Growers speak of coulure when many flowers drop without forming fruit, and of millerandage when the bunch mixes normal berries with small, seedless ones. Both reduce the crop, although the latter can concentrate the wine.

Berry development and veraison

In summer the hard green berries grow, accumulating mainly acids, especially malic and tartaric. Later comes veraison, one of the most visible moments of the cycle: black grapes change from green to red and purple, white grapes become translucent and yellowish, and the skin softens. From then on the berry starts to accumulate sugars, acidity falls, especially malic acid, and colour compounds, tannins and aroma precursors develop.

At this stage the grower may remove leaves to ventilate the bunches or, on the contrary, keep the leaves to shade them from the sun. Some also carry out green harvesting, removing part of the bunches to balance the crop load. Moderate water stress can favour concentration, but severe drought stalls ripening.

Ripening and harvest

Ripening lasts several weeks. During this time sugar content, acidity and pH are measured, and increasingly the grapes are tasted to assess the ripeness of skins and seeds. The harvest date depends on the style sought: grapes with higher acidity for sparkling wines, full ripeness for structured reds, over-ripeness or noble rot for some sweet wines. In the northern hemisphere harvest is concentrated between late August and October, although some ice wines or late-harvest wines are picked later.

Late summer and autumn also bring risks: rain that dilutes the grapes and encourages grey rot, caused by Botrytis cinerea, hail, or attacks by insects and birds.

Lignification and leaf fall

While the grapes ripen, the shoots stop growing and turn woody and brown. This lignification, known in French as aoûtement, enables them to withstand winter. After harvest the plant keeps photosynthesising as long as it has leaves, storing reserves for the following year's start. With the first cold weather the leaves change colour, yellow in many white varieties and reddish in many black ones, and fall. The cycle closes and dormancy begins again.

Climate change and the calendar

In many wine regions, the stages of the cycle, particularly veraison and harvest, have been observed to occur earlier over recent decades, in connection with rising temperatures. This forces growers to rethink practices, varieties and picking dates, a subject covered in the article on viticulture and climate change.
