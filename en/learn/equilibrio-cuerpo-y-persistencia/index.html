Tasting

Balance, body and length

Three key concepts for assessing a wine on the palate: how acidity, alcohol, tannin and sugar relate to one another, what is meant by body, and how the length of the finish is judged.

When a taster describes a wine on the palate, they seldom just list flavours. What interests them most is its structure: how its components combine, what sense of weight it leaves and how long its impression lingers after swallowing or spitting. Balance, body and length are three central concepts for judging a wine's quality, beyond personal taste.

The components of structure

Several elements come into play on the palate, and it helps to identify them separately before judging the whole:

  • Acidity, felt mainly along the sides of the tongue, which makes the mouth water. It brings freshness, tension and liveliness.
  • Alcohol, which gives warmth, a certain sweetness and volume. In excess, it produces a burning impression on the finish.
  • Tannins, found mainly in reds, which are not a taste but a tactile sensation: they dry the gums and tongue and create astringency. Both their quantity and their quality are assessed: they may be fine and silky or coarse and drying.
  • Sugar, which in sweet or off-dry wines softens acidity and adds richness.
  • Other elements, such as slight bitterness, carbon dioxide in sparkling wines, or the salty sensation some tasters describe in certain wines.

Balance

A wine is balanced when none of its components dominates the others in an unpleasant way. The acidity supports without being sharp, the alcohol gives body without burning, the tannins provide structure without excessive drying, and any sweetness is offset by freshness.

Balance works like a system of weights and counterweights. Sweetness and alcohol tend to soften the perception of acidity and tannin; acidity and tannin, in turn, make a wine seem less sweet and less heavy. That is why a great noble-rot sweet wine needs high acidity to avoid being cloying, and a powerful red needs freshness so as not to seem heavy.

There is no single formula for balance: a cool-climate white can be very acidic and perfectly balanced, because its lightness goes with that acidity, while a warm-climate red can carry high alcohol unnoticed if its concentration absorbs it. Balance can also change over time in a young wine: firm tannins may soften over the years, although a wine lacking fruit or acidity is unlikely to gain balance in the bottle.

Body

Body describes the sensation of weight, volume and density of the wine in the mouth. It is often compared with the difference between drinking skimmed milk, whole milk or cream: light-bodied wines slip down easily, while full-bodied ones fill the mouth.

Several factors contribute to body:

  • Alcohol, probably the most important: all else being equal, a more alcoholic wine seems fuller.
  • Dry extract, that is, all the non-volatile substances —glycerol, phenolic compounds, polysaccharides, minerals— that remain dissolved.
  • Residual sugar, which adds density.
  • Winemaking: lees ageing, oak or extended maceration can increase the sense of volume.

Body is not in itself a measure of quality. A light wine can be magnificent and a very full one coarse. What matters is that the body suits the style and is accompanied by enough freshness and flavour.

Length

Length, also called the finish or persistence, is the time during which a wine's aromatic sensations remain in the mouth after swallowing or spitting. It is one of the quality indicators most valued by tasters.

To measure it, tasters count the seconds that pleasant sensations last via the retronasal route, that is, the aromas rising from the mouth to the nose. This aromatic length must be distinguished from the mere duration of tactile or burning sensations, such as astringency or the heat of alcohol, which are not considered part of a quality finish. In the French tradition, each second of aromatic persistence is sometimes called a caudalie.

As a rough guide, a finish of a few seconds is considered short; one that clearly lingers after swallowing, medium; and one that remains distinct for a long while, long. More important than the exact figure is that the finish should be clean, consistent with what came before and, ideally, evolving to reveal new nuances.

Intensity and complexity

Two further concepts are usually added to these three. Flavour intensity indicates how strongly aromas are perceived on the palate, and should not be confused with body: a light Riesling may have very intense flavours. Complexity refers to the range of nuances and their capacity to change in the glass.

An overall assessment

Many tasting systems use these criteria to judge quality: balance, intensity, complexity and length. A wine that combines them is usually memorable, whatever its style or price. Learning to perceive them requires attention and practice, but it makes it possible to go beyond a simple “I like it” or “I don't like it” and understand why a wine is harmonious or, on the contrary, out of balance.
