Sparkling wine production methods

An overview of the main ways of putting bubbles into wine: the traditional method, the Charmat or Martinotti method, the ancestral method, the transfer method and carbonation, with their technical and stylistic differences.

The bubbles in a sparkling wine are dissolved carbon dioxide. When yeasts convert sugar into alcohol they also release carbon dioxide; if fermentation takes place in a sealed vessel, the gas cannot escape and remains dissolved in the liquid, building up pressure. When the bottle is opened, the pressure drops and the gas forms bubbles. All methods for making quality sparkling wine rely on this principle, but they differ on one key point: where and how the gas-producing fermentation takes place. That difference affects the size and persistence of the bubbles, the aromas of the wine, and the time and cost of production.

The base wine

Most methods start from a base wine, a still wine made from grapes harvested with high acidity and moderate potential alcohol, since the second fermentation will add a little more alcohol. The grapes are usually pressed very gently, often as whole bunches, to obtain clean, lightly coloured juice. In many cases wines from different plots, varieties or vintages are blended to achieve a consistent style.

The traditional method

In the traditional method, also called the classic method or, in its region of origin, méthode champenoise, the second fermentation takes place inside the very bottle that will reach the consumer. A mixture of sugar and yeast known as the liqueur de tirage is added to the base wine, and the bottle is sealed, usually with a crown cap. Fermentation produces alcohol, gas and a sediment of dead yeast cells.

The wine then rests on these lees for months or years. Yeast autolysis, the breakdown of the yeast cells, contributes aromas of bread, pastry or nuts and helps create fine, well-integrated bubbles. To remove the sediment, the bottles are gradually tilted neck-down, either by hand in wooden racks or by machines called gyropalettes, until the lees collect against the cap. The neck is then frozen, the bottle is opened and the pressure expels the sediment: this is disgorgement. Finally the liqueur d'expédition, which determines the level of sweetness, is added and the definitive cork is inserted.

This method is used for Champagne, Cava, Franciacorta, French crémants and many other sparkling wines. It is slow and labour-intensive, but it yields wines of great complexity that can age well.

The transfer method

The transfer method shares with the traditional method the second fermentation in bottle and ageing on lees, but it avoids riddling and individual disgorgement. Once ageing is complete, the contents of the bottles are emptied under pressure into a tank, filtered to remove the sediment, adjusted for sweetness and rebottled. It is common for very small or very large bottle formats, in which riddling is impractical, and in some New World production.

The Charmat or Martinotti method

In the Charmat method, the second fermentation takes place in large sealed stainless-steel tanks able to withstand pressure, known in Italy as autoclaves. The process is associated with Federico Martinotti, who developed it in Italy in the late nineteenth century, and with Eugène Charmat, who refined and popularised it in France in the early twentieth century; hence its two names.

Once fermentation is complete, the wine is filtered and bottled under pressure. The process is much quicker and cheaper than the traditional method, and contact with the lees is usually brief, although there are versions with extended ageing in tank. The result is fresh sparkling wine with very pronounced primary aromas of fruit and flowers, ideal for aromatic varieties. It is the characteristic method for Prosecco, made from Glera, and for many Lambruscos.

The ancestral method

The ancestral method is probably the oldest of all. There is no second fermentation: the wine is bottled before the first has finished, so the sugar remaining in the must continues to ferment in the bottle and produces the gas. The resulting wines usually have lower pressure, a certain cloudiness if the lees are not removed and, often, some residual sugar.

The French region of Limoux, in Languedoc, claims a long tradition of sparkling wines made in this way. In recent years the method has become popular worldwide under the name pétillant naturel, shortened to pét-nat, especially among producers interested in minimal intervention. The outcome is less predictable than with other methods, since control over fermentation in the bottle is limited.

The Asti method and other variants

An Italian variant, applied above all to the sweet Moscato sparkling wines of Piedmont, involves a single fermentation of the must in an autoclave, which is stopped by chilling and filtration when the wine reaches a low alcohol level and retains much of its sugar. The result is a light, aromatic, sweet wine in which the fruit of the grape is preserved almost intact. In the Rhône valley, the Die area uses a comparable process with a single fermentation in bottle.

Carbonation

The simplest way of making a wine sparkle is to inject it with carbon dioxide, as is done with fizzy drinks. The resulting bubbles are coarser and dissipate quickly. This process is reserved for inexpensive wines, and in the European Union wines produced in this way must say so on the label and may not use the names of sparkling wines made by secondary fermentation.

Pressure, sweetness and terminology

Pressure distinguishes fully sparkling wines, which in the European Union must reach at least 3 bar, from semi-sparkling or frizzante wines, which have less. Final sweetness, which in the traditional method depends on the liqueur d'expédition, is expressed through terms such as brut nature, extra brut, brut, extra dry, dry, demi-sec and doux, whose sugar limits are set by European regulations.

Choosing a method

No method is inherently superior: each serves a purpose. The traditional method favours complexity and the aromas of lees ageing; Charmat, freshness and fruit; the ancestral method, a more rustic and spontaneous expression. The choice depends on the variety, the style sought and the tradition of each region, and it explains the enormous diversity of sparkling wines found in the world today.
