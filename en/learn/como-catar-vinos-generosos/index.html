Tasting

How to taste fortified wines

Keys to serving and tasting finos, manzanillas, amontillados, olorosos, Port, Madeira and other wines with added spirit, recognising the marks of biological and oxidative ageing.

Fortified wines form a broad and varied family: from a pale, pungent fino to an almost black Pedro Ximénez, by way of Ports, Madeiras and the vins doux naturels of southern France. What unites them is the addition of grape spirit at some point in production. Tasting them calls for a few adjustments compared with still wines, because their alcohol, concentration and ageing make them especially intense.

Two main ways of fortifying

To understand what is in the glass, it helps to know when the spirit was added:

  • After fermentation: the wine ferments to dryness and is then fortified. This is the case with dry sherries —fino, manzanilla, amontillado, oloroso, palo cortado. Any sweetness comes from later blending.
  • During fermentation: the spirit is added while unfermented sugar remains, stopping the yeasts and preserving the grape's natural sweetness. This is how Port, most Madeiras and vins doux naturels such as Banyuls or fortified Muscats are made.

In Montilla-Moriles, the Pedro Ximénez grape often reaches the necessary strength naturally, so many of its finos need no fortification.

Biological and oxidative ageing

The most important feature when tasting the wines of Jerez and Montilla is the type of ageing:

  • Biological: the wine ages under a veil of yeast, the flor, which protects it from oxygen and transforms it. It produces acetaldehyde and other compounds that give aromas of almond, bread dough, yeast, dried herbs and a pungent character. This is the ageing of fino and manzanilla, which stay pale.
  • Oxidative: without flor, the wine is slowly exposed to air through the wood. It darkens towards amber and mahogany and develops notes of walnut, toasted hazelnut, caramel, leather, tobacco or spice. This is the ageing of oloroso.

Amontillado combines the two: it begins as a fino and, when the flor dies away, continues with oxidative ageing. That is why it shows both the pungent finesse of biological ageing and the depth of oxidative ageing. Palo cortado is traditionally described as a wine with the delicate nose of an amontillado and the body of an oloroso.

Serving and choosing the glass

Temperature varies greatly by style:

  • Fino and manzanilla: well chilled, like a white wine, at around seven or eight degrees Celsius.
  • Amontillado and palo cortado: a little less cold, around twelve to fourteen degrees.
  • Oloroso, tawny Port and Madeira: between twelve and sixteen degrees, depending on the style.
  • Ruby and vintage Ports, Pedro Ximénez: cool, rather than at warm room temperature, so that alcohol and sweetness do not feel heavy.

For a long time the catavino, a small, narrow glass, was used. Today many professionals prefer a medium-sized white wine glass, which shows aromatic complexity better without concentrating the alcohol too much. Servings are usually small, both because of the intensity of these wines and because of their high alcohol.

Appearance

Colour is a very reliable clue. A fino or manzanilla should be pale and bright, from straw yellow to light gold. Amontillado tends towards amber or topaz; oloroso towards mahogany. Pedro Ximénez is almost opaque, with an iodine-coloured rim, and its density shows when the glass is swirled. Among Ports, ruby keeps deep red tones, while tawny, aged for a long time in wood, evolves towards brick and amber. Vintage Ports, which age in bottle, usually throw a sediment and are best decanted.

The nose

Because of their strength, these wines can seem spirity if swirled vigorously. It is advisable to smell first without swirling and then with a gentle movement, holding the glass a little away, so as not to overwhelm the nose.

In biologically aged wines, look for notes of almond, yeast and herbs; manzanilla, aged in Sanlúcar de Barrameda, is often described as having hints of dried chamomile and a saline sensation. Oxidative wines bring nuts, old wood, coffee or chocolate. Very old wines often show a slight volatile acidity that lifts the aromas and, in the right measure, is part of their character. Young Ports are dominated by ripe black fruit; tawnies by dried fruit, caramel and walnuts. Madeiras are recognisable by their combination of toasted, caramel and candied citrus notes with very lively acidity.

The palate

On the palate, the first thing to assess is the integration of the alcohol: in a good fortified wine, the alcohol provides warmth and structure but does not burn. Then comes the balance between sweetness, acidity and bitterness. A dry oloroso can be surprisingly dry and firm for someone expecting a sweet wine; a Pedro Ximénez needs some freshness to avoid being cloying; a quality Madeira stands out for a cutting acidity that balances even the sweetest styles.

Length is one of the most remarkable features of these wines. Long-aged fortified wines can leave a very long finish in which notes of nuts, wood and spice follow one another.

Keeping an opened bottle

Resistance to air varies greatly. Fino and manzanilla are fragile: once opened, they should be kept cold and drunk within a few days, like a white wine. Olorosos, tawny Ports and above all Madeiras, which have already undergone long oxidation, keep for much longer once opened without deteriorating.

A family to discover

Tasting fortified wines is one of the most instructive experiences for an enthusiast: in a few glasses one covers the whole spectrum of ageing, from the protection of the flor to prolonged oxidation. Their intensity invites enjoying them in small quantities, attentively and often with food, with which they show surprising versatility.
