Old vines: the value of time in the vineyard

What is meant by an old vine, how a vine changes with age, what it brings to the wine and why preserving these vineyards has become a cause in many regions.

The phrases “old vines”, “viñas viejas” and “vieilles vignes” appear on labels more and more often. They evoke gnarled vines with thick trunks that have come through decades of droughts, frosts and harvests. Behind that image lies an interesting agronomic reality, and also a fragile heritage that many regions are trying to protect.

What is an old vine?

There is no universal definition. In most countries the term is not regulated, so its use on a label depends on the judgement of each producer. As a rough guide, people tend to speak of old vines from around thirty or forty years of age, and of centenarian vines when they pass a hundred, although in many areas the term is reserved for much older plantings.

Some regions have taken steps to make the concept more precise. In the Barossa, in Australia, an old vine charter classifies plots by age band. In South Africa, an initiative devoted to old vines certifies vineyards of at least thirty-five years as heritage vineyards. In Spain, some regulatory councils and associations have begun to define their own criteria. Even so, the diversity of usage means the term should be treated with some caution.

How a vine ages

With age, a vine changes visibly and also in the way it functions:

  • Deeper, more extensive roots: an old vine explores a greater volume of soil and reaches deeper layers, making it more resistant to drought and more stable in the face of weather variations.
  • Lower vigour: it produces fewer shoots and leaves, and its growth is more moderate.
  • Low yields: it gives fewer bunches, often smaller ones with small berries. For decades this was the reason why many old vineyards were uprooted: they produced little and were barely profitable.
  • Reserves in the wood: the trunk and arms store reserves that help the plant cope with difficult years.

Over time, many vines die or stop producing, and old vineyards commonly have gaps where vines are missing, which are sometimes filled with young plants.

Do they make better wines?

Many producers and tasters associate old vines with more concentrated, deeper and more balanced wines, with a truer expression of place and notable consistency from vintage to vintage. These observations have a reasonable basis: low yields, slower ripening and a plant less sensitive to climatic extremes.

However, age alone does not guarantee quality. An old vine on a poor site or badly tended will not produce a great wine, and relatively young vines on good soils can give excellent results. Moreover, old vineyards usually share other features that influence the wine: they are often bush-trained, dry-farmed and planted on poor soils, and they frequently mix several varieties or different types of the same grape. Separating the effect of age from all these factors is not easy, and scientific research on the question remains limited.

A genetic heritage

Beyond the wine itself, old vines are highly valuable as a reservoir of diversity. Planted before commercial clones became widespread, they preserve a genetic variety that has been lost in many modern plantings. Minor or nearly extinct varieties have been identified in them, and they serve as a source for massal selection, which allows material from the best vines to be propagated.

Some of these plantings are also historical witnesses. On sandy soils or in isolated areas there are ungrafted vineyards dating from before phylloxera, and certain regions preserve traditional forms of training and cultivation that help us understand the viticulture of the past.

Regions with old vines

Old vineyards exist on every wine-growing continent. Among the best-known examples:

  • Garnacha and Cariñena in areas such as Priorat, Campo de Borja and other districts of the Ebro valley and the Mediterranean.
  • Zinfandel in California, where a society devoted to historic vineyards documents old plantings.
  • The País grape of southern Chile, in valleys such as Itata and Maule, often on very old, ungrafted, dry-farmed vines.
  • Chenin Blanc and other varieties in South African regions such as Swartland.
  • Shiraz, Grenache and other grapes in the Barossa and other parts of Australia, where the absence of phylloxera in some areas allowed very old vines to survive.

Preserving old vines

For much of the twentieth century, economic logic led to the uprooting of many old vineyards, replaced by more productive plantings or by other crops. Since the end of the last century, interest in wines of place and in diversity has changed perceptions: today these vineyards are regarded as heritage, and various associations, registers and programmes seek to catalogue them and to guarantee their owners a fair price for the grapes, so that keeping them remains viable.

The challenges are still considerable: low yields, intensive manual work, an ageing rural population and the effects of climate change. Protecting an old vineyard demands a long-term commitment, because once it has been uprooted, the time it held can never be recovered.
