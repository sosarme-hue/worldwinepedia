History & culture

The history of Tokaji

The sweet wine of Tokaj in north-eastern Hungary: its fame at the courts of Europe, the early delimitation of the region, its twentieth-century decline and recent revival.

Tokaji is the wine of the Tokaj region in north-eastern Hungary, in the foothills of the Zemplén mountains, where the Bodrog and Tisza rivers meet. Although dry wines and other styles are made there, its historical fame rests on a very particular sweet wine, aszú, made from grapes affected by noble rot. For centuries it was one of the most prized wines in Europe, and its history is bound up with that of the Hungarian state itself.

A favourable landscape

Tokaj brings together unusual conditions. Its hills of volcanic origin offer varied soils and well-exposed slopes. The nearby rivers encourage, in autumn, an alternation of morning mists and dry, sunny afternoons. This combination allows the fungus Botrytis cinerea to develop in its beneficial form, noble rot, which dehydrates the berries and concentrates sugar and acidity without destroying them.

The main varieties are Furmint, with very lively acidity, and the more aromatic Hárslevelű, joined by Sárgamuskotály, a small-berried Muscat, and other minor grapes.

The origins of aszú

Viticulture in the area is old, and medieval documents already mention vineyards. Exactly when aszú began to be made deliberately is not known. A widespread tradition credits its invention, in the seventeenth century, to a preacher named Máté Szepsi Laczkó, in the service of the Lorántffy family; according to the story, the harvest was delayed by the threat of an Ottoman attack, and the shrivelled grapes produced an extraordinary wine. Historians believe the method is probably older and that the legend simplifies a longer process, but the seventeenth century is in any case when aszú became established.

The traditional method

The classic way of making aszú is unique. The botrytised berries are picked one by one, in several passes through the vineyard, and gathered into a paste. That paste was then macerated in must or base wine, and the proportion was traditionally expressed in puttonyos, the name of the hods in which the grapes were carried: the more puttonyos added to a cask of base wine, the sweeter and more concentrated the result.

Alongside aszú there are other historic wines. Eszencia is the juice that runs from the botrytised berries under their own weight, extremely sweet and very slow to ferment. Szamorodni, whose name means something like "as it was born", is made from whole bunches containing both healthy and botrytised grapes, and may be dry or sweet.

The wines aged in long galleries dug into the rock, whose walls are covered by a characteristic mould that helps keep the air humid.

One of the first delimited regions

The wine's reputation made protection necessary. In the early eighteenth century, under Prince Francis II Rákóczi, vineyards were classified according to their quality. In 1737 a royal decree officially delimited the territory entitled to produce Tokaji. For this reason Tokaj ranks, together with the Douro and Chianti, among the oldest delimited wine regions in Europe.

The wine of kings

Between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries, Tokaji was a wine of prestige at the courts of Europe. It was exported to Poland, to Russia, where the imperial court valued it highly, to the Habsburg lands and to France. It was given the Latin phrase vinum regum, rex vinorum, wine of kings, king of wines, which tradition puts in the mouth of Louis XIV, although the attribution is not reliably documented. The Hungarian national anthem, written by Ferenc Kölcsey in 1823, mentions the nectar of the vines of Tokaj among the gifts of the homeland.

Crises of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries

In the late nineteenth century, phylloxera devastated the vineyards of Tokaj, which had to be replanted with grafted vines. Later, the world wars and border changes reshaped the region: after the First World War, a small part of the historic territory went to Czechoslovakia, and today there is a Tokaj area in Slovakia.

After the Second World War, the communist regime nationalised land and cellars. Production was concentrated in a large state enterprise and geared to volume and exports to the Soviet bloc. Many families lost their property and much traditional knowledge weakened, although a number of growers kept old practices alive.

The revival after 1989

With the fall of communism, Tokaj underwent a profound transformation. Foreign investors arrived, among them French houses and the Spanish winery Vega Sicilia, which founded Oremus; Hungarian estates were created or restored, and historic vineyards were brought back. Cellars were modernised and more precise wines were encouraged, with closer control of oxidation.

In 2002 UNESCO added the Tokaj Wine Region Historic Cultural Landscape to the World Heritage List. Production rules have been revised in recent years, and attention now goes not only to aszú but also to dry Furmint wines, which show the potential of the region's different vineyards.

A singular legacy

The history of Tokaji shows how a wine can become a national emblem. Its method of production, its early legal protection and its fame at court place it among the world's great sweet wines, alongside those of Sauternes or the ice wines of Germany and Austria, but with a historical personality all its own.
