Wine and barbecue

Smoke, the char of the embers, rubs and sauces all shape the wine for a barbecue; this article explains how to deal with them and surveys the great grilling traditions, from the Argentine asado to the South African braai.

Cooking over embers is probably humanity's oldest culinary technique, and in many countries the grill is much more than a way of cooking: it is a social ritual. The Argentine and Uruguayan asado, the South African braai, the Australian barbie and the American barbecue each have their own customs, cuts and sauces. From the point of view of wine, they all share certain features worth knowing about: intense flavours, smoky and charred notes, fat and, often, sweet or spicy sauces.

What the embers bring to the dish

When cooked at high temperature, the surface of meat or vegetables undergoes the Maillard reaction and caramelisation, which generate toasty, caramel and roast-meat aromas. Smoke from wood or charcoal adds smoky notes, and fat dripping onto the embers intensifies that character. The result is powerful flavours that can overwhelm an overly delicate wine. At the same time, grilled meats are usually rich in fat and protein, which softens the perception of tannins. This is why barbecue is one of the settings in which full-bodied, structured reds shine brightest.

Grilled red meat

A rib steak, an entrecôte or beef ribs cooked over embers call for reds with ripe fruit, body and noticeable tannins. Tannins bind to the protein and fat of the meat, and the wine cleanses the palate between mouthfuls. Wines made from cabernet sauvignon, syrah, tempranillo or malbec are classic choices. An oak-aged red can echo the toasty notes of the grill, although too much oak can feel heavy alongside the smoke.

The great grilling traditions

Some of the best-known pairings arise from each country's own history:

  • Argentina: the asado, with cuts of beef, offal and chorizo, is traditionally accompanied by Malbec, especially from Mendoza, whose juicy dark fruit and soft tannins suit the meat. Chimichurri, with herbs, garlic and vinegar, calls for a wine with good fruit and acidity.
  • Uruguay: tannat, the country's emblematic grape, with firm tannins, is the natural partner for the Uruguayan asado and its rich meats.
  • South Africa: at the braai, pinotage, a cross of pinot noir and cinsault created in the country, and the syrah and cabernet sauvignon reds of Stellenbosch are common choices.
  • Australia: syrah, known there as shiraz, from regions such as the Barossa Valley, with its ripe fruit and spicy notes, goes well with grilled meats.
  • United States: slow-cooked, smoked barbecue, with pork ribs, beef brisket or pulled pork, often with sweet sauces, is frequently associated with California's ripe, spicy Zinfandel.
  • Chile: carmenère, with its notes of pepper and spice, suits marinated and seasoned meats.

Sauces, marinades and spices

Barbecue sauces, often sweet, tangy and smoky, alter the balance. A sweet sauce makes a very tannic red seem harsher and more bitter; in that case reds with generous fruit and ripe tannins, such as Zinfandel, garnacha or warm-climate syrah, work better. Spicy marinades, with chilli or hot paprika, are accentuated by high alcohol: wines of moderate strength with plenty of fruit, or even a full-bodied rosé, are preferable. Aromatic herbs such as rosemary or thyme go naturally with Mediterranean reds.

Pork, chicken and sausages

Grilled pork and chicken, milder than beef, suit medium-bodied reds, structured rosés and even whites with some oak ageing, especially when served with creamy or citrus sauces. Grilled chorizo and sausages, fatty and spiced, go well with young, fruity reds such as a garnacha or a young tempranillo.

Grilled fish and vegetables

Not every barbecue is about meat. Grilled sardines, sea bream, sea bass or octopus suit fresh, aromatic whites such as Albariño, or dry rosés. Oily fish such as tuna, salmon or mackerel can take full-bodied rosés or light reds such as Pinot Noir. Grilled vegetables, such as peppers, aubergines, courgettes or mushrooms, gain sweetness and toasty notes that suit rosés and light, fruity reds.

Serving wine outdoors

Barbecues are usually held in hot weather, and wine suffers at high temperatures. A red served too warm seems alcoholic and heavy, so it is worth chilling it slightly, keeping it in the shade and, on very hot days, placing it for a while in an ice bucket with cool water. Whites and rosés should be kept cold without being ice-cold. As at any outdoor meal, it is important to alternate wine with water, especially in the heat, and to keep quantities moderate.
