Practical guides

What is a vintage?

What a wine's vintage means, why each year's weather shapes its character, which wines do not state one and how to interpret vintage assessments.

The vintage is the year in which the grapes used to make a wine were harvested. It often appears on the label and is one of the most useful pieces of information for understanding what is in the bottle, because the vine is very sensitive to weather and each year leaves a different mark on the grapes.

What exactly it indicates

The vintage is not the year of bottling or release, but the year of harvest. A red with several years of ageing may go on sale long after the vintage shown on its label. In the European Union, a wine may state a vintage if at least 85% of the grapes used were harvested in that year; the rest may come from other harvests, for example to make up volume or adjust balance.

In the northern hemisphere, the harvest normally takes place between late summer and autumn; in the southern hemisphere, between the late southern summer and autumn, roughly from February to April or May. An Argentine or Australian wine of a given vintage therefore comes from grapes picked several months earlier than a European wine of the same vintage.

Why wine changes from year to year

Throughout the annual cycle, the vine responds to temperature, rainfall, sunshine and extreme events. Some factors that shape a vintage are:

  • Spring frosts, which can destroy newly emerged shoots and greatly reduce the crop.
  • Rain and temperature during flowering, which affect fruit set and the size of the crop.
  • Summer heat and drought, which can speed up ripening, raise alcohol and lower acidity, or even cause the plant to shut down.
  • Hail, which damages leaves and bunches.
  • Rain close to harvest, which can dilute the grapes and encourage fungal disease.

A cool vintage tends to give wines with higher acidity and moderate alcohol, sometimes with less concentration; a warm vintage, riper, more alcoholic wines with sweeter fruit. Neither is necessarily better: it depends on the region, the grape and the style sought.

Climate and regions

The importance of vintage varies greatly from area to area. In regions with marginal or variable climates, such as Burgundy, Champagne, the Mosel or northern Spain, differences between years can be very marked. In regions with more consistent climates, with dry, warm summers almost every year, variations tend to be smaller, though they never disappear. Climate change, with more heatwaves, droughts and extreme events, has altered the traditional pattern of vintages in many areas.

The work of the grower and winemaker also counts. Vineyard management, the choice of picking date and grape selection can soften the effects of a difficult year, and in challenging vintages the producer's judgement often makes a great difference between one wine and another.

Wines without a vintage

Not all wines state a vintage. Some styles are based precisely on blending several years to maintain a consistent character:

  • Most champagnes and many sparkling wines are blends of several harvests, with reserve wines from previous years. They are called non-vintage. Houses make vintage champagne, or millésimé, only in years they consider suitable.
  • Sherries aged in the criaderas and solera system combine wines from many years, so they do not usually carry a vintage, although there are exceptions.
  • Tawny port with an indication of age, such as 10 or 20 years, expresses an average character, not a specific harvest. Vintage port, by contrast, comes from a single year, which houses “declare” only when the quality justifies it.
  • Many simple everyday wines are made by blending harvests to ensure consistency.

Vintage charts and assessments

Magazines, producer associations and regulatory councils often publish assessments of each region's vintages. They are useful guides but should be used with caution. They sum up in a single rating what happened across an entire region, when within it there may be significant differences between areas, varieties and producers. A vintage considered mediocre can yield excellent wines in the hands of a good winemaker, and pleasant wines to drink young while the great vintages mature.

Vintage and drinking

Knowing the vintage helps decide when to drink a wine. Wines from lighter vintages are usually ready sooner, while those from concentrated, structured vintages may need more time in bottle. For simple whites and rosés meant to be drunk young, the vintage serves mainly to check freshness: in general, the most recent are the best choice. And at a tasting, comparing the same wine across several vintages, known as a vertical tasting, is one of the clearest ways to see how each year's weather translates into the glass.
