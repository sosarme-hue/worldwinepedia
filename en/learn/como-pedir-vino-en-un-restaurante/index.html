How to order wine in a restaurant

How to read a wine list, what to ask the sommelier, what the ritual of presenting and tasting the bottle means, and when it is legitimate to send a wine back.

For many people, choosing wine in a restaurant brings a certain unease: long lists, unfamiliar names and a small ritual whose purpose is not always explained. Yet the process is simple once you understand what each step is for. The aim is to find a wine that suits the food and what the table wants, not to show off knowledge.

How a wine list is organised

Wine lists are usually arranged in one of these ways, or a combination:

  • By type: sparkling, white, rosé, red, fortified and sweet.
  • By origin: countries, regions or designations.
  • By style: for example, crisp whites, oak-aged whites, light reds or structured reds.

Each entry normally gives the wine's name, the producer, the designation and often the vintage and grape varieties. Many restaurants also offer a selection of wines by the glass, useful when each diner orders different dishes or when you want to drink little. Some also serve wine by the carafe or in half bottles.

Think about the food first

Before opening the list, it helps to know, at least roughly, what you are going to eat. Grilled fish, a rice dish, roast meat or a tasting menu with many courses all have different needs. When everyone orders something very different, a versatile wine usually works: a white with some structure, a rosé or a light red with good acidity. Another option is to begin with a bottle of white or sparkling wine and continue with a red, or to turn to wines by the glass.

Talking to the sommelier

If the restaurant has a sommelier or staff trained in wine, they are the best source of guidance. They know the list, how the wines are developing and which go best with the kitchen's dishes. Their advice is more useful if you give them concrete information:

  • What you are going to eat.
  • Which styles the table likes: lighter or more intense, fruitier or more mature, with or without noticeable oak.
  • A wine you have enjoyed recently, as a reference.
  • A budget range. A discreet way to indicate it is to point to a wine or a section of the list and say something like “something along these lines”.

Asking is not a sign of ignorance. Even professionals consult the sommelier in another restaurant, because every list has its own particularities and often includes wines from little-known small producers.

Presenting the bottle

Once the wine is chosen, the waiter or sommelier shows the bottle to the person who ordered it before opening it. This gesture has a practical purpose: to confirm that it is exactly the wine requested, with the right producer, designation and vintage. If anything does not match, this is the moment to say so.

The bottle is then opened at the table or in view of the customer. Sometimes the cork is placed beside the glass; you can see whether it is in good condition, but there is no need to sniff it ceremoniously: the important information is in the wine.

The tasting: what it is for

Next, a small amount is poured for the person who ordered the wine, or for whoever the table designates. This tasting is not for deciding whether you like the wine, but for checking that it is free of faults. It is enough to look at it, smell it and, if you wish, take a sip. The most common problems that justify refusing a bottle are:

  • A smell of mould, damp cardboard or cellar, typical of wine tainted by TCA, known as “corked” wine.
  • Obvious oxidation in a wine that should not show it: a brownish colour, or aromas of bruised apple or walnut in a young white.
  • Strong vinegary or solvent smells.
  • A clearly unsuitable temperature, which is not a fault in the wine and can be corrected with an ice bucket or by waiting a few minutes.

If the wine is sound, a nod of approval is enough and the staff will serve the rest of the table. If you suspect a fault, say so calmly; a good professional will check and, if they agree, offer another bottle. On the other hand, the fact that a wine does not live up to your expectations is no reason to send it back, although the sommelier may suggest something else for the next bottle.

During the meal

In many restaurants the staff pour the wine throughout the meal; if you prefer to serve yourself or drink at a different pace, just say so. Whites and sparkling wines are kept in an ice bucket, and reds can be asked for slightly cooler if the room is warm. You can also ask for a wine to be decanted. It is wise to order water for the table and not to feel obliged to finish the bottle: in some places you may take leftover wine home, properly closed, depending on local customs and rules.

Bringing your own wine

Some restaurants allow customers to bring their own bottle in return for a service charge known as corkage. Conditions vary widely, so it is worth asking beforehand. As a courtesy, people do not usually bring a wine that already appears on the restaurant's list.

A few final pointers

Ordering wine in a restaurant is, above all, a conversation. Knowing what you will eat, which styles you prefer and how much you want to spend is enough to choose well. The ritual of presentation and tasting exists to make sure the wine arrives in good condition and is best approached naturally. And if someone at the table is driving or prefers not to drink, non-alcoholic alternatives are a fully legitimate part of a good meal.
