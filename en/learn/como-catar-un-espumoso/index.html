Tasting

How to taste a sparkling wine

A guide to serving, observing, smelling and tasting a sparkling wine: the bubbles, the aromas of lees ageing, the role of sweetness and the differences between the main production methods.

Sparkling wines are tasted following the same stages as any other wine —sight, nose and palate— but with an added element that changes everything: carbon dioxide. The bubbles affect how aromas, acidity and sweetness are perceived, and they are in themselves an indicator of production method and quality. They therefore deserve particular attention.

Know the production method

Before tasting, it helps to know how the bubbles were obtained, because each method leaves a recognisable imprint:

  • Traditional method: the second fermentation takes place in the bottle itself, where the wine then rests on its lees for months or years. This is the method of Champagne, Cava, Franciacorta, the French crémants and many English sparkling wines. Yeast autolysis contributes notes of bread, brioche or nuts and well-integrated bubbles.
  • Charmat or tank method: the second fermentation takes place in large sealed tanks. It preserves the fruity and floral aromas of the grape and is the usual method for Prosecco.
  • Ancestral method: the wine is bottled before the first fermentation has finished, and it completes in the bottle. It gives softer, sometimes cloudy sparkling wines, such as many so-called pét-nats.

Serving and choosing the glass

Temperature has a strong influence. Young, fruity sparkling wines are best enjoyed well chilled, around six to eight degrees Celsius, while long-aged ones show more nuance slightly less cold, around eight to ten degrees. Too cold dulls the aromas; too warm makes the mousse aggressive and the wine lose freshness.

To open the bottle, which holds considerable pressure —of the order of five or six atmospheres for traditional-method wines— remove the wire cage without letting go of the cork, hold the cork firmly and gently turn the bottle, tilted and pointing away from people. The ideal is a discreet sigh, not a bang that wastes wine and gas.

As for the glass, the narrow flute preserves the bubbles well and shows off their rise, but concentrates aromas poorly. Many tasters now prefer tulip-shaped glasses or even broader white wine glasses, which let complex sparkling wines express themselves better. The wide, shallow coupe, an old tradition, lets gas and aromas escape quickly. Glasses should be clean and free of detergent residues, which destroy the foam.

The visual stage: the bubbles

On pouring, an initial foam forms and subsides quickly. Then observe:

  • Bubble size: fine bubbles are usually prized, although size also depends on temperature, glass and pressure.
  • Persistence: the continuous release of strings of bubbles from the bottom of the glass.
  • The collar: the ring of foam that remains on the surface against the glass.

Bubbles form mainly on tiny imperfections or fibres clinging to the glass, so their appearance depends partly on the glassware. Some glasses have a point etched in the bottom to encourage them. The colour, from pale yellow to gold or, in rosés, from salmon to deep pink, tells you about the varieties, the age and the ageing.

The nose

On the nose, several families of aromas can be distinguished. Those from the grape: citrus, apple, pear, white fruit, flowers, red berries in rosés or wines made from black grapes. Those from lees ageing: bread, dough, yeast, brioche, biscuit, almond or hazelnut. And, in older wines, developed notes such as honey, toasted nuts, spices or mushroom.

It is best not to swirl the glass too vigorously, because the gas already carries the aromas and strong swirling loses bubbles. A gentle swirl is usually enough.

The palate

On the palate, the bubbles become a tactile sensation, the so-called mousse. Its texture is assessed: in the best sparkling wines it is creamy and integrated, while in others it may feel coarse or prickly. Acidity is the backbone of almost all sparkling wines and should bring freshness without harshness.

Sweetness also plays a key role. After disgorgement, in the traditional method, the dosage liqueur is added, and its sugar content determines the wine's category. In the European Union, from driest to sweetest, the terms used are brut nature (no added sugar and below three grams per litre), extra brut (up to six grams), brut (less than twelve), extra dry, dry, demi-sec and doux, the last above fifty grams per litre. Acidity and gas make sweetness less perceptible than the figures suggest, so a brut rarely tastes sweet.

Finally, balance, complexity and length are assessed: a good sparkling wine leaves a clean, fresh and lasting finish in which fruit and the nuances of ageing blend together.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing fizz with quality: more gas does not mean a better wine; what matters is how well integrated the bubbles are.
  • Serving it ice-cold: at very low temperatures, complex sparkling wines barely show their aromas.
  • Treating all sparkling wines alike: a fresh, floral Prosecco and a long-aged Champagne call for different expectations, glasses and occasions.
  • Forgetting food: thanks to their acidity and bubbles, many sparkling wines go very well with a wide range of dishes, well beyond the toast.

With attention and a little practice, tasting a sparkling wine allows you to appreciate one of the most technical and versatile styles of wine, in which the bubbles are not decoration but an essential part of its character.
