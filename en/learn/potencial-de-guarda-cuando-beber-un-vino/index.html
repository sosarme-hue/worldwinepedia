Ageing potential: when to drink a wine

What makes a wine improve with age, how it develops in the bottle, what storage conditions it needs, and how to tell whether to open it now or wait.

One of the questions enthusiasts ask most often is when to open a bottle. Will it improve if kept for a few years, or will it lose its charm? The answer depends on the wine, its style and how it is stored. A basic point is worth starting with: the vast majority of wines made in the world are intended to be drunk young, within the first few years after release. Only a relatively small share gains complexity with prolonged ageing.

What happens in the bottle

Inside a closed bottle, wine continues to change slowly. With very little oxygen, chemical reactions transform its components:

  • Aromas of fresh fruit and those from fermentation fade, and tertiary notes appear: nuts, forest floor, leather, tobacco or mushroom in reds; honey, wax, nuts or toasty notes in whites.
  • Tannins combine with one another and with pigments, forming larger molecules that feel softer and that partly fall out as sediment.
  • Colour changes: reds move from purple to garnet and then to brick; whites turn golden and may reach amber.

In the right wines, the result is a gain in complexity and harmony. In others, however, the loss of fruit is not compensated by anything interesting and the wine simply fades.

What gives a wine ageing potential

There is no infallible formula, but certain features are associated with longevity:

  • Acidity: perhaps the most important factor, especially in whites. It keeps the wine fresh and protects it. That is why very acidic whites, such as many Rieslings, Loire Chenin Blancs or Hunter Valley Semillons, can age for decades.
  • Tannins and phenolic compounds: they act as antioxidants and provide structure. Tannic reds, such as many made from Nebbiolo, Cabernet Sauvignon or long-aged Tempranillo, often need time to show at their best.
  • Sugar: great sweet wines, such as Sauternes or Tokaji, combine sugar and acidity and are among the longest-lived wines of all.
  • Added spirit and oxidative ageing: vintage Port can develop for decades in bottle, and Madeira, which has already undergone intense oxidation, is famous for its resistance to time.
  • Concentration and balance: a wine with enough fruit, extract and balance is more likely to develop well. A wine that is unbalanced in its youth will rarely correct itself over the years.

The quality of the vintage, the producer and the type of closure also play a part. Large bottles, such as magnums, usually develop more slowly than standard ones, and half-bottles more quickly.

The ageing curve

The life of a wine for ageing is often represented as a curve: after a youthful phase, in which fruit dominates and the structure may feel firm, the wine develops until it reaches a plateau of maturity, which may last for years, before beginning to decline. Some wines also go through a “closed” phase, in which they show little for a while before opening up again.

The so-called drinking windows published by producers and critics are rough estimates, not exact dates. Personal preference counts for a great deal: some people enjoy the fruit and energy of youth, while others prefer the nuances of maturity.

The importance of storage

A wine with great potential can be spoiled if stored badly. The ideal conditions are:

  • A cool, stable temperature, around twelve to fourteen degrees Celsius. Sharp fluctuations and heat speed up ageing and can damage the wine.
  • Darkness: light, especially sunlight and some fluorescent lighting, can cause faults, particularly in whites and sparkling wines in clear glass.
  • Moderate humidity, so that natural corks do not dry out.
  • Bottles lying on their side if they have natural corks, to keep the cork in contact with the wine.
  • No vibration or strong odours.

In a home without a cellar, a cool, dark corner without temperature changes —away from the kitchen and heating— is better than any visible but warm spot. For long-term storage, a temperature-controlled wine cabinet is a good solution.

How to tell whether a wine is ready

The best way to follow a wine's development, if you have several bottles, is to open one from time to time and observe the changes. Some signs:

  • Too young: very primary fruit, hard or drying tannins, poorly integrated oak aromas.
  • At its peak: fruit still present but accompanied by notes of development, soft tannins, good integration of the whole.
  • In decline: fruit absent, very evolved colour, flat or oxidised aromas, a short finish, or acidity that stands out because everything else has faded.

Serving an old wine

Wines with many years behind them need some care. It is best to stand the bottle upright a few hours beforehand so the sediment settles, open it gently —old corks can crumble— and, if there is sediment, decant it slowly. Unlike young reds, many old wines are fragile and can lose their aromas after prolonged aeration, so it is better to taste them promptly and watch how they evolve in the glass.

Patience and curiosity

Keeping wine is, to a large extent, an exercise in patience and curiosity. Not every wine deserves it, and drinking a bottle too early or too late is part of the learning process. What matters is to know the style of each wine, store it well and open it when it can be enjoyed attentively.
