The colour of wine: what the visual examination reveals

How to observe a wine's colour, intensity, clarity and legs, and what clues they offer about the grape, the winemaking and the age.

Tasting begins with the eyes. Before a wine is smelled or tasted, its appearance already offers valuable information: the hue and depth of colour, the clarity, the brightness or the presence of bubbles allow the first hypotheses about variety, winemaking and age. The visual stage is brief, but it is worth carrying out methodically.

How to look at a wine

Ideally, a moderate amount is poured into a clear, colourless glass, which is then tilted over a white background —a tablecloth or a sheet of paper— in good light, preferably daylight. Two zones can then be seen: the core, where the wine is deepest and its intensity can be judged, and the rim, the thin layer next to the glass, where the hue shows most clearly and where signs of development usually appear first.

Clarity and brightness

The first check is clarity: whether the wine is clear or cloudy. Marked cloudiness can indicate a fault, but not always: many wines are now bottled unfiltered or with minimal fining and may show a slight haze without any effect on quality. Sediment in older red wines and small crystals of potassium bitartrate in whites that have been chilled are natural, harmless deposits.

Brightness describes how the surface of the wine reflects light. Wines with good acidity tend to look lively and bright, while a dull appearance may suggest low acidity or advanced development.

Where colour comes from

Almost all grapes have colourless pulp; the colour lies in the skin. In red wines it comes mainly from anthocyanins, pigments that pass into the juice during maceration. In whites, the colour is much paler and is due to other phenolic compounds, whose yellowish hue deepens with oxidation and time. Rosés take their colour from brief contact with the skins of black grapes.

Acidity also plays a part: at lower pH, anthocyanins appear redder and more vivid; at higher pH, the colour tends towards violet or blue and is less stable.

White wine hues

White wines range from almost colourless to amber:

  • Pale yellow with green tints: typical of young wines, from cool climates or made without oak.
  • Straw or lemon yellow: the most common hue in young whites.
  • Gold: found in whites aged in barrel, from very ripe grapes, in sweet wines, or in whites with a few years in bottle.
  • Amber or brown: seen in oxidatively aged wines such as amontillado or oloroso, in very old whites, and in so-called orange wines, made with skin maceration.

Red wine hues

In reds, colour evolves in a fairly predictable direction:

  • Purple: very young wines, often with a violet rim.
  • Ruby: a straightforward red, common in reds a few years old.
  • Garnet: a darker red with orange tints at the rim, indicating some development.
  • Tawny or brick: orange and brown hues, typical of reds after long ageing.

Over time, free anthocyanins combine with tannins to form more stable but less vivid pigments, while part of the colouring matter falls out as sediment. That is why old reds become lighter and move from purple towards brick.

Intensity

The depth of colour depends above all on the variety and on the winemaking. Thick-skinned, heavily pigmented grapes such as Syrah, Malbec, Tannat or Alicante Bouschet —one of the few with red flesh— give almost opaque wines; others, such as Pinot Noir, Nebbiolo or Grenache, make paler ones. Long maceration, intense extraction or a sunny climate all increase intensity.

Colour is therefore not a direct indicator of quality: a pale red can be excellent and a very dark one mediocre. What matters is whether the colour is consistent with the type of wine in the glass.

Legs and viscosity

When the glass is swirled, the wine leaves drops on the sides that slowly run down: these are the legs or tears. They form because of the so-called Marangoni effect: alcohol evaporates faster than water from the film of wine clinging to the glass, creating differences in surface tension that draw the liquid upwards before it falls back in drops. Abundant, slow legs usually indicate high alcohol or, in sweet wines, a lot of sugar. They say nothing, however, about quality.

Bubbles

In sparkling wines, the visual stage includes observing the bubbles: their size, their persistence and the ring of foam that forms on the surface. In still wines, a few bubbles clinging to the glass may be due to residual carbon dioxide from fermentation, sometimes deliberately retained to add freshness.

What colour can and cannot tell you

Visual examination makes it possible to estimate a wine's approximate age, its likely variety, whether it has undergone oxidative ageing or whether it shows a fault. But it is only a first clue, to be confirmed on the nose and palate. A young wine bottled with little oxygen may keep a youthful colour for years, and an oak-aged white may look older than it is. As in the rest of tasting, experience lies in linking what you see to everything else.
