How to read a wine label

What information is mandatory on a wine label, what the appellation, vintage or variety mean, and how to interpret labels from different countries.

The label is a wine's calling card and often the only information the buyer has before opening the bottle. It can seem cryptic, especially on wines from other countries, but it follows fairly precise rules. Knowing how to distinguish what is mandatory from what is voluntary, and what is regulated from what is merely marketing, helps you choose better. This article focuses on European Union rules, shared by Spain, France, Italy and the other member states, with some references to other countries.

Front and back labels

Many bottles carry a main front label and a back label. The law does not require information to appear in a particular place, but it does require certain particulars to appear in the same field of vision. In Spain, many designations of origin also add a numbered strip or back label issued by the Regulatory Council, guaranteeing the origin and, where relevant, the ageing category.

Mandatory information

In the European Union, a wine label must include, among other particulars:

  • The product category: wine, sparkling wine, liqueur wine and so on. For wines with a designation of origin, the name of the designation may replace the word wine.
  • The protected designation of origin (PDO) or protected geographical indication (PGI), where applicable, with its traditional term, such as D.O., D.O.Ca., AOC, DOC or DOCG.
  • The actual alcoholic strength, expressed as a percentage by volume.
  • The nominal volume, for example 75 cl.
  • The provenance, that is, the country of origin.
  • The name of the bottler or, where relevant, the importer.
  • The declaration of allergens: sulphites, present in almost all wines, and, if they were used for fining and detectable traces remain, egg or milk derivatives.
  • For sparkling wines, the sugar content: brut nature, extra brut, brut, extra dry, dry, medium dry or sweet.
  • The lot number.

Since the end of 2023, European rules also require nutrition information and a list of ingredients for wines produced from that date. The energy value must appear on the label, but the rest of the information may be provided electronically, for example through a QR code.

Optional but regulated information

Other particulars are voluntary, but if they appear they must comply with certain rules:

  • Vintage: indicates the year of harvest. In the European Union, at least 85% of the grapes must come from that year.
  • Variety: if a single grape is named, at least 85% of the wine must come from it. If several are named, they must appear in order of importance.
  • Traditional ageing terms, such as crianza, reserva or gran reserva in Spain, or riserva in Italy, which are covered in a separate article.
  • Terms for sugar content in still wines: dry, medium dry, medium sweet or sweet.
  • Statements such as estate bottled or the name of a holding, which are reserved for specific cases.
  • Organic production logos, subject to certification.

What the label does not guarantee

Many expressions on labels have no precise legal definition in every country: selection, old vines, limited edition or premium may simply be marketing claims. The medals and scores shown on some bottles reflect the opinion of a particular competition or critic, not an official guarantee. And the design of the label, however attractive, says nothing about the contents.

Labels from different countries

Each wine tradition organises information in its own way.

  • Spain: the label usually highlights the brand, the winery and the designation of origin. Categories of origin include, among others, the D.O.; the D.O.Ca., currently held by Rioja and Priorat; Vino de Pago; and protected geographical indications, often called Vino de la Tierra.
  • France: in most regions place matters more than grape. A Burgundy label may show only the village or the vineyard, without mentioning Pinot Noir or Chardonnay. Terms such as Grand Cru or Premier Cru mean different things in different regions. In Bordeaux the name of a château is common.
  • Italy: the quality pyramid runs from wines without a geographical indication to IGT, DOC and DOCG. Many wines bear the name of the appellation, such as Barolo or Chianti Classico, rather than the variety.
  • Germany: in addition to region and variety, labels may include the traditional Prädikate, such as Kabinett, Spätlese or Auslese, which are based on grape ripeness at harvest and do not necessarily indicate sweetness. Terms such as trocken indicate a dry wine.
  • New World countries: in the United States, Australia, Chile, Argentina or New Zealand, the variety is usually the main piece of information, together with the region and the brand.

Practical tips

To get the most from a label, look first at the origin and category, which indicate the wine's legal framework; then at the vintage, which helps you judge whether it is at its best; at the variety or style, if indicated; and at the alcohol level, which offers a clue to the body and ripeness of the grapes. The back label, when there is one, often provides details of winemaking, ageing or serving suggestions. And when in doubt, asking a specialist retailer remains one of the best ways to interpret what a bottle is saying.
