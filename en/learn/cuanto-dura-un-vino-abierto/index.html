How long does an open bottle of wine last?

Why wine changes once opened, roughly how long each type keeps, which methods help preserve it and how to tell when it is past its best.

A closed bottle of wine can keep in good condition for years, but as soon as it is opened a countdown begins. Contact with air transforms the wine, imperceptibly at first and then obviously. How long it lasts depends on the type of wine, how it has been reclosed and, above all, the temperature at which it is kept. The figures below are indicative: every wine is different, and your nose and palate always have the final say.

What happens to an open wine

The main agent of change is oxygen. On contact with the wine it triggers oxidation reactions that dull fruity and floral aromas, darken the colour and can produce notes of bruised apple, walnut or dried fruit. In young, closed wines, brief aeration can be beneficial because it opens up the aromas; but if contact continues, the effect becomes negative.

Microorganisms also play a part, particularly acetic acid bacteria, which in the presence of oxygen turn alcohol into acetic acid, the main component of vinegar. In sparkling wines, carbon dioxide also escapes gradually and the wine loses its fizz. Cold slows all these processes; warmth speeds them up.

Indicative times by type of wine

With the bottle properly closed and kept in the refrigerator, these are the most common guidelines:

  • Sparkling wines: one to three days with a dedicated airtight stopper. Traditional-method wines, at higher pressure, usually keep their bubbles better than lightly sparkling ones.
  • Light whites and rosés: around three to five days. They lose aromatic freshness before structure.
  • Full-bodied or oak-aged whites: around three to five days, sometimes a little longer.
  • Light reds: three to five days.
  • Structured, tannic reds: three days to a week; tannins and acidity act as protection against oxidation.
  • Sweet wines: those high in sugar usually last more than a week, because the sugar and often the acidity protect them.
  • Biologically aged fortified wines, such as fino or manzanilla: these are delicate and are best treated like a white and drunk within a few days.
  • Oxidatively aged fortified wines, such as oloroso, Pedro Ximénez, tawny port or Madeira: having already aged in contact with air, they keep for weeks and, in the case of Madeira, even months.

Very old, fragile wines are the exception on the other side: they can deteriorate within hours of opening, so it is advisable to drink them the same day.

How to extend their life

Several simple measures buy time:

  • Reclose as soon as possible, with the original cork, a screw cap or a silicone stopper. The less time the bottle spends open, the better.
  • Keep it in the refrigerator, reds included. Just take them out a little before serving so they return to a suitable temperature.
  • Keep the bottle upright, to reduce the surface of wine in contact with air.
  • Transfer the wine to a smaller container, for example a clean half bottle, so that as little air space as possible remains.
  • Use inert gas: there are sprays of argon or other gases which, being denser than air, form a protective layer over the wine.
  • Vacuum pumps: these remove some of the air from the bottle. Their effectiveness is debated, and some believe they can strip aromas, but many users find them useful for a day or two.
  • Needle systems: some devices let you draw wine through the cork without removing it, replacing the volume extracted with inert gas. They are used in restaurants that serve expensive wines by the glass.

For sparkling wines, the most effective option is a clamp-style stopper designed for them. The habit of putting a teaspoon in the neck of the bottle has no proven basis.

Boxed wine and other formats

Bag-in-box wine, a flexible bag inside a carton with a tap, is designed precisely to keep once opened: the bag collapses as wine is drawn off and very little air gets in. It therefore stays in good condition for several weeks, although the date shown on the pack should be respected.

How to tell if a wine has gone off

An open wine is rarely dangerous, but it can become unpleasant. The clearest signs are:

  • A darker or browner colour than normal, especially in whites.
  • Aromas of vinegar, glue or bruised apple.
  • A flat taste, lacking fruit, or a sharp acidity.
  • In sparkling wines, the almost total loss of bubbles.

A wine that has lost its freshness but has not turned to vinegar can still be useful in the kitchen, for stews, sauces or marinades, provided its smell is not unpleasant.

A matter of planning

The best way not to waste wine is to open what you are going to drink. Half bottles, wines by the glass and smaller formats are a good solution when you want only a little, and a properly closed bottle kept cold can still offer a good wine the next day.
