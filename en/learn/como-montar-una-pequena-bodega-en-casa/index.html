How to set up a small wine cellar at home

How to choose the space, the type of cabinet or rack, the capacity and the selection of wines for a small home cellar, and how to organise it and keep records.

You do not need a large house or a major investment to keep a small stock of well-stored wines. A home cellar can be a corner of a basement, a temperature-controlled cabinet or simply a wine rack placed in the right spot. What matters is to combine proper storage conditions with a selection of wines suited to how they will be used.

Defining the goal

Before buying equipment or bottles, it is worth answering a few simple questions:

  • What is the cellar for? Having everyday wines to hand, keeping a few bottles for special occasions, or following the development of age-worthy wines over years are different aims.
  • How much wine do you drink? Capacity should match your actual pace of drinking and buying, not an ideal figure.
  • How long will bottles be kept? Most wines are made to be drunk within a few years; only some benefit from long ageing in bottle.

For occasional drinking, a few dozen bottles are enough. Anyone buying wines for ageing will need more space, because part of the cellar will be tied up for years.

The basic conditions

The storage rules are the same for a large or small cellar: a cool, stable temperature, around 12-14 °C as a guide, although a slightly higher temperature is acceptable if it does not fluctuate; darkness, because light, especially ultraviolet, degrades wine; moderate humidity, so that corks do not dry out; no vibration and no strong odours; and cork-sealed bottles stored on their side. Of all these factors, sudden temperature swings and excessive heat are the most harmful.

Choosing the space

If the house has a basement, an old cellar or an interior room without windows, it may be ideal, provided its temperature is checked in different seasons with a maximum-minimum thermometer. In a flat, the best options are usually a built-in cupboard on an interior wall, away from the kitchen, radiators and walls that get the sun. Lofts, uninsulated garages and kitchens should be avoided, as their temperature changes a great deal over the day and the year.

Temperature-controlled cabinets

When no space with suitable natural conditions is available, a wine cabinet or wine fridge is the most practical solution. When choosing one, consider:

  • Cooling system: compressor models are more powerful and stable, suitable for warm rooms; thermoelectric ones are quiet and vibrate less but perform worse when the room is hot.
  • Temperature zones: a single-zone cabinet suits long-term storage; those with two or more zones also allow wines to be kept at serving temperature.
  • Real capacity: manufacturers' figures are usually based on Bordeaux-shaped bottles; Burgundy, sparkling or larger bottles take up more room.
  • Door: if it is glass, it should be UV-protected.
  • Placement: cabinets need ventilation around them, as the manufacturer specifies, and work best in a place without large temperature changes.

A household refrigerator is no substitute for a wine cabinet for long-term storage: it is too cold and dry, and its vibrations and odours are unsuitable, although it is fine for chilling bottles to be drunk soon.

Racks and shelving

In a space with good natural conditions, wooden or metal racks are enough. Wood is quiet and absorbs vibration; metal is strong and takes up little room. Modular systems allow the cellar to grow gradually. Ideally each bottle should have its own slot, so it can be removed without moving the others, and there should be room for special formats. Original wooden cases are also a good way to store wines that will be kept unopened for a long time.

Which wines to keep

A balanced cellar combines several kinds of wine:

  • Everyday wines, with quick turnover: fresh whites, rosés, young reds and a sparkling wine or two.
  • Wines for occasions, to be drunk over the next few years.
  • Wines for ageing, with enough structure, acidity and concentration to improve over time: some oak-aged reds, full-bodied whites, sweet wines and some long-aged sparkling wines.

A common practice among enthusiasts is to buy several bottles of the same age-worthy wine and open them at intervals, to follow its development and find its best moment. Diversifying regions, grapes and styles also ensures there is always an option for every meal.

Organising and keeping records

Even with a few dozen bottles it is easy to lose track of what you have. Some useful ideas:

  • Group wines by type, region or, better still, by intended drinking time.
  • Put tags or cards on bottle necks to identify them without moving them.
  • Keep an inventory, in a notebook, spreadsheet or app, with the wine's name, vintage, quantity, location, purchase date and an approximate drinking window.
  • Note your impressions when opening each bottle, which helps decide when to open the next ones.

Common mistakes

The most frequent errors are buying more than you can drink, keeping wines meant to be drunk young for too long, placing the cellar somewhere warm and forgetting bottles at the back. A home cellar works best when it is renewed regularly: its purpose is for the wines to be drunk at a good moment, in moderation and in good company.
