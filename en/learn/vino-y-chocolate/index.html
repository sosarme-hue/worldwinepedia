Food pairing

Wine and chocolate

With its bitterness, fat and sweetness, chocolate is one of the hardest foods to pair with wine; this article explains why, and which sweet and fortified wines get on best with dark, milk and white chocolate.

The idea that a powerful red and a square of dark chocolate make a perfect pair is very widespread, and it is often presented as a romantic or sophisticated combination. Many sommeliers, however, regard it as one of the riskiest matches of all. Chocolate brings together in a single mouthful several of the elements that most complicate pairing: bitterness, astringency, fat and, in most cases, a considerable amount of sugar. Understanding how these components interact with wine helps to avoid disappointment and to find genuinely enjoyable combinations.

Why chocolate is difficult

Cocoa contains polyphenols that bring bitterness and an astringent sensation similar to that of wine tannins. When a dry, tannic red meets dark chocolate, the two bitternesses and the two astringencies add up, and the result can be harsh and drying. Then there is sugar: as with any dessert, if the chocolate is sweeter than the wine, the wine seems acidic, thin and bitter. Finally, cocoa butter coats the mouth and tends to mute the aromas of delicate wines. All of this explains why dry reds, even the most famous ones, rarely show their best alongside a chocolate dessert.

The principle: sweetness, fruit and body

The wines that best accompany chocolate share three features: they have enough sweetness to match or exceed that of the chocolate, they possess ripe fruit or nutty notes that converse with the cocoa, and they have enough body not to be crushed by its intensity. This is why sweet fortified wines and naturally sweet reds are the classic references.

Dark chocolate

With high-cocoa dark chocolate, intense and bitter, sweet reds with dark fruit and some structure are usually recommended. Ruby or LBV-style Port, with its blackberry and plum fruit, is probably the most common choice. In Roussillon, in the south of France, the grenache-based vins doux naturels of Banyuls and Maury have a long tradition of accompanying chocolate and cocoa desserts. In Italy, Recioto della Valpolicella, made from dried corvina grapes and other local varieties, offers another option with concentrated fruit. The more bitter the chocolate, the more important it is that the wine has fruit and sweetness to compensate.

Milk chocolate

Milk chocolate is sweeter, creamier and less bitter, with notes of caramel and vanilla. Wines with oxidative ageing suit it very well here: a tawny Port, with its aromas of walnut, hazelnut and caramel; a sweet malvasia Madeira, whose vibrant acidity balances the creaminess; or a sweet oloroso. When the chocolate contains hazelnuts, almonds or praline, this affinity with nuts becomes even more evident.

White chocolate

White chocolate, which actually contains no cocoa solids but cocoa butter, sugar and milk, lacks the bitterness of dark chocolate. It is very sweet and rich, and calls for sweet wines with good acidity and floral or fruity aromas: a Moscatel, a late-harvest wine or even a sweet sparkling wine, whose bubbles lighten the sense of richness.

Chocolate desserts

In elaborate desserts, chocolate is rarely alone, and the other ingredients can guide the choice:

  • With red berries or cherries: a ruby Port or a grenache-based vin doux naturel extends the fruit of the dessert.
  • With orange: a Moscatel with some age, showing notes of candied orange peel, reinforces the citrus.
  • With coffee or caramel: a Pedro Ximénez, with its aromas of raisin, fig and toast, is a natural partner, though the dessert should not be sweeter than the wine, which with such a concentrated wine is unlikely.
  • With nuts: a tawny or a sweet Madeira.

What about dry reds?

Some enthusiasts enjoy a very ripe dry red, with generous fruit and soft tannins, with dark chocolate that contains very little sugar. It can work if the chocolate is almost bitter and the wine particularly fruity, but it is a delicate combination with variable results. As a general rule, the sweeter the chocolate, the less sense it makes to serve it with a dry wine.

Serving tips

To appreciate the combination, it helps to serve the chocolate at room temperature so that it melts properly in the mouth, and to taste the wine first, then the chocolate, and then the wine again. Sweet and fortified wines are high in alcohol and concentration, so they are served in small glasses and in moderate quantities, as an accompaniment to dessert rather than the centrepiece of the end of the meal.
