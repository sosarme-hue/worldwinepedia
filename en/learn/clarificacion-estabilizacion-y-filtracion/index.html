Fining, stabilisation and filtration

The operations that leave wine clear and stable before bottling: settling and racking, fining agents, the prevention of tartrate and protein deposits, and the different types of filtration.

At the end of fermentation, wine is a cloudy liquid containing yeasts, bacteria, fragments of skin and pulp, salts and suspended molecules. Over time many of these particles settle, but others remain or may form later, even in the bottle. To ensure that wine reaches the consumer clear and retains its appearance and qualities, winemakers use a series of operations: fining, which removes cloudiness; stabilisation, which prevents future deposits or hazes; and filtration, which physically retains particles. Their intensity varies greatly according to the style of wine and the philosophy of each winery.

Settling and racking

The oldest and simplest way of clarifying wine is to let gravity do the work. At rest, the heavier particles sink to the bottom of the tank or barrel, forming the lees. By racking, the clear wine is moved to another vessel, leaving the sediment behind. Repeated during ageing, racking can produce fairly clear wines with no other intervention. For wines aged for a long time in barrel, this natural process may be enough to achieve good clarity.

Fining agents

Fining involves adding to the wine a substance that binds to suspended particles and certain unstable molecules, carrying them to the bottom as it flocculates and settles. The wine is then racked or filtered. Each fining agent acts on different compounds:

  • Bentonite: a clay that binds proteins, used mainly in white and rosé wines.
  • Egg white or albumin: traditional for great red wines, it softens the most astringent tannins.
  • Gelatine: also reduces tannins and improves clarity.
  • Casein: a milk protein used in white wines to remove oxidised, browned compounds.
  • Isinglass: obtained from the swim bladders of fish, it brings brilliance to white wines.
  • PVPP and charcoal: products that retain polyphenols responsible for browning and, in the case of charcoal, certain unwanted odours or colours.
  • Plant proteins: derived, for example, from peas or potatoes, they are increasingly replacing animal-based fining agents.

Fining agents are almost entirely removed with the sediment. Even so, in the European Union wines fined with egg or milk derivatives must state this on the label if detectable residues remain, since these are allergens. The use of animal products also explains why some wines are not suitable for vegans, and why many wineries have adopted plant-based or mineral alternatives.

Tartrate stabilisation

Wine contains tartaric acid and potassium, which can combine to form potassium bitartrate. This salt is poorly soluble in the presence of alcohol and even less so at low temperatures, so if the wine is not stabilised, crystals may form in the bottle when it is chilled. They are entirely harmless, but many consumers regard them as a fault or mistake them for fragments of glass.

The classic preventive method is cold stabilisation: the wine is held for a few days at a temperature close to its freezing point to make the crystals precipitate, after which they are removed. Other techniques exist, such as electrodialysis, which removes some of the ions responsible, or the addition of substances that prevent crystal growth, such as mannoproteins, carboxymethylcellulose or metatartaric acid, the last of which has only a temporary effect.

Protein stabilisation

Grape proteins, found mainly in white and rosé wines, can coagulate if the wine is exposed to heat, causing a haze or a whitish deposit. To prevent this, the laboratory subjects samples of the wine to heat tests and determines the required dose of bentonite, the most common treatment. In red wines, tannins combine with proteins and precipitate them during winemaking, so the problem is rare.

Other instabilities

In red wines, some of the colouring matter may precipitate in the cold and form deposits in the bottle; ageing and, sometimes, specific treatments reduce this risk. Wines can also become hazy through an excess of certain metals, such as iron or copper, a problem now uncommon thanks to the use of stainless materials. Microbiological stability, finally, is ensured by good hygiene, an adequate level of sulphur dioxide and, where appropriate, filtration that removes microorganisms.

Filtration

Filtration physically retains particles by passing the wine through a porous material. There are several types:

  • Earth filtration: diatomaceous earth or other minerals form a filtering layer; it is useful for very cloudy wines.
  • Sheet filtration: the wine passes through cellulose sheets of varying porosity, from coarse to fine filtration.
  • Membrane filtration: cartridges with very small pores retain yeasts and bacteria; it is normally carried out just before bottling, especially for wines with residual sugar or without malolactic fermentation.
  • Crossflow filtration: the wine flows parallel to the membrane, which prevents it from clogging quickly and allows cloudy wines to be treated in a single operation.

Centrifugation is an alternative that separates particles by centrifugal force and is used mainly for juice and very cloudy wines.

The debate over intervention

Some producers and wine lovers argue that heavy fining or filtration can strip a wine of aroma, body and texture, and they prefer to bottle without fining or filtering, or to do so very lightly. Others believe that, when carried out well, these operations hardly affect quality and ensure a stable, fault-free wine. The choice depends on the type of wine, its microbiological condition and the risk the winemaker is willing to take. An unfiltered wine is not necessarily better or worse, but it may show sediment or some haziness, which is worth knowing when serving it.
