Wine and responsible drinking

Basic information about the alcohol in wine, how to work out how much a glass contains, situations in which it is best not to drink, and why wine should not be regarded as a health food.

Wine is part of the culture, cuisine and landscape of many regions of the world, and this encyclopedia is devoted to explaining its history, production and diversity. But wine is also an alcoholic drink, and knowing it well includes understanding what drinking it involves. This article provides general information and is in no way a substitute for advice from a health professional.

The alcohol in wine

The alcohol in wine is ethanol, formed when yeasts convert grape sugar during fermentation. Most still wines contain between 11% and 15% alcohol by volume; some light wines contain less, and fortified wines such as sherry or port, to which grape spirit is added, usually exceed 15%. The alcoholic strength must be shown on the label and varies from wine to wine, even within the same region, depending on grape ripeness, style and each year's weather.

Ethanol is a psychoactive substance: it acts on the nervous system, alters perception, reflexes and judgement, and can cause dependence. Its effects depend on the amount consumed, how quickly it is drunk, whether one has eaten and individual characteristics such as weight, sex, age and state of health.

How much alcohol is in a glass

To talk about quantities, many countries use the concept of a standard drink or unit, though its value is not the same everywhere. In Spain, for example, the standard drink unit (UBE) is equivalent to 10 grams of pure alcohol; in the United Kingdom a unit is 8 grams, and in the United States a standard drink is about 14 grams.

The grams of alcohol in a drink can be calculated with a simple formula: volume in millilitres × strength ÷ 100 × 0.8, where 0.8 is the approximate density of ethanol. Thus a 100 ml glass of a 13% wine contains about 10 grams of alcohol, and a 750 ml bottle of a 13.5% wine contains around 80 grams. The glasses poured at home or in bars are usually larger than 100 ml, so it is easy to underestimate the actual amount consumed.

Alcohol and health: what reference bodies say

For years the idea was spread that moderate wine drinking could be beneficial, often backed by the so-called “French paradox” or by the polyphenol content of red wine. Later research has heavily qualified those conclusions, and health authorities do not recommend starting to drink, or drinking more, for health reasons.

The International Agency for Research on Cancer, part of the World Health Organization, classifies alcoholic beverages in Group 1, agents carcinogenic to humans, regardless of the type of drink. The World Health Organization has also stated that no level of alcohol consumption can be established as safe for health. For this reason, this encyclopedia treats wine as a cultural and gastronomic product, not as a health food. Specific guidelines vary by country and should be checked in each place's official sources.

Situations in which one should not drink

There are circumstances in which health authorities and the law recommend or require not drinking alcohol:

  • When driving or operating machinery. Legal blood alcohol limits vary by country and type of driver, but alcohol affects driving ability even in small amounts. The safest option is not to drink if you are going to drive.
  • During pregnancy and breastfeeding, when health authorities recommend avoiding alcohol completely.
  • Minors. The sale of alcohol to minors and its consumption by them are prohibited in most countries, and the legal minimum age varies from country to country.
  • With certain medicines or in the presence of certain illnesses. If in doubt, consult a doctor or pharmacist.
  • At work, in activities requiring concentration or in risky situations, such as swimming or mountain sports.

Guidelines for those who choose to drink

For those who choose to drink wine, a few guidelines help reduce risks:

  • Drink slowly and with meals.
  • Alternate wine with water.
  • Pour small amounts and do not top up the glass out of habit.
  • Keep some alcohol-free days during the week.
  • At tastings and winery visits, use the spittoon.
  • Respect those who do not drink or drink little, and always offer non-alcoholic alternatives.

It is worth remembering that the body eliminates alcohol at a limited, roughly constant rate. Neither coffee, nor a cold shower, nor exercise speeds up the process: only time lowers blood alcohol.

Appreciating wine in moderation

Wine culture has traditionally valued quality over quantity: looking, smelling and tasting attentively, knowing where what one drinks comes from and sharing it at the table. That approach to wine is compatible with moderate, mindful drinking, and also with the equally legitimate decision not to drink. If someone feels that drinking, their own or that of someone close to them, is becoming a problem, health services and specialist organisations can offer information and help.
