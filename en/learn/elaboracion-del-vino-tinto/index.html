Oenology & science

How red wine is made

From bunch to bottle: how fermenting the must in contact with the skins extracts colour, tannins and aromas, and which winemaking decisions shape the style of a red wine.

Red wine differs from other wines in one essential respect: the must ferments in contact with the solid parts of the grape, above all the skins. The pulp of almost all red varieties is colourless, so the colour, the tannins and much of the aroma come from the skins and, to a lesser extent, from the seeds. Making a red wine therefore means steering two simultaneous processes: the conversion of sugar into alcohol and the controlled extraction of the substances held in the skins. The decisions the winemaker takes at each stage explain why two wines from the same grape variety can turn out so different.

Harvest and arrival at the winery

Everything begins with choosing when to pick. The grower is not only looking for an adequate sugar level, but also for good ripeness in the skins and seeds, which determines whether the tannins will be supple or drying. Grapes picked too early give herbaceous, astringent wines; overripe grapes give heavy, alcoholic wines lacking freshness.

The fruit should reach the winery whole and healthy, as quickly as possible and without warming up. Many wineries use small crates so that the bunches are not crushed under their own weight, and some install sorting tables where rotten, unripe or shrivelled berries, leaves and other debris are removed by hand or by optical sorters.

Destemming and crushing

The first mechanical treatment is usually destemming, which separates the berries from the stalks. Stems contain coarser tannins and can contribute green notes, so they are normally removed. Some winemakers, however, ferment part or all of the crop as whole bunches, a traditional practice in places such as Burgundy with Pinot Noir, which can add aromatic freshness and a particular structure when the stems are properly lignified.

The grapes are then gently crushed to split the skins and release the juice without breaking the seeds, whose tannins are harsher. The resulting mixture of juice, skins and seeds is called the must or pomace mass, and it is transferred to fermentation vessels made of stainless steel, concrete or wood.

Preparation and pre-fermentation maceration

Before fermentation begins, the winemaker usually adds a moderate dose of sulphur dioxide to protect the must from oxidation and unwanted microorganisms. Acidity may also be adjusted where local regulations allow it.

Some producers carry out a cold pre-fermentation maceration, or cold soak: the must is kept at a low temperature for a few days to delay yeast activity. In this watery, still alcohol-free medium, the extraction of colour and aroma is favoured over that of the harder tannins. The technique is common for thin-skinned varieties that are meant to be fruity and expressive.

Fermentation and maceration

Alcoholic fermentation may be started by the yeasts naturally present on the grapes and in the winery, or by selected cultured yeasts. For reds it normally takes place at higher temperatures than for whites, often somewhere between about 24 and 30 degrees Celsius, because warmth encourages extraction from the skins. Excessive heat, however, can stall the fermentation or dull the aromas, which is why modern tanks are fitted with temperature control.

The carbon dioxide released pushes the skins to the surface, where they form a compact layer known as the cap. Left alone, the cap would dry out and heat up, could encourage acetic bacteria, and would give little extraction. The winemaker therefore keeps it in contact with the liquid using several techniques:

  • Pumping over: juice is drawn from the bottom of the tank and sprayed over the cap.
  • Punching down: the cap is pushed into the liquid with a paddle or a mechanical device; it is common in small open-top vessels.
  • Rack and return: the liquid is drained into another tank and then poured back over the drained cap, which aerates and homogenises the mass.

The frequency and intensity of these operations allow the style to be fine-tuned. Gentle handling gives light, fruity wines; vigorous work produces more structured wines suited to long ageing.

Post-fermentation maceration and running off

Once the yeast has consumed nearly all the sugar, the winemaker decides how long to extend skin contact. For young wines, the wine is usually run off at the end of fermentation or even earlier. For wines intended for ageing, maceration may continue for days or weeks: in the presence of alcohol, tannins are extracted more easily and combine with the anthocyanins, which helps to stabilise the colour.

At running off, the liquid that drains freely by gravity, known as free-run wine, is separated. The skins remaining in the tank are then pressed to obtain press wine, which is more concentrated, more tannic and sometimes bitter. The winemaker may blend part of it back into the free-run wine to add body and structure, or keep it separate.

Malolactic fermentation

Almost all red wines then go through malolactic fermentation, in which lactic acid bacteria convert malic acid into the softer-tasting lactic acid. The wine gains roundness and microbiological stability. This transformation can take place in tank or in barrel; in the latter case it is said to promote better integration with the oak.

Ageing, fining and bottling

From here the paths diverge. Young reds are stabilised, fined and bottled within a few months to preserve their fruit. Others spend time maturing in oak barrels, concrete tanks or clay vessels, where the wine clarifies naturally, its tannins soften and it develops aromas of maturity. During this stage the wine is racked to separate it from its lees, and its volatile acidity and sulphur levels are checked regularly.

Before bottling, the wine may be fined with agents such as egg white, traditionally used for great reds, and filtered to a greater or lesser degree. Some producers prefer to keep these interventions to a minimum in order to preserve the texture of the wine, accepting that a deposit may form in the bottle.

Variations on a common theme

The scheme described above has many variations. Carbonic maceration, in which whole bunches ferment in an atmosphere of carbon dioxide, produces very aromatic reds for early drinking. Some wineries ferment directly in open barrels or in clay jars. The choice of variety, degree of extraction and type of ageing makes it possible to obtain anything from light, fresh reds to dense, deep wines capable of developing for decades. In every case the principle is the same: to turn sugar into alcohol while drawing from the grape skin everything that will give the wine its colour, structure and personality.
