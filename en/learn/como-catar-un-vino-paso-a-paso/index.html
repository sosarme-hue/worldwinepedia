Tasting

How to taste wine step by step

Sight, nose and palate: a simple, orderly method for observing, smelling and tasting a wine and drawing conclusions about its quality and style.

Tasting is not the same as drinking. Tasting is an exercise in attention in which a wine is examined with the senses, in an orderly way, in order to describe, compare and assess it. No special talent is needed: with a clear method and some practice, any enthusiast can perceive far more than they expect. Professionals around the world follow a similar scheme organised in three stages, visual, olfactory and gustatory, followed by a conclusion.

Preparing to taste

Conditions influence what we perceive. It is best to taste in a well-lit place, ideally in natural light, free from strong smells of perfume, smoke or cooking, with a white background such as a tablecloth or a sheet of paper for looking at the colour. The glass should be thin, clear crystal or glass, with a bowl that narrows towards the rim to concentrate the aromas; standard tasting glasses follow this design. Pour a small amount, no more than about a third of the glass, so the wine can be swirled without spilling.

The wine's temperature should suit its type, because excessive cold mutes aromas and warmth emphasises alcohol. It also helps to taste with a clean palate, away from very spicy or sweet food, and to have water to hand and, when tasting several wines, a spittoon. In professional tastings wine is spat out to preserve judgement throughout the session.

Appearance

Hold the glass by the stem or the foot, so as not to warm the wine or smear the bowl, and tilt it over the white background. First assess clarity: a wine may be brilliant, clear or hazy. Slight haze is not always a fault, since some wines are bottled unfiltered, but it is if it comes with abnormal smells.

Next observe the colour and its intensity. Whites range from pale yellow with green tints to gold and amber; rosés from pale pink to salmon or raspberry; reds from purple to ruby, garnet and brick. The rim of the wine, where it is thinnest, often reveals age: young reds have violet rims and mature ones orange rims. Whites, by contrast, gain colour with time.

When the glass is swirled, the wine leaves drops on the sides that slowly run down, known as tears or legs. They are mainly due to alcohol and its evaporation, and to a lesser extent to other components; they do not indicate quality. In sparkling wines, the size, abundance and persistence of the bubbles are also noted.

Nose

The nose is the richest stage. First smell the glass at rest, without swirling, to catch the most volatile aromas and detect possible faults. Then swirl it gently in circles, which increases the surface in contact with air and releases more compounds, and smell again with short, repeated sniffs.

The first question is whether the wine is clean, free from smells of damp, vinegar, stables or rotten eggs, which are covered in the article on wine faults. Then assess aromatic intensity and, finally, try to describe the aromas. It helps to think in families: fruit, distinguishing fresh, ripe, cooked or dried fruit; flowers; herbs and vegetal notes; spices; notes of oak, toast, dairy or bread; and developed aromas such as leather, earth, nuts or honey. It is also useful to place them by origin: aromas from the grape, from fermentation or from ageing.

There is no single right answer. Smell memory is trained by paying attention to the fruits, spices and flowers of everyday life and linking those memories to what is found in the glass.

Palate

Take a moderate sip and spread it around the whole mouth. Some tasters draw in a little air with the wine in their mouth, which helps aromas reach the nose retronasally. On the palate several elements are analysed.

  • Sweetness: from dry to sweet, depending on residual sugar.
  • Acidity: felt as freshness and making the mouth water; low acidity feels heavy, excessive acidity feels harsh.
  • Tannins: mainly in reds, they cause astringency, a drying feeling on the gums and tongue. Both quantity and quality are assessed, whether they are fine or coarse.
  • Alcohol: adds body and a warming sensation; in excess it burns.
  • Body: the sense of weight or volume of the wine in the mouth, from light to full.
  • Flavours and aromas on the palate: these confirm or refine what was perceived on the nose.

Finally, after swallowing or spitting, measure the length: how long the pleasant sensations remain in the mouth. A long finish is usually a sign of quality.

Conclusion

The tasting ends with an overall assessment. Are acidity, alcohol, sugar and tannins in balance? Is the wine complex, with many nuances, or simple? Is it intense and long? Does it clearly express its variety or its origin? You can also estimate its drinking window: whether it is ready, whether it will improve with time or whether it is past its best. Many tasting schools use tasting sheets with these headings, and some tasters add scores, although a good description is often more useful than a number.

Tips for improving

The best way to learn is to compare: tasting two wines from the same variety but different regions side by side, or two vintages of the same wine, teaches more than tasting wines one at a time. Taking brief notes helps fix memories. Tasting blind, without seeing the label, avoids preconceptions. And above all, remember that personal taste is legitimate: tasting does not aim to impose preferences but to understand better what is in the glass and to enjoy it in moderation.
