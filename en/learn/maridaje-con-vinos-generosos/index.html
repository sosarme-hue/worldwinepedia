Food pairing

Food pairing with fortified wines

Fortified wines, from fino to Pedro Ximénez and from Port to Madeira, are among the most versatile table companions there are; this article reviews, style by style, the dishes they suit best.

Fortified wines are those that, at some point in their production, receive an addition of grape spirit. This family includes very different styles: dry, saline wines aged under a veil of flor, highly complex oxidative wines, and sweet wines whose fermentation was halted to preserve the grape's sugar. They are often associated only with the aperitif or the end of the meal, but sommeliers have insisted for years that they are among the most versatile wines at the table, able to accompany dishes in front of which other wines fail.

Fino and manzanilla

Fino from Jerez and Montilla-Moriles and manzanilla from Sanlúcar de Barrameda are dry, biologically aged wines: they mature under a layer of yeast called flor, which protects them from oxygen and gives them pungent aromas of almond, bread dough and saline notes. In Jerez they are made from palomino; in Montilla-Moriles pedro ximénez predominates, and there it usually reaches the necessary strength naturally. Their dryness, moderate acidity and saline character make them ideal partners for:

  • salty appetisers: olives, almonds, Iberian ham, anchovies;
  • fish and shellfish, especially fried fish, prawns, clams and raw fish, including sushi;
  • ingredients considered difficult, such as artichoke, asparagus, egg or vinaigrettes;
  • cold soups such as gazpacho or salmorejo.

They are served well chilled and, once the bottle is open, should be drunk within a few days and kept in the refrigerator, as they quickly lose their freshness.

Amontillado and palo cortado

Amontillado begins life as a fino and, when the flor dies away, continues to age in contact with oxygen. It thus combines the finesse of biological ageing with the hazelnut, wood and spice aromas of oxidative ageing. It is an exceptional wine for consommés and broths, mushrooms, artichokes, fish stews, white meats and aged cheeses. Palo cortado, which unites the delicate nose of an amontillado with the body of an oloroso, goes well with small game, meat stews and hard cheeses.

Oloroso

Dry oloroso ages only oxidatively and develops body and notes of walnut, leather, tobacco and spice. Although dry, its intensity allows it to accompany dishes normally reserved for a red: oxtail, meat stews, game, grilled red meat or very mature cheeses. It is also a classic partner for Andalusian dishes cooked with wine, such as kidneys in sherry.

Sweet fortified wines: cream, Pedro Ximénez and Moscatel

Cream sherries, made by sweetening an oloroso, and above all the Pedro Ximénez and Moscatel wines made from sun-dried grapes are very sweet. Pedro Ximénez, almost black in colour, with aromas of raisin, fig, date and coffee, accompanies chocolate desserts, vanilla ice cream, nuts and also blue cheeses. Moscatels, such as those of Málaga or Chipiona, more floral and citrusy, suit fruit desserts, tarts and traditional baking.

Port

Port, made in the Douro Valley, is fortified during fermentation to preserve part of its natural sugar. Ruby and vintage Ports, with intense dark fruit, accompany dark chocolate and blue cheeses; Port with Stilton is a British classic. Tawny Ports, aged for long periods in wood, go with nut desserts, caramel, flan and aged cheeses. Dry white Port, for its part, is drunk as an aperitif, often mixed with tonic.

Madeira

Madeira wines stand out for their ageing in warm conditions, which gives them remarkable resistance to oxidation and very lively acidity. Their styles are usually named after the grape: sercial, the driest, is a great partner for consommés, soups and appetisers; verdelho, medium-dry, accompanies pâtés and nuts; bual and malvasia, sweeter, go well with chocolate, caramel and nut desserts. An open bottle of Madeira keeps in good condition for a long time.

Other fortified wines

Sicilian Marsala, in its dry versions, can be drunk as an aperitif or with cheese, although it is best known as a cooking ingredient, for example in zabaione. The vins doux naturels of southern France, such as the grenache-based wines of Banyuls and Maury or the Mediterranean Muscats, also offer interesting possibilities with desserts and cheeses.

Serving and moderation

Fortified wines are higher in alcohol than most table wines, so they are served in moderately sized glasses and in small quantities. A medium-sized white wine glass, rather than the small traditional sherry copita, allows their aromas to be better appreciated. As a general rule, the dry styles are served colder and the sweet and older ones a little less so, and all of them show at their best when accompanying food.
