Pruning and training systems: bush vines, trellises and more

Why vines are pruned, the difference between spur and cane pruning, and how bush vines, trellises, pergolas and other systems affect grapes and wine.

Left to its own devices, the vine is a liana that climbs trees, produces a great deal of wood and foliage and bears small, irregular bunches that are hard to reach. Viticulture largely consists of taming this tendency with two complementary tools: pruning, which decides each winter how much wood and how many buds to keep, and the training system, which defines the permanent shape of the plant and how its shoots are arranged in space.

What pruning is for

The vine bears fruit on shoots that grow from buds on the previous year's wood. When pruning, the grower chooses how many of these buds to leave and where. The aims are several: to regulate the quantity of grapes, since too many bunches delay and dilute ripening; to maintain the balance between leaf area and crop; to give the plant a shape suited to the climate and to the work; and to ensure the renewal of productive wood year after year.

The main pruning, known as winter or dormant pruning, takes place while the vine is at rest. During spring and summer it is completed by so-called green operations: removing surplus shoots, trimming, leaf removal and bunch thinning. Poor pruning, with large, badly placed cuts, can encourage grapevine trunk diseases such as esca, a growing problem in many vineyards. This is why pruning methods that respect sap flow and avoid unnecessary wounds have spread.

Spur and cane pruning

Two main types are distinguished. In spur pruning, short stubs of cane with one or two buds are left, spread along the arms of the plant. It is typical of bush vines and cordons and suits varieties that are fruitful on their basal buds. In cane pruning, one or two longer canes with more buds are kept and tied horizontally to the wire, usually together with a renewal spur that will provide the following year's cane. The Guyot system, widespread in France and many other regions, is the classic example. Some varieties with low fruitfulness in their first buds need cane pruning in order to crop.

Bush vines

The bush vine, called vaso in Spain, gobelet in France and alberello in Italy, is the traditional system across much of the Mediterranean and in most of Spain's old vineyards. The plant is formed without wires, with a short trunk from which several arms spread in a circle, like the fingers of an open hand, and it is spur-pruned. Its compact, low shape copes well with scarce water, since it is usually planted at low density, and the foliage shades the bunches from intense sun. It also stands up well to wind.

Its main drawback is that it is hard to mechanise: pruning, spraying and harvesting are almost always done by hand. For this reason many bush-vine vineyards have been grubbed up or converted in recent decades, although today there is renewed appreciation for old bush vines of Garnacha, Monastrell, Cariñena, Bobal or Tempranillo, which give low yields and concentrated grapes.

Trellising

A trellis trains the vine on a system of posts and wires that forms a continuous wall of foliage along the row. The canopy is kept upright by movable wires; in its most common form, known as vertical shoot positioning or VSP, shoots grow upwards and the bunches sit in a clearly defined lower zone. It can be combined with spur pruning on a cordon, with one or two permanent horizontal arms, or with Guyot-style cane pruning.

Its advantages are good exposure of the leaves to light, better air circulation around the bunches, which reduces disease, and the possibility of mechanising most tasks. It has become dominant in many regions of the world. In very hot climates, however, excessive exposure of the bunches can cause sunburn, so row orientation and canopy management are adjusted accordingly.

Pergolas and other systems

In humid areas vines have traditionally been trained high. In Rías Baixas, the parral, with foliage spread over a raised mesh supported by posts, often made of granite, keeps bunches away from the damp ground and improves ventilation against fungal diseases. Similar systems, the pergola, are used in northern Italy, for example in Trentino and Alto Adige.

There are also systems that divide the canopy into two planes to increase the exposed area in vigorous vineyards, such as the Lyre or the Geneva Double Curtain, and high-yielding ones such as the tendone in some Italian areas. In extreme soils or climates growers use local solutions: on Lanzarote, vines grow in hollows surrounded by stone walls that shelter them from the wind; on the Greek island of Santorini, they are coiled into basket shapes on the ground.

Density and balance

The choice of system goes hand in hand with planting density. High-density vineyards, typical of Burgundy or Champagne, aim for each plant to produce little and for roots to compete with each other. Low-density vineyards, common in dry areas, share scarce water among fewer plants. There is no best system in the abstract: the right one depends on climate, soil, variety, rootstock vigour, the availability of labour and the style of wine sought.

A long-term decision

Changing training systems is costly and slow, because the shape of the plant is built over several years. That is why it is one of the most important decisions when planting a vineyard. With rising temperatures, some growers are again valuing systems such as the bush vine, which shade the bunches better, while others are adapting the height and orientation of their trellises to create shade and keep the grapes fresh.
