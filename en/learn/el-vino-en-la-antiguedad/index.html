Wine in antiquity: Georgia, Egypt, Greece and Rome

From the first vessels of the Caucasus to Roman amphorae: how wine was born and spread across the Mediterranean to become a pillar of European culture.

Wine is one of humanity's oldest drinks. Its history is bound up with the domestication of the vine, the birth of agriculture and the first civilisations of the Near East and the Mediterranean. Archaeology, residue chemistry and genetics have made it possible in recent decades to reconstruct much of this journey, although many questions remain open.

Origins in the Caucasus and the Near East

The wild grapevine, Vitis vinifera subspecies sylvestris, grows naturally across a broad band stretching from Central Asia to the Iberian Peninsula. Its domestication, which turned a plant with separate sexes into a hermaphrodite, more productive one, took place according to most studies in the area between the southern Caucasus and the Near East during the Neolithic, although recent genetic research suggests there was more than one centre.

The oldest chemical evidence of grape wine known so far comes from Georgia: Neolithic pottery fragments found south of Tbilisi retain residues characteristic of wine and have been dated to around the sixth millennium BCE, some eight thousand years ago. Slightly later finds in the Zagros Mountains, in present-day Iran, and a winemaking installation in a cave in Armenia, with a pressing floor and fermentation vessels, confirm the region's early importance.

Georgia preserves an age-old tradition connected with those origins: winemaking in qvevri, large earthenware vessels buried in the ground in which wine ferments and is stored, often with its skins. This method has been inscribed by UNESCO on its list of intangible cultural heritage.

Egypt: the wine of the pharaohs

In ancient Egypt, beer was the everyday drink, while wine was associated with the elite, with temples and with funerary worship. In a royal tomb of the predynastic period at Abydos, hundreds of jars with wine residues were found, probably imported from the Levant. Later, local vineyards developed, especially in the Nile Delta and in some oases.

Tomb wall paintings show in detail the grape harvest, the treading of grapes in large vats and the pressing of skins in twisted sacks. Egyptian wine jars often bore inscriptions with the year, the place of origin and sometimes the person responsible for making it: jars labelled in this way were found in the tomb of Tutankhamun, a distant forerunner of the modern label.

The Phoenicians and the spread across the Mediterranean

The Phoenicians, great traders of the first millennium BCE, helped carry the vine and wine towards the western Mediterranean, including the south of the Iberian Peninsula. They founded settlements such as Gadir, present-day Cádiz, in an area that centuries later would become famous for its wines. Their amphorae circulated throughout the Mediterranean.

Greece: wine as culture

In ancient Greece wine occupied a central place in social, religious and literary life. It was linked to the cult of Dionysus, god of wine and theatre, and was at the heart of the symposium, a gathering of men at which they drank, talked, recited poetry and debated philosophy. The Greeks usually drank wine mixed with water in a krater, and considered it barbaric to drink it neat. It was often flavoured with resins, spices or honey; today's retsina is a reminder of the custom of flavouring wine with pine resin.

Some islands, such as Chios, Thasos and Lesbos, were famous for their wines, which travelled in amphorae stamped to indicate their origin. With Greek colonial expansion, the vine reached southern Italy, which the Greeks called Oenotria, and the Mediterranean coast of France, where they founded Massalia, present-day Marseille.

Rome: the great expansion

The Romans inherited wine culture from the Greeks and Etruscans and turned it into a large-scale agricultural and commercial activity. Authors such as Cato, Varro, Columella, who was born in Gades, and Pliny the Elder left detailed treatises on vine growing, pruning, choice of land and winemaking. Pliny compared and classified numerous varieties and wines.

Among the most prized wines was Falernian, from Campania. The Romans recognised memorable vintages and valued aged wines. Wine was fermented and stored in large earthenware jars called dolia and transported in amphorae; towards the end of the Empire, the wooden barrel, of Gaulish origin, gradually gained ground.

Legions, settlers and trade spread viticulture throughout the Empire: Gaul, with the valleys of the Rhône, the Garonne and the Moselle and later Burgundy and Champagne; Hispania, where regions such as Tarraconensis and Baetica exported wine to Rome; and the banks of the Rhine and the Danube. Many of Europe's great wine regions today have their roots in that period.

An enduring legacy

After the fall of the Western Roman Empire, viticulture survived largely thanks to the Church, which needed wine for the Eucharist, and to monasteries, which preserved and improved agricultural knowledge during the Middle Ages. In the Islamic world, although drinking was condemned by religion, the vine continued to be cultivated and bacchic poetry had notable authors.

From the ancient world we have inherited not only the basic techniques of growing and winemaking, but also the idea of wine as a product tied to a place, worthy of being compared, discussed and celebrated in moderation at a shared table.
