Biological ageing and flor

Some wines age beneath a layer of yeast that floats on their surface and protects them from oxygen. This is how Andalusian fino and manzanilla, the vin jaune of the Jura and other singular wines are born.

In most wineries around the world, a layer of yeast on the surface of a wine is a cause for alarm: it usually signals the presence of what is known as wine flowers, a form of spoilage that degrades the wine. In the Sherry district, in Montilla-Moriles or in the French Jura, however, this whitish film is carefully tended, because the character of some of the most original wines in existence depends on it. This type of ageing, in which a living organism acts on the wine for years, is called biological ageing.

What flor is

Flor is a film formed by yeasts that grow on the surface of a wine in contact with air. It consists mainly of special strains of Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the same species responsible for alcoholic fermentation, which have developed the ability to live in an already fermented wine and to form floating aggregates. Once the sugar is exhausted, these yeasts switch metabolism: instead of fermenting, they respire, feeding on alcohol, glycerol and other components of the wine and consuming the available oxygen.

The film, which can be several millimetres thick, acts as a barrier that prevents oxygen from reaching the wine. As a result, even though the wine is aged in barrels that are deliberately not filled to the top, precisely to leave a surface in contact with air, it remains protected from oxidation and keeps a pale colour.

The necessary conditions

Flor is a demanding organism. It needs a wine of moderate alcohol: in the Sherry region, wines intended for biological ageing are usually fortified to around 15 degrees, since flor yeasts do not survive above roughly 16 or 17 degrees. It also needs mild, stable temperatures and high humidity. This is why traditional Andalusian bodegas have high ceilings, thick walls and windows positioned to catch the humid Atlantic winds, and why their earthen floors are watered in summer to keep them cool.

The film is not constant throughout the year: it tends to thicken in spring and autumn, when conditions are most favourable, and to thin in the coldest and hottest months. Flor also needs nutrients; if these are not renewed, the film weakens and eventually disappears. The criadera and solera system, in which young wine is periodically added to barrels holding older wine, supplies this nourishment and keeps the flor alive for years.

How it transforms the wine

The action of flor profoundly changes the wine. The yeasts reduce glycerol, giving very dry wines that are light on the palate; they consume some of the alcohol and volatile acidity; and they produce large amounts of acetaldehyde, the compound responsible for the pungent aroma characteristic of these wines. To this are added notes of bread dough, nuts, especially almonds, and wild herbs. The wine also becomes paler, because compounds that could oxidise and darken it do not come into contact with oxygen.

Fino and manzanilla

The best-known examples of biological ageing are the finos of the Sherry district and of Montilla-Moriles, and the manzanilla of Sanlúcar de Barrameda. They are made from the Palomino variety in Jerez and Sanlúcar, and mainly from Pedro Ximénez in Montilla-Moriles, where the grapes naturally reach sufficient alcohol, so that many finos from this area need no fortification.

Manzanilla is aged in Sanlúcar, at the mouth of the Guadalquivir, where the maritime influence encourages a particularly thick and persistent flor. The result is a very delicate wine with subtle aromas and a salty touch. These wines are bottled after years of ageing and are best drunk fairly soon once opened, because without the protection of the flor they quickly lose their freshness.

Mixed ageing

When the flor disappears, either naturally or because the cellar master raises the wine's alcohol, oxidative ageing in direct contact with the air begins. This is how amontillado is produced, combining the features of biological and oxidative ageing: an amber colour, aromas of hazelnut and wood, and greater body. In other Andalusian wines, such as oloroso, ageing is oxidative from the start, because they are fortified to a strength at which flor cannot develop.

Beyond Andalusia

Ageing under flor is not exclusive to southern Spain. In the Jura, in eastern France, vin jaune is made from the Savagnin grape and aged at length in barrels that are not topped up, beneath a film known there simply as the voile; it cannot be released until more than six years after the harvest. Its most famous example is Château-Chalon, and it is sold in a distinctive bottle called the clavelin. Unlike fino, vin jaune is not fortified and is aged in individual barrels, without a solera system.

Other wines also undergo forms of ageing under flor, such as Vernaccia di Oristano in Sardinia and some dry Szamorodni from Tokaj in Hungary. In each case, local conditions and grape varieties produce different results, but the principle is the same.

A delicate balance

Biological ageing demands constant vigilance. The cellar master must monitor the state of the flor, the alcohol level of the wine and the temperature and humidity of the bodega, and decide the fate of each barrel according to how it develops. A weak film can let oxidation in; a contaminated one, unwanted spoilage. Because of this complexity, and the patience they require, biologically aged wines are regarded as one of the most singular expressions of winemaking, in which the work of a microorganism takes centre stage.
