Wine tourism: how to visit a winery

How to plan a winery visit, what kinds of visits exist, how to behave during a tasting and how to combine the trip with responsible drinking.

Visiting a winery is one of the best ways to understand wine. Walking through the vineyards, seeing where the wine ferments and ages and talking to the people who make it help to link what is in the glass with a landscape, a climate and a way of working. Wine tourism has grown considerably in recent decades in almost every wine region in the world, from the great classic areas to small, little-known districts.

Planning the visit

Most wineries, especially small ones, are not permanently open to the public. The usual practice is to book ahead, by phone, email or through their website. When booking it is worth checking:

  • Opening times and how long the visit lasts.
  • The languages in which it is offered.
  • What is included: the tour, how many wines are tasted, any snacks or a meal.
  • Whether it is accessible for people with reduced mobility.
  • The conditions for minors, if travelling as a family.

It is better not to cram too many visits into one day. Two, or at most three, allow you to enjoy each one without rushing and without drinking too much. Many regions have organised wine routes, tourist offices and visitor centres that make planning easier and suggest itineraries combining wineries, heritage, food and nature.

When to go

Each season has its interest. Spring shows the vine budding; summer, the growing bunches; autumn, the harvest and the colours of the leaves; and winter, pruning and the calm of the cellars. Harvest is a very attractive time but also the busiest: some wineries reduce visits during those weeks, while others organise special activities. It is worth asking.

Types of visit

The offer is very varied:

  • Classic guided tour: a walk through the facilities, from grape reception to the barrel hall and bottling line, followed by a tasting of several wines.
  • Vineyard visit: walks or drives to see the plots, soils and grape varieties.
  • Special tastings: verticals of several vintages of the same wine, comparisons of individual plots, or tastings paired with local produce.
  • Hands-on activities: tasting workshops, taking part in the harvest, making your own blend.
  • Combined experiences: a meal at the winery, accommodation, cultural or sporting activities.

At large wineries the visit is usually highly organised; at small ones it is often the grower or a family member who shows visitors round, allowing a more direct conversation.

During the visit

A few guidelines make the experience more pleasant for everyone:

  • Arrive on time, as visits are usually in groups and on a tight schedule.
  • Wear comfortable shoes and suitable clothing: vineyards may be muddy or stony, and underground cellars are cool even in summer.
  • Avoid strong perfumes, which interfere with the wine's aromas, for yourself and for others.
  • Follow instructions about photography and restricted areas; some spaces carry risks, such as tanks during fermentation, which give off carbon dioxide.
  • Ask questions: guides and winemakers usually appreciate interest in the work of the vineyard and cellar.

Tasting and responsible drinking

At a tasting, small amounts of each wine are poured. There is no obligation to finish them: wineries almost always provide a spittoon, and using it is common practice among professionals that nobody considers impolite. You can also pour into it any wine you do not wish to drink. It is advisable to eat something beforehand and to drink water during the visit.

If travelling by car, the most sensible approach is for the driver not to drink alcohol, to book an organised tour with transport, or to use taxis or public transport. Legal drink-driving limits vary from country to country, but the safest option is always not to drink if you are going to drive. Many wineries offer grape juice, other juices or non-alcoholic drinks for drivers and companions.

Buying wine

At the end, most wineries have a shop. Buying is not obligatory, though it is a good way to take home a memento and support small producers; sometimes you will find wines that are not distributed outside the area. If flying, check the rules on carrying liquids and the allowances at customs in the destination country; some wineries offer shipping.

Beyond the winery

Wine tourism is also an invitation to discover the territory: villages, churches and castles, markets, local cuisine, wine museums and landscapes. In many regions the vineyard landscape is part of the cultural heritage, and some have received international recognition. Travelling respectfully, towards local communities and the environment, helps these regions keep their identity.
