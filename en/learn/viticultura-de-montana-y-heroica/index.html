Viticulture

Mountain and heroic viticulture

What is meant by heroic viticulture, how vines are worked on extreme slopes, terraces and at high altitude, and what characterises the wines of these landscapes.

In some corners of the world, vines grow in places where it seems impossible to cultivate anything: slopes so steep that they have to be worked while tied to a rope, terraces held up by hand-built stone walls, vineyards at great altitude or on small, windswept islands. The expression heroic viticulture has spread as a way to describe this kind of cultivation, emphasising the human effort it demands.

A definition

The term is associated above all with the work of CERVIM, a research centre based in the Aosta Valley devoted to mountain and steep-slope viticulture. According to its criteria, a vineyard can be considered heroic if it meets at least one of these conditions:

  • it lies above five hundred metres in altitude, excluding plateaux;
  • it has slopes of more than thirty per cent;
  • it is planted on terraces;
  • it is found on small islands.

Beyond the technical definition, what unites these vineyards is difficulty: almost all the work must be done by hand, mechanisation is very limited or impossible, and production costs are far higher than on flat land.

Working the slope

On a steep hillside, every task is multiplied. Pruning, spraying, leaf removal and harvesting are carried out on foot, with tools and crates carried along narrow paths. In some places small rail tracks with carts, monorails or cables have been installed to bring the grapes up and down; elsewhere, everything is still carried on people's shoulders.

The dry-stone walls that hold up the terraces need constant maintenance: heavy rain, frost and roots can bring them down, and rebuilding them requires a craft that is becoming ever rarer. These walls nonetheless perform valuable functions: they slow erosion, store heat during the day and release it at night, and create habitats for plants and animals.

Emblematic landscapes

Among the best-known examples are:

  • Ribeira Sacra, in Galicia, with dizzying terraces above the canyons of the Miño and Sil rivers, where Mencía predominates.
  • The Douro valley in Portugal, whose schist terraces, carved out over centuries, form one of the most spectacular wine landscapes in the world.
  • The Mosel in Germany, with slate slopes above the river planted mainly with Riesling.
  • The Wachau in Austria, with stone terraces beside the Danube.
  • The Valais in Switzerland, with terraced vineyards along the upper Rhône.
  • In Italy, areas such as Valtellina, the Aosta Valley and the Cinque Terre, and the volcanic slopes of Etna, where vineyards reach considerable heights.

Several of these landscapes have been recognised by UNESCO as World Heritage sites, among them the Alto Douro wine region and the Wachau valley.

The role of altitude

Altitude changes the vineyard's climate. With every hundred metres of height, the average temperature falls, and the difference between day and night usually becomes greater. Solar radiation is more intense, especially ultraviolet, which in some varieties encourages thicker skins richer in phenolic compounds.

These conditions allow grapes to ripen more slowly and to retain their acidity better, which is why altitude has become a tool for adapting to climate change. In warm regions, such as parts of Argentina, Spain or Italy, planting higher makes it possible to produce fresher wines. At the same time, altitude brings risks: spring and autumn frosts, shorter growing seasons and greater exposure to wind and hail.

Slope and aspect

On hillsides, aspect is decisive. In the northern hemisphere, slopes facing south or south-east receive more sun and warm up earlier, which is essential in cool climates such as the Mosel's, where the gradient and the reflection from the river help the grapes ripen. A slope also helps water and cold air drain away —cold air tends to collect at the bottom of valleys— and so reduces the risk of frost.

The wines

There is no single style of heroic wine, because the places are so different. Often, however, these are wines of strong personality, with lively acidity, precise aromas and a close link to poor soils of slate, granite, schist or volcanic rock. Production is limited and frequently comes from small plots and from local varieties that have survived thanks to isolation.

A heritage at risk

Heroic viticulture faces serious threats: land abandonment, rural depopulation, a lack of younger people to take over, and production costs that are hard to cover. In many areas, terraces that were cultivated for centuries have been overgrown by scrub. Associations, public programmes and committed producers are working to recover plots, rebuild walls and make these wines better known, their value lying as much in the glass as in the preservation of unique landscapes.
