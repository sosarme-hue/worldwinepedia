Polyphenols, colour and tannins

Anthocyanins, tannins and other phenolic compounds explain a wine's colour, its astringency and much of how it evolves over time.

Polyphenols, or phenolic compounds, are a large family of molecules found in grapes, mainly in the skins and seeds and to a lesser extent in the pulp. In wine they are responsible for colour, astringency and part of the bitterness, and they play a role in ageing potential. Understanding them helps explain why a young red is purple and rough and, with the years, turns brick-coloured and silky.

Where they are in the grape

In most red varieties the colour is concentrated in the skin; the pulp is colourless. That is why white wine can be made from black grapes, as happens with much of the Champagne made from Pinot Noir. There are exceptions, the so-called teinturier grapes, whose flesh is also coloured. Tannins are found in both skins and seeds, and also in the stems if the grapes are not destemmed.

The main groups of wine polyphenols are the flavonoids, notably anthocyanins, flavanols (which form tannins) and flavonols, and the non-flavonoids, such as hydroxycinnamic acids and stilbenes, including resveratrol.

Anthocyanins and colour

Anthocyanins are the red and bluish pigments of black grapes. In Vitis vinifera the most abundant is usually malvidin, in its glucoside form. Their colour depends heavily on pH: in the acidic medium of wine they take on red hues, and the lower the pH, the more intense and vivid the red. Sulphur dioxide also reversibly bleaches part of the anthocyanins.

Varieties differ greatly in anthocyanin content. Pinot Noir gives wines of moderate colour, while others, such as Syrah or Monastrell, usually give much deeper colours. Climate and the exposure of bunches to sunlight also have an influence.

Free anthocyanins are unstable. Over time they combine with tannins and with other molecules, such as acetaldehyde or pyruvic acid, forming more stable, more orange-toned polymeric pigments and pyranoanthocyanins. This process explains how the colour of reds evolves from youthful purple to garnet and brick in maturity. There is also copigmentation, an association of anthocyanins with other colourless compounds that intensifies the colour of young wines.

Tannins and astringency

Wine tannins are mainly proanthocyanidins, chains of flavanol units such as catechin and epicatechin. Seed tannins tend to be shorter and can taste harder and more bitter; skin tannins are usually larger and feel softer. To these are added the hydrolysable tannins released by oak during barrel ageing.

Astringency is not a taste but a tactile sensation. It occurs when tannins bind to saliva proteins, which act as a lubricant, and make them precipitate. The mouth feels dry and rough. Perception depends on the type and quantity of tannin, the wine's acidity, alcohol and the presence of polysaccharides, which can soften it.

This interaction with proteins also explains something familiar at the table: a tannic red seems friendlier when drunk with meat or cheese, because the proteins and fats in the food take up part of the tannins and protect the saliva. Conversely, a very astringent wine drunk on its own may come across as dry and hard. Tasters describe tannins with tactile terms such as grainy, fine, silky, drying or polished, which refer to their size, their ripeness and how well they are integrated into the wine as a whole.

Extraction in the cellar

In red winemaking the must ferments in contact with the skins, and the winemaker controls extraction with several tools. Temperature and alcohol favour the dissolution of compounds. Pump-overs and punch-downs bring the liquid into contact with the cap of skins. The length of maceration is crucial: anthocyanins are extracted early, while tannins, especially seed tannins, increase as maceration is prolonged. A cold pre-fermentation maceration aims to extract colour and aromas before alcohol appears.

Grape ripeness also matters. Growers speak of phenolic ripeness to describe the development of tannins and colour, which does not always coincide with sugar ripeness. Seeds that have turned from green to brown usually indicate less aggressive tannins.

Evolution with time and oxygen

During ageing, tannins and anthocyanins keep reacting. Small inputs of oxygen, through the barrel or techniques such as micro-oxygenation, encourage condensation reactions mediated by acetaldehyde that stabilise colour and change the feel of the tannins. Over the years some of these compounds form molecules so large that they precipitate and settle in the bottle as sediment, which explains why old reds are less astringent and sometimes need decanting.

White wines also contain polyphenols, though in much smaller quantities. Their oxidation is responsible for the browning of a white exposed to too much air. That is why many whites are made by protecting the must from oxygen and limiting skin contact, while in so-called orange wines, made by macerating white grapes, polyphenols bring an amber colour and some tannic texture.

Polyphenols and health

Some wine polyphenols, such as resveratrol, have attracted scientific interest for their antioxidant properties in laboratory studies. However, the relationship between wine consumption and health is complex, and alcohol has well-documented harmful effects. A rigorous encyclopedia cannot present wine as a remedy: polyphenols are, above all, a key to understanding its colour, its texture and its life in the bottle.
