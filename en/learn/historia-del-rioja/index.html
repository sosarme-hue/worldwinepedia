History & culture

The history of Rioja

From medieval vineyards to Spain's first qualified designation of origin: how Rioja forged its oak-ageing style and its international prestige.

Rioja is probably the best-known Spanish wine region in the world. It stretches along the Ebro valley between the Sierra de Cantabria and the Sierra de la Demanda, taking in parts of La Rioja, the Basque Country (Rioja Alavesa) and Navarre. Its history combines a wine tradition many centuries old with a decisive moment in the second half of the nineteenth century, when the region adopted Bordeaux techniques and found an unexpected market in France.

Ancient and medieval roots

There are signs of viticulture in the Ebro valley since Roman times. In the Middle Ages, monasteries played an important part: San Millán de la Cogolla, famous for the Glosas Emilianenses, one of the oldest written records of an Iberian Romance language, owned vineyards, and the region preserves medieval documents mentioning vineyards and wine presses. The Way of Saint James, which crosses the region, also encouraged the movement of people and goods.

For centuries, Rioja wine was drunk mainly in the region itself and in neighbouring lands to the north, such as the Basque Country and Cantabria. Towns and villages regulated its trade, and the cellars dug into hillsides, such as the clusters of underground cellars in some villages, are a legacy of that period.

The problem of transport and keeping

Until the nineteenth century, the region's wines were mostly young wines, made from one year to the next and poorly suited to long journeys. Poor connections with the ports and the cost of transport over mountain roads limited their reach. Attempts to improve winemaking were already made in the eighteenth century; Manuel Quintano, a cleric from Labastida, is often mentioned for travelling to Bordeaux to study its methods and trying to apply them, with results that did not take hold.

The influence of Bordeaux

The decisive change came in the mid-nineteenth century. Two aristocrats, Luciano de Murrieta, later Marquis of Murrieta, and Camilo Hurtado de Amézaga, Marquis of Riscal, introduced in their wineries winemaking and ageing techniques inspired by Bordeaux, including the use of the oak barrel of about 225 litres. Elciego, in Rioja Alavesa, and Logroño were the scenes of those first steps. The result was more stable wines that could travel and improve with time.

French phylloxera and the arrival of the railway

From the 1860s, phylloxera began to destroy the vineyards of France. French merchants, especially from Bordeaux, looked for wine in other countries, and Rioja, relatively close and still free of the pest, became a major supplier. Some settled in the region and brought technical know-how.

The arrival of the railway was crucial. The line linking Tudela with Bilbao passed through Haro, and around its station several wineries were established in the last decades of the nineteenth century that would in time become historic houses; Haro's so-called Barrio de la Estación, the station district, is now a symbol of that era. In 1892 an Oenological Station was founded in Haro, devoted to studying and improving winemaking.

Crisis and reorganisation

The boom did not last. When France replanted its vineyards on American rootstocks, demand for Rioja wine fell. At the same time, phylloxera itself reached Rioja at the end of the nineteenth century, and the region had to replant with grafted vines. The first decades of the twentieth century were difficult.

To defend the wine's reputation against imitations, protection of the name was pursued. In the 1920s the designation of origin was created and its Regulatory Council set up, one of the oldest in Spain. Wars and the post-war years then held back its development, but in the second half of the century controls were tightened and the region enjoyed a new expansion, with new wineries founded and many others modernised.

The classic style

The model that defined Rioja's image for much of the twentieth century was that of wines with extended ageing in barrel, traditionally American oak, with categories such as crianza, reserva and gran reserva, distinguished by the minimum length of ageing. The reds were based mainly on Tempranillo, joined by Garnacha, Mazuelo, the local name for Carignan, and Graciano. Whites from Viura, the Rioja name for Macabeo, sometimes with long ageing, were also important.

From DOCa to wines of place

In 1991, Rioja became the first Spanish region to be granted the status of Denominación de Origen Calificada, the most demanding category in Spanish wine law. The following decades brought diversification: alongside the classic style came wines with more emphasis on fruit, greater use of French oak and growing interest in old vineyards and in the expression of each area.

That evolution was reflected in the rules. The designation recognises three zones, Rioja Alta, Rioja Alavesa and Rioja Oriental, the last formerly called Rioja Baja, and in recent years it has added indications for municipalities and for viñedo singular, single vineyards, which make it possible to highlight a wine's precise origin. Thus a region that built its fame on ageing and on blending from different sources now also pays attention to place, without abandoning the style that made it famous.
