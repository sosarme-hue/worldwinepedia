History & culture

Champagne and the traditional method

How a still wine from northern France became the world's most famous sparkling wine, what truth there is in the Dom Pérignon legend and how a wine with second fermentation in bottle is made.

Champagne is probably the wine most closely associated with celebration. Yet for much of its history the Champagne region produced still wines, and bubbles were long considered a fault. The transformation of these wines into sparkling wines was a slow process involving climate, trade, science and glassmaking, and it gave rise to a winemaking method imitated today all over the world.

A northern vineyard

Champagne lies in north-eastern France, close to the northern limit of vine growing in Europe. Its cool climate and chalk soils produce grapes with high acidity, ideal for sparkling wine. Viticulture in the area dates back to Roman times and developed in the Middle Ages around the abbeys and the city of Reims, where the kings of France were crowned. The region's wines, mostly pale reds, competed at court with those of Burgundy.

Accidental bubbles

In such a cold climate, fermentation often stopped with the arrival of winter, before all the sugar had been consumed. In spring, as temperatures rose, it resumed in barrels or bottles, and the carbon dioxide was trapped. In the region this was seen as a problem: bottles burst and fizzy wines were considered faulty.

It was in England that this sparkling character was first appreciated. The English imported Champagne wine in barrels and bottled it themselves, using stronger glass made in coal-fired furnaces and cork stoppers. In 1662 the scientist Christopher Merret presented a paper to the Royal Society describing the practice of adding sugar to wine to make it sparkle, one of the first written records of a deliberately induced second fermentation.

Dom Pérignon: myth and reality

A widespread legend credits the invention of champagne to Dom Pierre Pérignon, a Benedictine monk in charge of the cellar at the abbey of Hautvillers between the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries. Historians agree that he did not invent sparkling wine. His real contribution was important but different: he improved the quality of the abbey's wines through careful selection of grapes, the art of blending different plots and the making of white wines from black grapes, pressing them gently so that the skins would not colour the must.

The nineteenth century: an industry is born

During the eighteenth century, the taste for sparkling champagne spread at the French court and other European courts, and the first trading houses were founded. But production remained risky: pressure was unpredictable and many bottles burst. During the nineteenth century, several advances turned champagne into a reliable product. Early in the century, at the house run by the widow Clicquot, the riddling rack was developed, a board with angled holes that made it easier to turn the bottles and move the sediment towards the neck. Around the 1830s the pharmacist Jean-Baptiste François established a method of measuring sugar and calculating the amount needed for the second fermentation, which greatly reduced breakages.

Nineteenth-century champagnes were generally quite sweet. The drier styles that dominate today developed gradually in response to demand from certain export markets, particularly Britain.

Protection and crisis

At the beginning of the twentieth century, the delimitation of the production area caused tension and, in 1911, serious riots among growers protesting against the use of grapes from outside the region. The appellation d'origine contrôlée Champagne was recognised in the 1930s. Today the name is protected in the European Union and in many countries, and it may only be used for wines from the region made according to its rules.

How it is made: the traditional method

The traditional method, called méthode champenoise in Champagne, is based on a second fermentation in the same bottle that reaches the consumer. Its main stages are as follows:

  • Base wine: the grapes, mainly Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Meunier, are pressed gently and the must is fermented into still, dry, very acidic wines.
  • Blending: wines from different varieties, plots and, in non-vintage champagnes, different years, including reserve wines, are blended to achieve a consistent style.
  • Tirage: the wine is bottled with the liqueur de tirage, a mixture of sugar and yeast, and the bottle is sealed, usually with a crown cap.
  • Second fermentation: the yeasts consume the sugar in the bottle, producing alcohol and carbon dioxide, which remains dissolved in the wine.
  • Ageing on the lees: after fermentation, the wine stays in contact with the dead yeast cells. Their autolysis contributes notes of bread, pastry and nuts and a finer mousse. In Champagne the minimum ageing is fifteen months for non-vintage wines and three years for vintage wines; many producers extend it.
  • Riddling: the bottles are gradually turned and tilted, by hand in riddling racks or by machines called gyropalettes, until the sediment collects in the neck.
  • Disgorgement: the neck of the bottle is frozen and, when the bottle is opened, the pressure expels the plug of ice containing the sediment.
  • Liqueur d'expédition: the bottle is topped up with a small amount of wine and often sugar. This dosage determines the style: brut nature, extra brut, brut, extra dry, sec, demi-sec or doux.

The traditional method beyond Champagne

The method is used in many regions of the world, but outside Champagne it may not be called champenoise: the terms traditional or classic method are used instead. In Spain, cava, made mainly in Penedès from varieties such as Macabeo, Xarel·lo and Parellada, has followed this method since the second half of the nineteenth century. In France it is used for the crémants of various regions; in Italy, by appellations such as Franciacorta and Trento; and in other countries, by many quality sparkling wines. A technique born in a vineyard in northern France has thus become the common heritage of sparkling-wine makers all over the world.
