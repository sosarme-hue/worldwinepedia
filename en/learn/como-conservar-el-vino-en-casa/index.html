How to store wine at home

Temperature, humidity, light and bottle position: how to store wine at home, which wines are worth ageing and how long an open bottle lasts.

You do not need an underground cellar to store wine well. It is enough to know what harms it and to avoid those things as far as possible. Wine is a living product that keeps evolving in the bottle, and poor conditions can speed up its ageing or spoil it within a few months. At the same time, it is worth remembering that the vast majority of wines are made to be drunk in their first few years, so for many households the aim is not to age wine but to keep it in good condition until it is opened.

Wine's enemies

Five factors most affect stored wine: heat, temperature fluctuations, light, excessively dry air and, to a lesser extent, vibration and strong odours.

Temperature

Temperature is the most important factor. Heat speeds up chemical reactions and makes wine evolve too quickly, losing freshness and fruit and, in extreme cases, acquiring cooked-fruit notes. Prolonged exposure to temperatures above about 25 °C greatly increases the risk, and exposure to very high temperatures, as in a car in the sun, can spoil a bottle quickly.

A storage temperature of roughly 10 to 15 °C is usually recommended. Even more important than the exact figure is stability: sudden or frequent changes make the wine expand and contract, which can let air in past the cork or even push the cork out. A slightly warmer but stable place is preferable to a cooler one with large swings.

Light

Light, especially ultraviolet light from the sun and some lamps, causes reactions that alter the wine's aroma, particularly in whites, rosés and sparkling wines in clear glass bottles, which can develop so-called light strike. Bottles are therefore best kept in a dark place or in their boxes. Dark glass protects, but not completely.

Humidity and position

Wines with natural cork stoppers are traditionally stored on their side, so that the wine keeps the cork moist and it does not dry out and shrink, which would let air in. For the same reason, moderate ambient humidity of around 60 to 75% is recommended; very dry air can dry out corks during long storage, while very damp air encourages mould on labels and corks, although it does not usually affect the wine.

Wines with screw caps or synthetic closures can be stored upright without any problem. For short periods of a few weeks, position matters little even with cork.

Vibration and odours

Continuous vibration, such as that from some household appliances, can affect the development of wines intended for long ageing and disturb the sediment in old reds. Strong smells from cleaning products, paint or fuel are best avoided where bottles are stored, especially for long periods.

Where to keep wine at home

In many homes the best option is a cupboard or an interior corner, away from the kitchen, heating and windows, preferably on the lower floor, where the temperature is cooler and more stable. A basement or garage can work if it does not undergo big temperature changes between seasons.

For those who keep a considerable number of bottles or wines for long ageing, wine cabinets with climate control maintain a controlled temperature and humidity. A domestic refrigerator, by contrast, is not suitable for keeping wine for months: it is too cold and dry and it vibrates, though it is perfectly useful for chilling a bottle before serving or keeping one that has been opened.

Which wines are worth keeping

Only a small proportion of wines improves with years in bottle. Wines with ageing potential typically include reds with good structure, acidity and tannin, such as many great wines from Rioja, Ribera del Duero, Priorat, Bordeaux, Barolo or Burgundy; some high-acid or barrel-aged whites, such as certain Rieslings or white Burgundies; some long-aged sparkling wines; and concentrated sweet and fortified wines. Young whites and rosés, light reds and most everyday wines are meant to be drunk within a year or two, and keeping them longer simply makes them lose freshness.

Biologically aged fortified wines such as fino and manzanilla are best enjoyed relatively fresh after bottling, although some enthusiasts appreciate their development in bottle. Olorosos and other oxidatively aged wines are generally very stable.

An opened bottle

Once opened, the bottle comes into contact with oxygen and the wine begins to oxidise. To extend its life, re-seal it and keep it in the refrigerator, even if it is red; take it out a little while before serving. Vacuum pumps and devices that add inert gas help reduce contact with air, and decanting the wine into a smaller bottle filled to the top is also effective.

As a general guide, still wines stay in good condition for a few days, although this depends greatly on the wine: tannic reds and high-acid whites hold up somewhat better. Sparkling wines lose their bubbles quickly, although a dedicated airtight stopper can keep them for a day or two. Fino and manzanilla, once opened, should be treated like a white wine and drunk soon; olorosos, Pedro Ximénez and tawny ports last considerably longer.

Keeping a record

For anyone who keeps several bottles, it is useful to note which wines you have, when they were bought and their approximate drinking window. This avoids discovering too late that a special bottle is past its best. Storing wine well is, in the end, a way of respecting the work of those who made it and of enjoying it fully when the occasion arrives.
