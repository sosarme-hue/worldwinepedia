History & culture

Wine comes to the New World

How the European vine crossed the oceans with conquistadors, missionaries and settlers, from Mexico and the Andes to California, the Cape, Australia and New Zealand.

Until the end of the fifteenth century, the European vine, Vitis vinifera, was grown only in Europe, western Asia and North Africa. The Americas had wild vine species, but their grapes had not been used to make wine in the way it was made in the Mediterranean. Within a few centuries, vinifera spread to every inhabited continent. That expansion, tied to European colonisation, is why we speak today of New World wines.

A religious and everyday necessity

For Spanish and Portuguese conquistadors and missionaries, wine was indispensable: the Mass required it and it was part of the normal diet. Bringing it from Europe was expensive and slow, and wine often spoiled on the voyage. The solution was to plant vineyards in the new lands. The first vines probably came from the Canary Islands and the Iberian Peninsula, as cuttings or seeds.

Mexico, the first stage

In New Spain, cultivation was encouraged very early. In 1524, Hernán Cortés issued ordinances requiring holders of encomiendas to plant a set number of vines each year in proportion to the indigenous people under their charge. In the north, at Parras in today's state of Coahuila, a wine estate was founded at the end of the sixteenth century that is often described as the oldest winery on the continent still in operation.

The Spanish crown at times issued rules limiting the planting of vineyards in the Indies, to protect the trade in wine from Spain. Enforcement was patchy and varied by region, and viticulture kept developing, above all where conditions were most favourable.

Peru, Chile and Argentina

The coastal valleys of Peru became an important wine area in the sixteenth century. Distillation of wine also developed there, giving rise to pisco. From Peru the vine moved south.

In Chile, the Spanish planted vines in the mid-sixteenth century, and vineyards spread through the Central Valley and southern areas such as the Itata valley. In Argentina, the vine arrived from Chile and from Upper Peru and took root in the irrigated oases at the foot of the Andes, in Mendoza and San Juan, as well as in the north-west.

The variety that dominated these regions for centuries was a grape of Spanish origin, Listán Prieto, known in Chile as País, in Argentina as Criolla Chica and in California as Mission. Its presence throughout Spanish America is the living trace of that first expansion.

The California missions

California was one of the last frontiers of the Spanish empire. From 1769, Franciscans led by Father Junípero Serra founded a chain of missions along the coast, where vines were planted to make wine for Mass and for daily use. In the nineteenth century, under Mexican and then American sovereignty, European immigrants drove commercial viticulture forward. The Gold Rush brought people and demand, and by the end of the century areas such as Sonoma and the Napa Valley already had well-known wineries.

The East Coast and American vines

On the Atlantic coast of North America, English colonists repeatedly tried to grow European vines, without lasting success. Diseases, cold winters and, as was later understood, phylloxera killed them off. Thomas Jefferson was among those who tried in Virginia. Eastern viticulture long relied on native or hybrid varieties such as Concord. This contact between American and European vines would have unexpected consequences: in the nineteenth century, pests of American origin such as phylloxera reached Europe.

South Africa: wine of the Cape

The Dutch East India Company set up a supply station at the Cape of Good Hope in 1652. Its commander, Jan van Riebeeck, recorded in his diary the first winemaking at the Cape, in 1659. Later, Governor Simon van der Stel founded the Constantia estate, whose sweet wine became very famous in Europe in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The arrival of French Huguenots at the end of the seventeenth century, who settled around Franschhoek, strengthened the wine tradition.

Australia and New Zealand

The first vines reached Australia with the British fleet that founded the colony of New South Wales in 1788. Early attempts near Sydney had little success, but in the following decades viticulture took hold. James Busby, regarded as one of the pioneers of Australian wine, gathered a large collection of varieties in Europe in the 1830s, which helped spread them in the colony. The Hunter Valley was one of the first notable regions, and German immigration was decisive in the development of the Barossa Valley.

In New Zealand, the missionary Samuel Marsden planted vines in 1819, and Busby himself made wine there in the 1830s. The New Zealand industry, however, did not develop significantly until the second half of the twentieth century.

From colonial wine to modern wine

During the nineteenth century, French varieties such as Cabernet Sauvignon, Malbec and Carmenère reached these countries, often brought by European immigrants or technicians, sometimes just before phylloxera devastated the French vineyards. In Chile, for example, Carmenère was grown for decades under the mistaken name of Merlot, until it was identified in the 1990s.

The term New World, applied to wine, is therefore a convention: some of these vineyards have almost five centuries of history. What unites them is the European origin of their vines and a path shaped by colonisation, immigration and, over the past half-century, profound modernisation.
