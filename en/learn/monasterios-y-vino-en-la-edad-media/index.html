Monasteries and wine in the Middle Ages

The role of monks, abbeys and bishops in preserving and refining European viticulture between the fall of Rome and the end of the Middle Ages.

After the Western Roman Empire broke apart in the fifth century, Europe went through centuries of political fragmentation, insecurity and urban decline. Viticulture did not disappear, but it changed hands. Among those who kept it alive and improved it, the Church and, above all, the monasteries stand out. Their role has sometimes been idealised, since nobles, peasants and towns also owned vineyards, but their influence on the history of European wine is beyond dispute.

Why the Church needed wine

The most obvious reason was liturgical: the Eucharist requires wine, and every church, cathedral and monastery needed a regular supply. Other needs added to this. Wine was part of the daily diet in much of Europe, it was offered to guests and pilgrims, it had medicinal uses according to the knowledge of the time, and it provided income when the surplus was sold.

Bishops, based in former Roman towns, often kept the vineyards around them. But it was the monastic communities that, thanks to their stability and organisation, developed a more continuous form of viticulture.

The Rule of Saint Benedict

The Rule of Saint Benedict, written in the sixth century, became the way of life for much of Western monasticism. It made manual labour part of the monk's day and regulated wine drinking with moderation: it set a daily ration, the hemina, and urged sobriety, while allowing the ration to be adjusted to local conditions and to the work being done. Wine thus appeared as part of ordinary life, never as an end in itself.

Monasteries were stable communities that lasted for centuries. That continuity allowed something few lay owners could afford: observing the same plots year after year, comparing results and passing on accumulated experience in writing.

Cluny and Cîteaux in Burgundy

Two of the great forces of medieval monasticism met in Burgundy. The abbey of Cluny, founded in 910, headed a vast network of Benedictine houses and built up considerable holdings, vineyards included. The Cistercian order, born in 1098 at the abbey of Cîteaux near Dijon, called for a return to austerity and work on the land, and its monks and lay brothers became skilled farmers.

Through gifts and purchases, the Cistercians assembled plots in the Côte de Nuits and built the wall surrounding the Clos de Vougeot, one of the best-known images of monastic viticulture. It is often said that the monks noticed that neighbouring plots gave wines of different character, helping to shape the Burgundian idea of climats. This is a plausible and widely held view, though the precise delimitation of those sites is the work of many centuries and many hands, not of the monasteries alone.

From the Rhine to the Iberian Peninsula

Cistercian expansion carried its farming know-how across Europe. In the Rheingau, Eberbach Abbey, founded in the twelfth century, became one of the great wine centres of the Rhine, with extensive vineyards and a busy river trade. In Champagne, many abbeys had owned vines since the early Middle Ages.

In the Iberian Peninsula, the Christian reconquest and resettlement were closely bound up with the founding of monasteries, which received land and brought it under cultivation. In La Rioja, the monastery of San Millán de la Cogolla, famous for the Glosas Emilianenses, had vineyards around it. In Galicia, the name Ribeira Sacra is traditionally linked to the abundance of monasteries and hermitages in the canyons of the Sil and the Miño, and in Ribeiro the Cistercian monastery of San Clodio was tied to the vineyard. In Priorat, the district owes its very name to the priory of the Carthusian monastery of Scala Dei, founded in the late twelfth century at the foot of the Montsant range.

What they really contributed

Crediting monks with every advance in medieval viticulture would be an exaggeration, but their contributions were real:

  • Continuity: they kept vineyards going through times of crisis and depopulation.
  • Writing and archives: their cartularies and registers record gifts, rents and plots, and are now essential sources for historians.
  • Observation: long-term management of the same land favoured the selection of the best sites.
  • Trade: many abbeys sold wine and drew substantial income from it.
  • Hospitality: they offered wine to pilgrims and travellers and so spread its reputation.

Other players in medieval wine

The history of wine in the Middle Ages is not confined to the cloister. Kings and great nobles owned prestigious vineyards; towns regulated trade and protected their markets; peasants farmed most of the vines. A famous case is Bordeaux: after Aquitaine was joined to the English crown in the twelfth century, Bordeaux wine found a privileged market in England, driven by merchants rather than monks.

Religious charitable institutions also mattered. In Beaune, the chancellor Nicolas Rolin founded a hospital in 1443, the Hospices de Beaune, which over time received vineyards as gifts and whose wines are still auctioned today for charity.

A legacy that lives on

From the sixteenth century onwards, the Protestant Reformation in northern Europe and, later, the French Revolution and the confiscation of Church property in other countries put an end to much of the Church's vineyard holdings. Many vineyards passed to the bourgeoisie, the nobility or the state. Yet the monastic imprint is still visible in walls, cellars and plot names, and in the very idea that wine expresses the place it comes from.
