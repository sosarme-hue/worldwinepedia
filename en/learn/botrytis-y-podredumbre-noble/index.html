Oenology & science

Botrytis and noble rot

The same fungus, Botrytis cinerea, can ruin a harvest or give rise to some of the world's most celebrated sweet wines. Everything depends on the weather, the grape and the moment it strikes.

Few phenomena in viticulture are as paradoxical as botrytis. The fungus Botrytis cinerea is one of the grape grower's great enemies: it attacks leaves, flowers and bunches, and in damp conditions causes grey rot, which destroys the fruit and gives the wine mouldy aromas and a tendency to oxidise. Yet in very specific circumstances the same fungus acts beneficially and produces what is known as noble rot, the origin of sweet wines of extraordinary complexity such as those of Sauternes, Tokaj and the great sweet wines of Germany and Austria.

Two faces of the same fungus

The difference between grey rot and noble rot does not depend on different fungi being involved, but on the conditions under which the infection develops. Grey rot appears when humidity is persistent, especially if the grapes are not yet ripe or their skins are damaged. The fungus spreads rapidly, the berries become covered in a greyish mould, split open and let in other microorganisms such as acetic bacteria and unwanted yeasts.

Noble rot, by contrast, requires healthy, ripe grapes and a very particular alternation of weather. Damp mornings, with mists that tend to form near rivers or lakes, encourage the spores to germinate on the skin. Dry, sunny afternoons slow the fungus down and prevent the rot from becoming destructive. If this alternation lasts for several weeks in autumn, the infection progresses in a controlled way.

What happens to the grape

The fungus pierces the skin of the berry with its filaments without breaking it open completely. Through these tiny holes, water from the pulp evaporates during the dry afternoons, and the grape gradually dehydrates until it looks shrivelled and turns brown or purplish. The result is a strong concentration of sugars and acids.

But botrytis does more than concentrate. The fungus consumes some of the sugar and acids, especially tartaric acid, and produces other substances, such as glycerol, gluconic acid and various aromatic compounds. It also alters the grape's own varietal aromas and generates new ones. That is why a wine made from botrytised grapes is not simply a concentrated sweet wine: it has its own aromatic character, with notes often described as honey, dried apricot, candied fruit, crystallised citrus, saffron or wax.

A painstaking harvest

The infection never progresses evenly. Within a single bunch there may be healthy berries, berries in the early stages of attack and berries completely shrivelled by the fungus. The great regions for botrytised wines therefore harvest in successive passes, or tries: the pickers go through the vineyard several times during the autumn, gathering on each pass only the bunches, or even the individual berries, that have reached the desired state.

This work makes production extremely expensive, and yields are very low because the grapes have lost much of their water. There is also the climatic risk: if the rain continues, noble rot turns into grey rot and the crop may be lost; if the autumn is too dry, the fungus does not appear at all. In many vintages, producers are unable to make their best sweet wines.

Winemaking

Pressing botrytised grapes is slow and difficult, because the pulp is dense and viscous. The resulting juice is very rich in sugar, which makes life hard for the yeasts: fermentation is usually long and stops naturally when the alcohol and sugar concentration inhibit the yeasts, leaving a wine with high residual sugar. Botrytis also produces substances that bind sulphur dioxide, so these wines require particularly careful management.

Many botrytised wines are aged in barrel, and their richness in sugar and acidity allows them to develop over decades, taking on an amber colour and increasingly complex aromas.

The great regions

Some places regularly provide the necessary conditions, generally thanks to nearby water that produces autumn mists:

  • Sauternes and Barsac, in Bordeaux, where the meeting of the Ciron and Garonne rivers encourages mist; the wines are made mainly from Sémillon, Sauvignon Blanc and Muscadelle.
  • Tokaj, in Hungary, whose aszú wines, made from Furmint, Hárslevelű and other varieties, have been documented since at least the seventeenth century.
  • Germany, where the Beerenauslese and Trockenbeerenauslese categories correspond to wines from overripe, often botrytised grapes, above all Riesling, in regions such as the Rheingau and the Mosel.
  • Austria, especially around Lake Neusiedl in Burgenland, whose humidity creates very favourable conditions.
  • The Loire, with sweet Chenin Blanc wines from areas such as Coteaux du Layon, and Alsace, with the designation Sélection de Grains Nobles.

Outside Europe, botrytised wines are also made in places such as Australia, South Africa and the United States, sometimes using techniques that encourage the infection artificially.

An unpredictable gift

Noble rot is an example of how nature, climate and human effort can combine to turn a risk into an asset. Its unpredictability, the difficulty of the harvest and the low yields explain why great botrytised wines are scarce. Their acidity, which balances their sweetness, and their capacity to age place them among the most remarkable wines in existence.
