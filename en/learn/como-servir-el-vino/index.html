How to serve wine

A practical guide to serving wine at home: preparation, opening still and sparkling wines, the order of bottles, pouring technique and quantities.

Serving wine well does not call for elaborate ceremony, only attention to a few details that affect what you perceive in the glass. Temperature, when the bottle is opened, the order in which several wines are presented and how much is poured can make the same wine seem more expressive or more subdued. This guide goes through service step by step, as it can be done at home.

Before opening

Preparation starts ahead of time. If the bottle has been stored on its side and is an older wine, it is worth standing it upright a day or two beforehand so the sediment settles at the bottom. Temperature also needs checking: as a general guide, sparkling wines and light whites are served well chilled; fuller whites and rosés, slightly less cold; light reds, cool; and structured reds at a moderate temperature, below that of a heated room. It is better to serve a wine a little colder than ideal, since it warms in the glass, than too warm.

A refrigerator needs several hours to chill a bottle; an ice bucket filled with equal parts water and ice does it much faster, because water conducts cold better than air. Glasses should be clean, dry and free of cupboard smells or traces of detergent.

Opening a still wine

The corkscrew most used by professionals is the two-step lever type, sometimes called a waiter's friend. The usual procedure is:

  • Cut the capsule below the lip of the bottle neck so the wine does not touch the foil or plastic when poured.
  • Wipe the mouth of the bottle if there is any mould or dust, which does not usually indicate a fault in the wine.
  • Insert the worm into the centre of the cork, turning it without going right through.
  • Draw the cork out gently and vertically, without jerking, and smell it: a mouldy or damp-cardboard smell may signal a faulty wine.

Old corks can be fragile. In such cases a two-pronged cork puller, which slides between cork and glass, helps. If the cork breaks and falls in, the wine can be poured into a jug through a fine strainer. Screw caps are opened by holding the cap and turning the bottle.

Opening a sparkling wine

A sparkling wine holds gas at considerable pressure, so opening it calls for care. The bottle should be well chilled, as gas stays better dissolved at low temperatures. Remove the foil, loosen the wire cage while keeping a thumb on the cork, and tilt the bottle at about forty-five degrees, always pointing away from people. Then grip the cork firmly and turn the bottle, not the cork, letting the pressure ease it out gradually. The aim is for it to leave with a sigh, not a bang: less gas is lost and no wine is spilled.

The order of wines

When several wines are served at one meal, tradition suggests a progression that keeps one wine from overshadowing the next:

  • From lighter to fuller-bodied.
  • From dry to sweet.
  • From whites and rosés to reds, although a powerful white can follow a light red.
  • From young to more mature, and from simple to complex.

Sparkling wines fit well at the start, as an aperitif, and sweet or fortified wines at the end. These guidelines are indicative and should be adapted to the food: the order of the wines usually follows that of the meal naturally.

How to pour

Hold the bottle by its body, with the label visible to guests, and pour in a steady stream towards the centre of the glass. A slight twist of the bottle at the end prevents drips. With sparkling wines it is best to pour in two stages: a short first splash, a pause for the foam to subside, then top up.

Glasses are not filled: the usual practice is to pour up to a third of their capacity, or slightly more in small glasses. The empty space allows the wine to be swirled and its aromas to gather. At the table, the host usually pours a little first to check the wine is sound, and then serves the guests.

To decant or not

Some wines benefit from being transferred to a decanter: reds with sediment, to separate them from it, and some young, closed wines, to aerate them. Very old, fragile wines, on the other hand, can lose aromas with prolonged aeration, so they are decanted just before serving or not at all. When in doubt, pour a small amount, taste it and decide.

During the meal

During the meal it helps to keep whites and sparkling wines in an ice bucket or the refrigerator between pours, and to make sure reds do not warm up near a heat source. Water should always be on the table, and non-alcoholic alternatives offered. Serving well also means serving in moderation: not topping up glasses out of habit and respecting those who prefer not to drink, or to drink little.

After service

If wine is left over, it is best to reclose the bottle as soon as possible and keep it in the refrigerator, even if it is red, to slow oxidation. Fine glasses are best washed in hot water with little detergent and dried with a lint-free cloth.
