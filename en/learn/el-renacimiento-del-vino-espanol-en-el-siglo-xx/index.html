History & culture

The revival of Spanish wine in the twentieth century

From the phylloxera crisis and bulk wine to the modernisation of the century's final decades: how Spain went from exporting volume to being recognised for the quality and diversity of its wines.

Spain has one of the largest vineyard areas in the world and a winemaking tradition thousands of years old. Yet for much of the twentieth century its wine was associated on international markets mainly with volume and low prices, with some notable exceptions such as sherry or the great Riojas. In the final decades of the century, a series of political, economic and technical changes, combined with the initiative of many winemakers, transformed that image. This process is often described as a genuine renaissance.

A difficult start to the century

Phylloxera reached Spain later than France and spread through the wine regions between the late nineteenth century and the first decades of the twentieth. Replanting on American rootstocks was costly and slow, and many areas lost their vineyards for good. At the same time, France's recovery reduced the foreign demand that had benefited regions such as Rioja.

It was in this context that the first steps towards protecting origin were taken. Rioja and Jerez were pioneers in organising their designations, and in 1932 the Wine Statute, passed under the Second Republic, established a general legal framework for designations of origin in Spain.

War, post-war and cooperatives

The Civil War and the harsh post-war years halted any progress. In the middle decades of the century the priority was production. Many cooperatives were founded, bringing together small growers and making large quantities of wine, often sold in bulk. Regions such as La Mancha, with vast plantings of Airén, specialised in everyday wines and wine for distillation. Wineries generally lacked temperature control and modern equipment.

In 1970 a new law, the Statute of the Vine, Wine and Spirits, updated the regulation of the sector and created a national body for designations of origin. Even so, most Spanish wine remained geared towards volume.

The pioneers of modernisation

During those years, some producers opened new paths. In Penedès, the Torres family introduced techniques such as temperature-controlled fermentation in stainless steel tanks and experimented with French varieties alongside native ones; others, such as Jean León, planted Cabernet Sauvignon and Chardonnay in the area. In Rueda, the arrival in the 1970s of a Rioja winery, Marqués de Riscal, helped promote fresh white wines from Verdejo, in an area that until then had mostly made oxidative fortified wines.

In Ribera del Duero a legendary winery, Vega Sicilia, had existed since the nineteenth century, but the area was known mainly for its cooperative wines. From the 1970s, winemakers such as Alejandro Fernández, with Tinto Pesquera, showed the potential of the local Tempranillo, known there as Tinto Fino or Tinta del País. The Ribera del Duero designation of origin was created in 1982 and became one of the great successes of Spanish wine.

Democracy and Europe

The arrival of democracy and, above all, Spain's entry into the European Community in 1986 accelerated the transformation. European funding, open markets and competition forced wineries and vineyards to modernise. Stainless steel, temperature control and the renewal of barrel stocks became widespread, and a new generation of winemakers trained at Spanish and foreign schools.

Designations of origin also multiplied, coming to cover much of the country. Some emerged in areas with tradition but no recognition, such as Rías Baixas, created in 1988 and centred on Albariño, which gave Galicia a new international profile.

The case of Priorat

One of the most striking episodes was the revival of Priorat, a district in Tarragona with very poor slate soils known as llicorella, which had become almost depopulated and whose vineyards were in decline. In the late 1980s a small group of winemakers, including René Barbier and Álvaro Palacios, settled in the area and began producing highly concentrated wines from old Garnacha and Cariñena vines, sometimes complemented by French varieties. Their first wines quickly attracted international attention. In the early twenty-first century, Priorat gained qualified designation status, the second in Spain after Rioja, which had obtained it in 1991.

Reclaiming what is native

In the last years of the twentieth century and the first of the twenty-first, a trend that now defines Spanish wine gathered strength: the recovery of native varieties and old vineyards. Mencía from Bierzo and Ribeira Sacra, Garnacha from various inland areas, Monastrell from the south-east and Galician Godello came to the fore. Many old bush-trained vineyards, once uprooted for their low yields, came to be regarded as a valuable heritage.

The 2003 Law of the Vine and Wine introduced new quality categories, such as vinos de pago, linked to a specific estate, and in the following decades several designations have added indications for municipalities or individual sites.

Taking stock

Over the twentieth century, Spanish wine travelled a long road: from the aftermath of phylloxera and bulk production to a diverse range recognised internationally. Spain still produces large volumes, but it is now also known for the variety of its landscapes, grapes and styles, from Atlantic whites to mountain reds and traditional oak-aged wines. That renaissance was the work of institutions, cooperatives and wineries of every size, and it is still under way.
