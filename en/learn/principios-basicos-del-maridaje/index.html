Food pairing

The basic principles of food and wine pairing

Intensity, acidity, fat, salt, sweetness and tannins: the simple rules that explain why some wines and dishes get along and others clash.

Pairing means seeking a harmonious relationship between a wine and a dish, so that both are enjoyed more together than apart. There is no exact science of pairing, and personal taste has the last word. There are, however, well-established principles based on how the components of wine, such as acidity, sugar, alcohol and tannins, interact in the mouth with those of food, such as fat, salt, sweetness, spice heat and umami. Knowing them helps avoid the most obvious clashes and find successful combinations more often.

Matching intensity

The first principle is the simplest: wine and dish should have similar intensity. A steamed white fish will be overwhelmed by a very powerful red, and a hearty game stew will make a light white taste like water. Intensity depends not only on the main ingredient but also on how it is cooked and, above all, on the sauce. Grilled chicken with lemon and chicken in a rich almond and saffron sauce call for different wines. When choosing a wine, therefore, it is worth thinking about the dominant element of the dish, which is often the sauce or the side.

Matching and contrasting

There are two basic strategies. Matching looks for similarities: a buttery, oak-aged white with a creamy dish, a spicy red with peppered meat, an earthy wine with mushrooms. Contrast seeks to have an element of the wine offset one in the dish: the acidity of a fresh white cutting through the fat of fried food, or a sweet wine balancing the salt of a blue cheese. Both strategies work and are often combined.

Acidity, a great ally

Acidity is probably a wine's most useful trait at the table. It cleans the palate after fatty mouthfuls, refreshes and brightens flavours, much as a squeeze of lemon does. Wines with good acidity, whether white, sparkling, rosé or fresh reds, are generally easier to pair than low-acid wines. A practical rule is that the wine should have at least as much acidity as the dish: a vinegar dressing or a tomato-based dish will make a low-acid wine seem flat and heavy.

Fat and tannins

Red-wine tannins are perceived as astringency because they bind to saliva proteins. When taken with foods rich in protein and fat, such as red meat or mature cheese, this interaction softens and the wine seems friendlier. That is why tannic reds and roast meats are a classic pair. With light, lean dishes, on the other hand, a very tannic wine can feel harsh.

Fat also goes well with acidity, which balances it, and with sparkling wines, whose bubbles help to cleanse the mouth.

Salt, sweetness and bitterness

Salt is a good friend of wine: it softens the perception of tannins and bitterness and makes the wine seem fruitier. Salty foods such as cured ham, anchovies or blue cheese pair well with high-acid wines, with dry fortified wines such as fino and manzanilla, or, by contrast, with sweet wines.

Sweetness in food is trickier. A sweet dessert makes a dry wine taste sour, bitter and thin. The general rule for desserts is therefore that the wine should be at least as sweet as the dish. A touch of sweetness in savoury dishes, such as a fruit sauce or caramelised onion, calls for wines with ripe fruit or a little residual sugar.

Bitterness adds up: a tannic wine or one with bitter notes accentuates the bitterness of foods like endive, grilled aubergine or very dark chocolate.

Spice heat and umami

The heat of chillies is intensified by alcohol and tannins. With very spicy dishes, wines of moderate alcohol, low in tannin and with some fruit or slight sweetness work better, such as some off-dry Rieslings or fruity rosés. Umami, found in mushrooms, aged cheese, soy sauce or ripe tomato, tends to make wines seem more bitter and astringent; it is partly offset by salt and by wines with soft tannins.

Difficult foods

Some ingredients complicate pairing. Artichokes contain cynarin, which makes the wine drunk afterwards taste oddly sweet or metallic. Asparagus, eggs, strong vinegar and very bitter chocolate also pose difficulties. In these cases dry, fresh, unoaked whites or dry fortified wines, which stand up well to intense flavours, tend to work best.

What grows together goes together

A traditional and very useful principle is to combine wines and dishes from the same region. The cuisines and wines of a place have evolved together over centuries: Albariño with Galician seafood, manzanilla with the fried fish of Sanlúcar, Rioja with roast lamb or Chianti with Tuscan cooking. It is not an infallible rule, but it rarely fails.

How to use these rules

The principles of pairing are guidelines, not laws. Many excellent combinations are discovered by each person through trial. A good practice is to have more than one wine on the table, for example a fresh white and a soft red, and notice which one each dish is enjoyed with most. When in doubt, dry sparkling wines and wines with good acidity and little oak are the most versatile options. And, as in every aspect of wine, moderation is part of the pleasure.
