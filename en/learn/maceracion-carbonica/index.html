Oenology & science

Carbonic maceration

A red winemaking method in which whole bunches, held in an atmosphere of carbon dioxide, begin fermenting inside each berry. It produces very fruity wines, light in tannin and intended to be drunk young.

In classic red winemaking, the grapes are destemmed and crushed before fermentation, so that yeasts act on the released juice. Carbonic maceration follows a different path: whole, uncrushed bunches are placed in a sealed vessel filled with carbon dioxide. Deprived of oxygen, the intact grape sets in motion a metabolism of its own that transforms its composition from the inside. The result is wines with a bright colour, very intense fruity aromas and soft tannins, among the most recognisable of all for their aromatic profile.

The principle: fermentation inside the berry

Grape cells remain alive after harvest. In the presence of oxygen they respire, like any plant tissue. But when the berry is surrounded by carbon dioxide and no oxygen is available, its own enzymes activate an anaerobic metabolism known as intracellular fermentation. During this process some of the sugar is converted into alcohol without the involvement of yeasts, though only in modest amounts, generally one or two degrees at most. At the same time a significant part of the malic acid is broken down, which lowers acidity, and numerous aromatic compounds are formed.

In addition, pigments and other substances in the skin begin to migrate into the pulp, which gradually becomes coloured. This extraction takes place without free juice or pumping over, so it is much gentler than in classic maceration.

The grape cells cannot withstand these conditions indefinitely: once the internal alcohol reaches a certain level, or after a certain time, they die and the intracellular process stops. Its duration depends mainly on temperature: in warm conditions the carbonic phase lasts only a few days; in cool conditions it can go on for longer.

How it is done

The grapes must reach the winery as whole bunches with intact berries, which requires hand harvesting and careful transport. The bunches are placed in the tank, which has been filled beforehand with carbon dioxide or in which the gas from fermentation will itself displace the air.

Inevitably, the weight of the bunches crushes those at the bottom, and the juice released begins to ferment conventionally with yeasts. This fermentation produces carbon dioxide, which saturates the tank and keeps the atmosphere free of oxygen. In practice, then, two processes coexist in the same tank: a classic alcoholic fermentation at the bottom and an intracellular fermentation in the whole berries above.

After the carbonic phase, which usually lasts from a few days to a couple of weeks, the liquid is run off and the bunches are pressed. The berries, now soft, release juice that still contains much of its sugar. This juice completes its alcoholic fermentation with yeasts, away from the skins, and normally goes through malolactic fermentation afterwards.

Full and semi-carbonic maceration

Strictly speaking, full carbonic maceration requires the tank to be saturated with carbon dioxide before the grapes are added, and the process to be precisely controlled. More common, however, is what is known as semi-carbonic maceration, in which the gas comes from the fermentation of the juice released by the crushed bunches. Many producers also blend, in the same wine, portions made by carbonic maceration and portions made by the classic method, to balance fruit with a little more structure.

The style of the wines

Carbonic maceration wines have a very characteristic aromatic profile. They are often described as having notes of fresh red fruit, cherry, raspberry, strawberry, banana or candy, and sometimes of cinnamon or kirsch. They are intensely and brightly coloured, usually with purple tints when young, moderately acidic and soft in tannin, because extraction has been limited.

Precisely because of their low tannin and the fragility of their primary aromas, most of these wines are meant to be drunk young, in the months or few years after the harvest. Over time their most striking aromas fade. There are, however, carbonic or semi-carbonic wines capable of developing well, especially when they come from high-quality grapes and are combined with other techniques.

Regions and traditions

The best-known example is Beaujolais, in France, where the Gamay grape is traditionally vinified by semi-carbonic maceration. The region's primeur wines, released a few weeks after the harvest, spread this style internationally. In the Beaujolais crus the technique is also used for more ambitious wines. In Italy, the rules for vino novello require part of each wine to be made by carbonic maceration.

In Spain, whole-bunch winemaking has a long tradition in Rioja, especially in Rioja Alavesa, where growers made young Tempranillo wines by placing whole grapes in open stone troughs or tanks. In southern France the technique has been used to soften rustic varieties such as Carignan. In recent decades, moreover, many producers around the world have turned to carbonic maceration or whole-bunch fermentation to make light, fresh reds, often in the field of minimal-intervention wines.

Advantages and limits

Carbonic maceration makes it possible to produce pleasant, expressive wines quickly, with softened acidity and without the need for intensive extraction. It is useful for varieties with rustic tannins and for wines intended for early drinking. Its limitations are a reduced capacity for ageing and the risk that its very marked aromatic profile will overshadow the character of the variety and the terroir. That is why many winemakers use it as one tool among others, combining it with different techniques to achieve the balance they want.
