Food pairing

Wine with vegetables and vegetarian cooking

How to pair wine with vegetables, pulses, mushrooms and vegetarian dishes, which vegetables are especially difficult, how the cooking method matters, and what it means for a wine to be suitable for vegans.

For a long time, pairing manuals were organised around meat and fish, and vegetables appeared only as side dishes. Growing interest in vegetarian cooking and in dishes in which vegetables, pulses or mushrooms take centre stage has forced a fresh look at wine. Far from being a minor field, plant-based cooking offers a wide variety of combinations, although it also includes some notoriously difficult ingredients.

Think about the dish, not the absence of meat

The first useful idea is to abandon the question “which wine goes with a meatless dish?” and replace it with the same questions one would ask of any other: is the dish light or hearty? Does acidity, sweetness, bitterness or fat dominate? How is it cooked? Which sauces and spices accompany it? A lentil stew with paprika, a salad with vinaigrette and an aubergine lasagne with cheese are all vegetarian dishes, but they call for completely different wines.

The cooking method changes everything

Raw or barely cooked vegetables have fresh, green and often sharp flavours that suit light whites with lively acidity. Steamed or boiled vegetables keep their delicacy and call for equally subtle wines. When vegetables are roasted, grilled or caramelised, however, they develop sweetness and toasty notes that allow fuller-bodied wines, including light or moderately aged reds. Vegetables cooked over embers with olive oil and rosemary, for example, are perfectly happy with a young garnacha or mencía.

The difficult vegetables

Some vegetables have a reputation as enemies of wine, and with good reason:

  • Artichoke: it contains cynarin, a compound that in many people makes whatever is drunk next taste sweeter. Very dry, fresh whites, or a fino or manzanilla, work best with it.
  • Asparagus: green asparagus in particular has vegetal, sulphurous notes that make many reds taste metallic. Herbaceous whites such as Sauvignon Blanc or Verdejo, and Austrian Grüner Veltliner, are regular partners. In Germany and Alsace, the white asparagus season is traditionally celebrated with dry local whites such as Silvaner or Riesling.
  • Spinach and other leafy greens: their bitter, slightly metallic taste is accentuated by tannins; whites or rosés are better.
  • Brassicas: Brussels sprouts, broccoli and cauliflower have sulphurous and bitter notes that call for fresh, unoaked wines.

Peppers, tomatoes and aubergines

Green peppers share with certain grape varieties aromatic compounds called pyrazines, which are responsible for their green notes. This is why they go naturally with Sauvignon Blanc or with Loire Cabernet Franc reds, which carry those same notes. Roasted red peppers, which are sweeter, suit rosés and fruity reds. Tomato, being acidic, calls for wines with good acidity, as in Italian cooking. Aubergine, meaty and absorbent, gains a great deal when roasted or baked au gratin, and then accepts medium-bodied reds.

Mushrooms and pulses

Mushrooms are perhaps the great allies of red wine in vegetarian cooking. Their earthy notes and wealth of umami find an echo in wines with forest-floor aromas, such as Burgundian Pinot Noir or Mencía from Bierzo. A mushroom risotto or stew can even take reds with some oak ageing. Pulses, rich and often cooked with spices, also accept reds: braised lentils, a chickpea stew or a vegetable chilli go well with young garnacha, mencía or tempranillo. Pulse dishes with curry or coconut milk, on the other hand, prefer aromatic whites or rosés.

Plant proteins, cheese and eggs

Tofu has a very neutral flavour and, like rice, takes on the character of its preparation: with soy and ginger an aromatic white works better; smoked or grilled, a light red. In vegetarian cooking that includes dairy and eggs, cheeses and creamy sauces add fat that is best balanced with acidity, and egg dishes, such as omelettes or baked eggs, usually prefer dry whites or sparkling wines.

Vegan-friendly wines

Vegans should know that, although wine comes from grapes, animal products may be used during winemaking for fining, the process that removes suspended particles. Gelatine, egg white, milk casein and isinglass, made from fish bladders, have traditionally been used. These agents are removed afterwards, but many vegans prefer to avoid them. Today there are mineral or plant-based alternatives, such as bentonite or pea proteins, and some producers do not fine their wines at all. Some wineries state on the label that their wine is suitable for vegans, although this statement is not compulsory in every country; when in doubt, the most reliable course is to ask the producer.

In summary

Fresh, herbaceous whites for raw and green vegetables, rosés for sweet and roasted vegetables, and light, earthy reds for mushrooms and pulses make a good starting guide. Plant-based cooking, with its enormous variety, rewards curiosity and experimentation.
