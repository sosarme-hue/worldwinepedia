Wine with meat and game

Poultry, pork, lamb, beef and game: how fat, cooking and sauce influence the choice of wine, and why not everything demands a powerful red.

Meat is the natural territory of red wine, and the reason is simple: proteins and fat soften the astringency of tannins, so that a structured red that seems hard on its own becomes friendly alongside a roast. But the word meat covers very different dishes, from a grilled chicken breast to a wild boar stew. As in all pairing, the intensity of the dish, the cooking technique and the sauce determine the choice as much as the type of meat.

Poultry

Chicken and turkey have white flesh and a mild flavour, and suit a wide range of wines. Grilled or roasted with herbs, they pair with whites with some body, rosés or light reds. In creamy sauces, with mushrooms or cream, they call for whites with texture, such as an oak-aged Chardonnay. Braised in red wine, as in Burgundian coq au vin, they call for a red, ideally from the same region or style as the wine in the sauce.

Duck is fattier and more intense. Duck breast or confit finds good partners in reds with lively acidity and medium tannins, such as Pinot Noir, or in more structured reds from south-west France. With fruit sauces, a red with ripe fruit works well.

Pork

Pork is very versatile. Grilled loin or tenderloin can take full-bodied whites, rosés and light or medium-bodied reds. Roast suckling pig, an emblematic dish of Segovia and other parts of Castile, with crackling skin and tender flesh, is traditionally served with Tempranillo reds from Ribera del Duero or other Castilian areas, although structured whites and sparkling wines also suit its richness. Pork dishes with sweet sauces or fruit, such as apple, call for fruity wines with little tannin.

Cured sausages and cured ham, salty and fatty, work well with young, fresh reds, with rosés and, very classically, with fino and manzanilla, whose dryness and salinity enhance Iberian ham.

Lamb

Roast milk-fed lamb, typical of Castilla y León and other Spanish regions, has tender flesh and a delicate flavour. It is served with aged Tempranillo reds, such as those from Rioja or Ribera del Duero, in which oak and fruit are well integrated. Older lamb or grilled lamb, with a more intense flavour, can take more powerful reds made from Garnacha, Syrah, Cabernet Sauvignon or Monastrell. In Bordeaux, Pauillac lamb and the Cabernet-based reds of the area are a traditional partnership.

Beef

Beef is the classic partner for structured reds. The marbled fat of a good chargrilled rib steak softens the tannins of a Cabernet Sauvignon, a Malbec, a Tempranillo from Toro or a Syrah. In Argentina, asado and Malbec form an emblematic pair.

  • Rare or raw meat, such as carpaccio or tartare, goes well with less tannic reds or full-bodied rosés.
  • Roasts and grills: medium- to full-bodied reds with firm tannins.
  • Braises and stews, such as oxtail or beef cheeks: aged reds with ripe fruit, able to match long, concentrated sauces.
  • Meat with pepper sauce: spicy reds such as Syrah or Garnacha.

Game

Game has more intense flavours than farmed meat and is often leaner. A distinction is made between small and large game.

Feathered game, such as partridge, quail or woodcock, has fine but flavoursome flesh. Braised or pickled partridge, very traditional in Toledo and other parts of Spain, pairs with medium-bodied aged reds and, in the case of the pickled escabeche version, because of its vinegar, with fresh reds or rosés. The more delicate game birds find a good partner in Pinot Noir or in mature reds with forest-floor notes.

Small furred game, such as wild rabbit or hare, is often stewed. Hare, with its very pronounced flavour, calls for intense reds.

Large game, such as red deer, roe deer or wild boar, lends itself to long stews, sauces made with red wine, chocolate or red berries, or to grilling. Structured reds with some development, showing earthy, leathery or spicy notes, are usually the best choice: great Tempranillo reds, old-vine Garnacha, Rhône Syrah, Nebbiolo or mature red Bordeaux. Acidity is important to balance rich sauces.

Cooking and sauces

A few general guidelines help refine the choice:

  • Chargrilling and smoking bring toasty notes that suit barrel-aged reds.
  • Long braises call for wines with some development, ripe fruit and polished tannins.
  • Tomato sauces demand wines with good acidity, such as Sangiovese or fresh Garnacha.
  • Sweet or fruit sauces favour generously fruity, low-tannin reds.
  • Spicy dishes do not get on with very alcoholic, tannic reds.

Beyond red

Although red dominates, it is not the only option. Many dishes of white meat, charcuterie or cold meats suit full-bodied whites, rosés or sparkling wines. Dry amontillado and oloroso, with their structure and nutty notes, are surprising partners for meat stews, consommés or meat with mushrooms. Experimenting outside the rules is a good way to discover new combinations, always in moderation.
