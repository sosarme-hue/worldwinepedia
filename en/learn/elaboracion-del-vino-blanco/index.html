Oenology & science

How white wine is made

White wine is made by fermenting juice separated from the skins; its quality depends on preserving fruit, preventing oxidation and controlling temperature precisely.

Unlike red wine, white wine is made by fermenting only the grape juice, which is separated from the skins before fermentation begins. Most whites come from white grapes, but white wine can also be made from red grapes with colourless pulp if they are pressed gently and quickly, as happens with Pinot Noir and Pinot Meunier in the production of blanc de noirs Champagne. The central aim is to preserve the freshness, acidity and aromas of the fruit, and to do so the winemaker must fight two enemies at every stage: oxidation and heat.

The harvest

The picking date is decisive. For fresh, aromatic whites, the goal is a balance between sugar and still-lively acidity; a later harvest produces broader, more alcoholic wines with less tension. In warm climates it is common to pick at night or in the early morning, when the grapes are cool, and some wineries chill the bunches before processing them. The more intact the fruit arrives, the lower the risk that the juice will oxidise or begin fermenting in an uncontrolled way.

Pressing and skin contact

At the winery the grapes may be destemmed and crushed before pressing, or pressed as whole bunches. The second option, which is slower, yields cleaner juice with fewer phenolic compounds and is widely used for very fine wines and for sparkling wine bases. Modern pneumatic presses, which squeeze the grapes with an inflatable membrane, allow low, controlled pressures and, in some models, work under an inert atmosphere.

The juice that runs off with little or no pressure, known as free-run juice, is the most delicate. As pressure increases, the fractions obtained contain more tannin, more potassium and less acidity; they are usually kept apart and used for other wines.

Some winemakers practise a brief skin maceration, leaving the juice in contact with the skins for a few hours at low temperature to extract aroma precursors located in the skin. It is a useful technique for aromatic varieties, but it must be handled carefully, because excessive contact brings bitterness and encourages oxidation. When that contact lasts for days or weeks and the wine ferments on its skins, it is no longer a conventional white but an orange wine.

Protection against oxidation

White grape juice is highly sensitive to oxygen. Enzymes present in the grape, particularly polyphenol oxidases, cause the juice to brown and lose its fresh aromas. To limit this, the winemaker usually adds sulphur dioxide in moderate doses, works at low temperatures and sometimes protects the juice with inert gases such as nitrogen or carbon dioxide. There is also an opposite approach, hyperoxygenation, which deliberately exposes the juice to oxygen so that the most easily oxidised compounds precipitate before fermentation. The aim is a finished wine that resists browning better, although the technique does not suit every variety.

Settling

Freshly pressed juice contains particles of skin, pulp and soil, collectively called gross lees or solids. Before fermentation it is clarified by settling, known in French as débourbage: it is left to stand in the cold for some hours so that the solids sink to the bottom, or it is treated by flotation or centrifugation. Juice that is too cloudy can produce heavy aromas and unwanted sulphur compounds; juice that is too clean may ferment with difficulty, because yeasts need certain nutrients found in the solids. The degree of clarification is therefore a stylistic choice.

Cool fermentation

White wines normally ferment at lower temperatures than reds, often somewhere between about 12 and 18 degrees Celsius. Cold slows yeast activity and helps to retain fruity and floral aromas, which would otherwise be carried away by the carbon dioxide released at higher temperatures. This is why temperature-controlled tanks, which became widespread in the second half of the twentieth century, transformed the quality of white wines in warm regions.

The vessel matters too. Stainless steel preserves the purity of the fruit and allows precise temperature control. Barrel fermentation, traditional in Burgundy for Chardonnay, adds texture, complexity and oak notes, and is often combined with lees ageing. Concrete, clay jars and other earthenware vessels offer intermediate solutions that are attracting growing interest.

Malolactic fermentation and lees work

For white wines, malolactic fermentation is an option rather than a rule. If it takes place, the wine loses its sharp acidity and gains roundness, sometimes with lactic or buttery notes; if it is prevented through cold, sulphur dioxide or filtration, the wine keeps its freshness and a more incisive character. Many whites from aromatic varieties or cool climates are made without it, while in other styles it is carried out fully or partially.

After fermentation the wine may be left in contact with its fine lees, made up largely of dead yeast cells. As they break down they release mannoproteins and other substances that add volume, a creamy texture and some protection against oxidation. Periodic stirring of the lees, known by its French name bâtonnage, intensifies this effect.

Stabilisation and bottling

Before bottling, white wines need careful stabilisation. Tartrate stabilisation, often by chilling, prevents the formation in the bottle of potassium bitartrate crystals, which some consumers mistake for fragments of glass. Protein stabilisation, usually with bentonite, prevents a haze from appearing if the wine is exposed to heat. The wine is then filtered and bottled, often with minimal exposure to oxygen to preserve its freshness.

A diversity of styles

With these tools very different wines can be made: young, light and aromatic whites intended for drinking in their first or second year; age-worthy whites fermented and matured in barrel, capable of developing for a long time; wines with residual sugar, whose fermentation is stopped before all the sugar is consumed; and whites aged oxidatively or biologically, such as the finos and amontillados of Andalusia. Variety, climate and soil set the starting point, but the cellar decisions regarding pressing, settling, temperature, malolactic fermentation and lees largely determine the final profile.
