Minerality in wine: an open debate

What tasters mean when they speak of minerality, why the idea that wine tastes of the rocks in the soil is disputed by science, and what alternative explanations are being considered.

Few words have spread as widely in tasting notes in recent decades as minerality. It is applied to wines from very different places and is usually offered as praise, associated with freshness, precision and the expression of terroir. Yet it is also one of the most debated terms in the vocabulary of wine: there is no accepted definition, tasters do not always agree on what they perceive, and the most intuitive explanation —that the wine tastes of the rocks in the soil— finds no support in current scientific knowledge. This article summarises the state of the debate with the caution it requires.

What is described as mineral

The label of minerality covers sensations of diverse kinds:

  • Aromas evoking wet stone, flint, struck flint, chalk, graphite, smoke or a struck match.
  • Taste sensations, above all a salty or savoury impression.
  • Impressions of texture and structure: taut acidity, a “chalky” feel on the palate, a certain austerity or linearity.

The term is also often used by exclusion: a wine is called mineral when its aromas are not clearly fruity, floral or oaky. This variety of uses is itself one of the causes of confusion.

The literal hypothesis and its problems

The idea that vines absorb minerals from the rocks and pass them on to the wine, where they are perceived as a taste of stone, is appealing and widespread, but it runs into several objections:

  • Roots do not absorb fragments of rock but nutrients dissolved in soil water, in the form of ions such as potassium, calcium or magnesium. Many of the minerals that make up rocks are only very slightly soluble.
  • The plant actively regulates what it absorbs and what it accumulates in the berry, so the composition of the wine does not directly reflect that of the soil.
  • The concentrations of these elements in wine are low and generally do not correspond to the flavours described. The most abundant is usually potassium, which tastes neither of flint nor of chalk.
  • Dry rocks have hardly any smell. The aroma of wet stone or of soil after rain is due mainly to organic compounds produced by micro-organisms and plants, not to the minerals themselves.

Geologists who have studied wine, such as Alex Maltman, have stressed that there is no known mechanism by which the geology of the vineyard could be “tasted” in the glass. This criticism does not deny the influence of soil, but rather one particular way of understanding it.

How soil really has an influence

Soil does affect wine, albeit indirectly. Its capacity to retain or drain water, its temperature, its depth and the availability of nutrients —nitrogen in particular— shape the vine's vigour, the pace of ripening and the composition of the grape, including aroma precursors. Wines from different soils can therefore be different, and those differences may, by association, remind the taster of what they know about the ground. But that is not the same as saying the wine contains the taste of the rock.

Alternative explanations

Sensory and chemical research has proposed several possible causes of what is perceived as mineral, which probably act in combination:

  • Sulphur compounds: certain volatile molecules formed during fermentation or in low-oxygen conditions produce notes of flint, struck match or struck stone, especially in some whites. Here winemaking, not soil, would be the main source.
  • High acidity and low pH: many tasters associate minerality with taut, fresh wines from cool climates.
  • Restrained fruit: in wines with little fruit expression and no oak, less obvious sensations come to the fore and tend to be described as mineral.
  • Salinity: some components of wine, such as certain organic acids and salts, can give a salty impression; in some vineyards near the sea or with saline soils or water, sodium or chloride levels may be somewhat higher.

Sensory studies carried out with expert tasters, for example on French whites and on New Zealand Sauvignon Blanc, have found that the concept relates to some of these characteristics, but also that experts do not always agree with one another on which wines are mineral. In addition, prior knowledge of origin can influence judgement: knowing that a wine comes from slate or limestone soils predisposes the taster to find “stony” notes in it.

A useful word, used with care

Despite its ambiguity, the term is not without value. Many tasters use it to point to a set of real features —freshness, restraint, non-fruit aromas, a certain texture— that they recognise in particular wines, and which often appear in specific areas such as Chablis, the Mosel, Santorini or Sancerre. The problem arises when it is presented as a literal description of the soil or used as a generic synonym for quality.

For the taster, the most prudent advice is to be specific: saying whether one perceives an aroma of flint, a salty sensation, marked acidity or a chalky texture communicates much better what is in the glass. Research on the subject continues, and future studies may shed more light on the origin of these sensations. For now, minerality should be understood as a shared sensory metaphor, not as proof that wine transmits the mineral composition of its vineyard.
