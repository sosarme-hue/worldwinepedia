Serving temperature, glassware and decanting

What temperature to serve each type of wine at, which glass to choose and when and how to decant: the practical details that change what we perceive.

The same wine can seem dull or splendid depending on how it is served. Temperature, the shape of the glass and, in some cases, a spell in a decanter all influence the release of aromas and the perception of acidity, tannins and alcohol. These are not rituals but small details with a fairly simple physical and sensory basis.

Why temperature matters

Wine aromas are volatile compounds, and their evaporation increases with temperature. A very cold wine releases few aromas and seems closed; cold also accentuates the perception of acidity and, above all, the bitterness and astringency of tannins. A wine that is too warm, by contrast, emphasises alcohol, which masks the fruit and produces a heavy sensation. The ideal temperature seeks a balance between aromatic expression and freshness on the palate.

The well-known advice to serve reds at room temperature dates from a time when homes were considerably cooler than today. In a heated room or in summer, a red served at that temperature is usually too warm.

Guideline temperatures

The following ranges are a common reference among sommeliers; each wine and each person may call for adjustments.

  • Sparkling wines: 6 to 8 °C for young, light ones; a little higher, up to about 10 °C, for long-aged or more complex ones.
  • Light, aromatic whites, such as many Albariño, Verdejo or Sauvignon Blanc wines: 8 to 10 °C.
  • Full-bodied or barrel-aged whites: 10 to 13 °C, so that their aromas can show.
  • Rosés: 8 to 12 °C depending on their structure.
  • Light, young reds: 12 to 14 °C; some even benefit from a light chill.
  • Aged and full-bodied reds: 15 to 18 °C.
  • Fino and manzanilla: well chilled, around 7 to 9 °C. Amontillado and oloroso are served a little warmer, around 12 to 14 °C.
  • Sweet wines: the fresher, lighter ones chilled; the denser, oxidative ones less cold.

It is best to serve wine slightly below the desired temperature, because it warms in the glass. To chill quickly, an ice bucket with water and ice is far more effective than the freezer. To warm a red that is too cold, simply wait a few minutes or warm the glass slightly in your hands.

The glass

A good wine glass shares a few essential features: clear, colourless crystal or glass, to appreciate the colour; a stem, to hold it without warming the wine; a bowl large enough to swirl the wine; and a mouth somewhat narrower than the widest part of the bowl, to concentrate aromas. A thin rim is more pleasant to drink from.

There are specialised glasses for almost every style. Glasses with a very wide bowl, often called Burgundy glasses, are used for aromatic, delicate reds such as Pinot Noir, and tall, slightly narrower Bordeaux glasses for more structured reds. Whites are usually served in somewhat smaller glasses, which help keep them cool. For fortified wines, Jerez traditionally uses the catavino, a small tulip-shaped glass. A standard tasting glass, small and egg-shaped, suits any wine and is used in many competitions and schools.

For sparkling wines, the narrow flute preserves the bubbles and lets you watch them, but many professionals now prefer tulip-shaped glasses or even white-wine glasses, which are wider and let the aromas of complex sparkling wines show better. The broad, shallow coupe quickly dissipates both gas and aromas.

Cleanliness matters too: traces of detergent or a cupboard smell can alter the aroma and affect the mousse of sparkling wines. It helps to rinse glasses well with hot water and, if they have been stored for a long time, to rinse them again before use.

Decanting

Decanting means pouring the wine from the bottle into another vessel, the decanter. It is done for two different reasons.

The first is to separate the sediment. Old reds, some unfiltered wines and vintage ports form deposits that cloud the wine and add bitterness. To decant them, stand the bottle upright from the day before so the sediment settles to the bottom. Then open it carefully and pour slowly and steadily over a light source, traditionally a candle, so you can see the neck of the bottle and stop when the sediment appears.

The second reason is to aerate the wine. Young, powerful reds, some structured whites and wines with slight reduction can improve after contact with air: the aromas open up and the tannins feel somewhat softer. In this case a wide decanter is used, which increases the exposed surface, and the wine is poured with some vigour. The aeration time depends on the wine and is a matter of trial: the sensible approach is to taste at intervals.

When not to decant

Very old, fragile wines can quickly lose aromas they have developed over years. With them, if there is sediment, it is better to decant just before serving and drink without delay, or to pour very carefully straight from the bottle. Young, fruity wines, light whites and most sparkling wines do not need decanting. When in doubt, opening the bottle and tasting a little is always the best guide.

Other details of service

Pour a moderate amount, without filling the glass beyond its widest point, to leave room for the aromas. When several wines are served, the usual order is from lighter to more structured, from dry to sweet and from white to red, although there are exceptions. And it is always good to have water available for guests: wine is enjoyed more when it is drunk calmly and in moderation.
