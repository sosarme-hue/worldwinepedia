Food pairing

Wine with fish and seafood

From raw shellfish to oily fish and salt cod: which wines suit each preparation and why the rule of white wine with fish has exceptions.

White wine with fish is probably the best-known pairing rule. It has a real basis, but also many exceptions. Fish and seafood vary widely in texture, fat and intensity, and they are cooked in very different ways: raw, grilled, fried, baked, stewed or in sauce. The choice of wine depends on all of this as much as on the species.

Why white usually works

White fish and most shellfish have delicate flavours and little fat. Dry white wines respect that delicacy, and their acidity acts like the lemon traditionally served with fish: it lifts the flavour and refreshes. In addition, red wines with marked tannins can produce, with some fish, a metallic sensation or an unpleasant aftertaste on the finish. Laboratory studies have linked this effect to certain components of wine, such as iron, rather than to tannins as such, but in practice it is noticed mainly with young, tannic reds. When in doubt, therefore, a white is the safest choice.

Raw shellfish and oysters

Oysters and other raw shellfish have an iodine-rich, saline flavour. They call for very dry, fresh, unoaked whites or brut sparkling wines. Muscadet from the Loire, Chablis and Champagne are classics. In Spain, an Albariño from Rías Baixas, a white Txakoli or a brut nature cava play the same role. Manzanilla, with its saline character, is another excellent companion.

Cooked and grilled shellfish

Prawns, langoustines, velvet crabs or goose barnacles, boiled or grilled, keep a sweet, marine flavour. In Galicia, seafood and the region's whites, Albariño, Godello, Treixadura or the wines of Ribeiro, form one of the most natural partnerships in Spanish gastronomy. Lobster, with its richer flesh, can take whites with a little more body, even with discreet oak, especially when served with butter or sauces.

White fish

Finely flavoured white fish such as hake, sole, sea bass or turbot suit light or medium-bodied whites. The preparation makes the difference:

  • Steamed or poached: delicate, fresh, not very aromatic whites.
  • Grilled: whites with a little more structure, such as a Godello or a lees-aged Verdejo.
  • Baked with butter or in a cream sauce: full-bodied whites with a creamy texture, such as an oak-aged Chardonnay or a barrel-aged white Rioja.
  • In green sauce, with garlic and parsley: herbaceous, fresh whites such as Sauvignon Blanc or Verdejo.

Fried fish

Fried fish, typical of Andalusia, needs a wine that cleans the fat from the palate. The traditional partner on the Cádiz coast is manzanilla or fino: their dryness, freshness and saline notes suit fried fish, anchovies or tiny fried squid. Dry sparkling wines, with their bubbles and acidity, are another excellent choice for any fried dish.

Oily fish

Oily fish such as tuna, bonito, mackerel, sardines or salmon are richer and more intense. Here the white-wine rule becomes more flexible:

  • Chargrilled sardines or mackerel go well with high-acid whites and dry rosés.
  • Salmon, grilled or baked, is one of the few fish often mentioned alongside a light red with soft tannins, such as Pinot Noir, as well as full-bodied whites and rosés.
  • Bluefin tuna, with its almost meaty flesh, can take structured rosés and even light, fresh reds, such as some delicately styled Mencía or Garnacha, especially when seared or served with intense sauces.

Salt cod

Desalted salt cod is a special case in Iberian cooking. Served al pil pil, with its emulsion of oil and gelatine, it calls for full-bodied whites with good acidity or rosés. Cooked a la vizcaína, with a sauce of dried choricero peppers, it can take rosés and light young reds. In Portugal, where salt cod is almost a national emblem, it is often served with both whites and light reds.

Octopus and squid

Galician-style octopus, with paprika and olive oil, is a traditional partner for the wines of Galicia: whites, but also young, light, fresh Mencía reds, which are often served in octopus taverns. Fried squid follows the rules for fried food. Squid in its own ink, intensely flavoured, can take structured whites and some soft reds.

Raw and marinated dishes

Ceviche, marinated in lime, and other very acidic preparations demand high-acid wines so as not to taste flat: dry Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc or Albariño. Sushi and sashimi go well with delicate whites and dry sparkling wines; soy sauce, salty and rich in umami, suits wines without tannins. Smoked fish, such as salmon or eel, pairs with sparkling wines, aromatic whites or fino.

Seafood rice and stews

A seafood paella, black rice, a Catalan suquet or a Galician caldeirada have more intensity than a piece of grilled fish. Full-bodied whites, rosés and, in stews with tomato and paprika, some light reds work well. Once again, the sauce and the sofrito matter more than the fish.

In summary

When choosing wine for fish and seafood, look at three things: the fat and intensity of the species, the cooking technique and the sauce. Fresh whites and sparkling wines are the most versatile base; dry fortified wines are an option as traditional as it is successful; and rosés and light reds are a valid alternative when the dish has enough strength to welcome them.
