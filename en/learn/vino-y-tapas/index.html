Food pairing

Wine and tapas

How to choose wine for a tapas meal, where salty, fried, cured, sharp and spicy dishes share the table, and which Spanish wines get on best with the bar-counter classics.

Tapas are one of the most distinctive ways of eating and drinking in Spain: small portions shared standing at the bar or around a table, often while moving from one bar to another over the course of an evening. The origin of the word is surrounded by popular anecdotes that are hard to verify, but what is certain is that tapas and wine have grown up together. From a food-and-wine point of view, a tapas meal poses a particular challenge: instead of a single dish, the wine has to accompany a succession of very different flavours, from the saltiness of olives to the heat of patatas bravas or the richness of a croqueta.

The principle of versatility

When the table fills up with different dishes, the best strategy is not to look for the perfect match for each one, but to choose versatile wines. The features that help most are lively acidity, which cleans the palate after fried and fatty food; moderate alcohol, which does not become heavy as the evening goes on; and discreet tannins, which do not clash with salt, vinegar or chilli. This is why fresh whites, rosés, dry sparkling wines and dry fortified wines are natural allies of tapas, while very concentrated, heavily oaked reds tend to work less well, except with some meat-based tapas.

Fino and manzanilla, the great companions of the bar

In Andalusia, and increasingly in the rest of the country, fino and manzanilla are the classic reference. Made from the palomino grape and aged under a veil of flor in the Sherry region, they are bone-dry, saline wines with pungent aromas reminiscent of almonds and yeast. Their dryness and salinity suit flavours that defeat many other wines: marinated olives, fried almonds, anchovies, boquerones in vinegar, Iberian ham or fried fish. Served well chilled, they are also refreshing. An amontillado, fuller-bodied and with nutty notes, goes very well with more savoury tapas such as sautéed wild mushrooms, artichokes or a consommé.

Fried food and croquetas

Fried tapas, from Andalusian-style squid to ham croquetas, call for wines that cut through fat. Brut or brut nature cava is an excellent choice here: its acidity and bubbles sweep away the oil and prepare the palate for the next mouthful. Atlantic whites such as Albariño from Rías Baixas also work well, as do young Verdejos from Rueda, whose freshness and herbal notes balance the richness of the batter.

Ham, charcuterie and cheese

Iberian ham, with its melting fat and touch of salt, is perhaps the most emblematic tapa of all. Although it is commonly served with red wine, many sommeliers prefer a fino, a manzanilla or a sparkling wine, which respect its aromatic delicacy. With more heavily spiced cured sausages such as chorizo or salchichón, young, fruity reds from tempranillo or garnacha are a logical option: their fruit matches the paprika and their soft tannins do not harden against the salt. With mature sheep's cheeses, a red with some oak ageing or a dry oloroso gives good results.

Spicy tapas and tapas with vinegar

Patatas bravas, Padrón peppers and tapas dressed with vinegar are tricky territory. Chilli heat is accentuated by alcohol and by tannins, so powerful reds are best avoided. A fresh rosé, a young white with some fruit or a light, sharply acidic txakoli from Getaria hold up much better. In the Basque Country, txakoli traditionally accompanies the gilda, the skewer of pickled chilli, anchovy and olive, and much of pintxo cuisine.

Seafood tapas and spoon dishes

Galician-style octopus, garlic prawns, mussels or grilled razor clams find their match in the whites of their own surroundings: Albariño, Godello or the whites of Ribeiro. With spoon dishes such as tripe, meatballs or braised pork cheeks, the wine can gain body, and a moderately oaked Rioja tempranillo or a ripe garnacha suits the sauce and gelatinous texture of these stews.

How to organise the wine at a tapas meal

In practice, a good way to plan the evening is to move from the lightest to the most intense: begin with a sparkling wine or a fino alongside the olives and fried dishes, continue with whites and rosés for the fish and vegetable tapas, and keep a red for the end, with the meat and the stews. If only one bottle is to be opened, a dry rosé or a fino are probably the safest options. The tapas tradition also encourages drinking slowly and always with food, a custom that makes it possible to enjoy several wines in moderation.
