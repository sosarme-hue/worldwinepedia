Yields and quality: less fruit, better wine?

What a vineyard's yield means, how it is measured and controlled, and why the relationship between quantity of fruit and quality of wine is more complex than it seems.

Few ideas are as widespread in the world of wine as the notion that low yields make better wines. There is a good deal of truth in it, but the relationship between how much fruit a vineyard produces and the quality of the resulting wine is not as simple as a rule of inverse proportion. Understanding why requires looking at the plant as a whole.

How yield is measured

Yield expresses the amount of grapes or wine produced by a given area of vineyard. It is measured in several ways:

  • Kilograms of grapes per hectare: the usual measure in Spain and many other countries.
  • Hectolitres of wine per hectare: the most common form in France and other European regions; it also depends on how much juice is extracted at pressing.
  • Kilograms per vine: perhaps the most revealing figure, because it takes planting density into account. The same yield per hectare can mean a lot of fruit on a few vines or a little fruit spread across many.

That is why per-hectare figures, taken in isolation, can be misleading: two vineyards with the same total output may be placing very different loads on each plant.

Why quantity affects quality

A vine has a limited capacity to produce sugars through photosynthesis in its leaves. If it has to ripen too many bunches, those resources are spread over more fruit and ripening may fall short: less sugar, less colour, less developed aromas and less ripe tannins. In extreme cases the plant becomes exhausted and its productive life is shortened.

What matters, therefore, is not the absolute figure but the balance between the area of leaves well exposed to the sun and the amount of fruit. A balanced vine has enough foliage to ripen its crop, but not so much that it shades itself or wastes energy on growth.

When less is not better

Reducing the crop does not always improve the wine. A vine carrying very little fruit and with plenty of vigour may simply keep producing leaves and shoots without concentrating its grapes, and some varieties respond to a light crop with larger berries. In some cases, an excessively low yield speeds up sugar accumulation without the other components keeping pace, giving alcoholic, unbalanced wines.

Moreover, each variety and each site has its own point of balance. Some grapes give high-quality wines at relatively generous yields, while others lose character as soon as they are pushed. Great wines from very low yields, such as those from old vines on poor soils, are the result of a combination of factors and not merely of scarce fruit.

How yield is controlled

Growers have several tools for adjusting production:

  • Choice of site and plant material: poorer soils, low-vigour rootstocks and less productive clones limit the crop from the outset.
  • Planting density: more vines per hectare allow production to be spread out, so that each plant carries less fruit.
  • Pruning: the main tool. By leaving more or fewer buds in winter, the grower determines the number of shoots and therefore of potential bunches.
  • Shoot thinning: the removal in spring of surplus or badly placed shoots.
  • Bunch thinning or green harvesting: bunches are removed during the summer, often around véraison, so that the vine concentrates its resources on those that remain. It is effective but costly, and must be applied with judgement.
  • Water management: where irrigation is permitted, how much is given has a strong influence on berry size and output.

The role of regulation

Many appellations set maximum permitted yields per hectare, which may vary by wine style and, in some cases, be adjusted each year according to the harvest. These limits are intended to protect the quality and reputation of the area while also regulating the amount of wine that reaches the market. As a rule, the most demanding categories have stricter limits.

The legal maximum, however, is only a ceiling. Producers seeking more concentrated wines often work well below it, while others come close to the limit. The label rarely tells you the actual yield.

Yield, weather and vintage

Yield also varies from year to year. Spring frosts, poor weather during flowering, hail or drought can drastically reduce the crop, and that does not always mean higher quality: a vintage cut short by frost does not necessarily produce better grapes. Conversely, a generous year with a favourable summer and autumn can produce plenty of good-quality fruit.

With climate change, many areas are seeing faster ripening, and the balance between crop load and foliage needs rethinking: in some places, a slightly larger crop helps to slow down sugar accumulation that would otherwise be too rapid.

A question of balance

In short, the relationship between yield and quality is real but not linear. The aim is not to produce as little as possible, but as much as each vine, in its soil and climate, can fully ripen. That optimum depends on the variety, the age of the plant, the style of wine sought and the conditions of each year, and finding it is one of the grower's essential skills.
