Crianza, reserva and gran reserva (and their equivalents in other countries)

What the ageing terms on Spanish wines mean, what minimum periods they require and how they compare with Italian riserva, Portuguese garrafeira and other terms.

The words crianza, reserva and gran reserva are among the best known on Spanish labels. They are not mere marketing terms: they are traditional terms regulated by law that indicate a minimum period of ageing in barrel and in bottle before the wine is released. Knowing what they mean helps you understand a wine's style, although they do not by themselves guarantee quality.

The origin of the categories

Ageing in oak barrels and in bottle has a long tradition in Spain, especially in Rioja, where it became established in the nineteenth century under Bordeaux influence. Over time wineries began to distinguish their wines by the length of ageing, and legislation eventually set common minimums. Spain's wine law now establishes the general requirements, which designations of origin may tighten in their own rules.

General minimums in Spain

For wines with a designation of origin, the general rule is as follows. Periods are counted from the harvest, and ageing in wood must take place in oak barrels with a maximum capacity of 330 litres.

  • Crianza: for reds, a minimum ageing period of 24 months, at least 6 of them in barrel. For whites and rosés, 18 months, with at least 6 in barrel.
  • Reserva: for reds, a minimum of 36 months, with at least 12 in barrel and the rest in bottle. For whites and rosés, 24 months, with at least 6 in barrel.
  • Gran reserva: for reds, a minimum of 60 months, with at least 18 in barrel and the rest in bottle. For whites and rosés, 48 months, with at least 6 in barrel.

The law also provides for other, less common terms, such as noble, añejo and viejo, which indicate periods of ageing in wooden vessels or in bottle; the last is reserved for wines with a marked oxidative character.

Appellation rules

Many designations require more than the legal minimum. In Rioja, for example, a red crianza must spend at least one year in barrel, and a gran reserva at least two years in barrel and as many in bottle. In Ribera del Duero, crianza also requires a minimum of one year in barrel. Each designation publishes its rules in its product specification, and the Regulatory Councils check compliance through numbered back labels, which usually have different designs for each category.

Young wines and wines without a category

Wines that have not been aged in wood, or only briefly, are often sold as joven or, in some areas, with the traditional term cosecha. Many designations also use the word roble for wines with shorter barrel ageing than a crianza, although its use and definition vary by region.

It is important to know that many high-quality wines carry none of these terms. Some wineries prefer not to follow the established periods, or decide that the wine better expresses its vineyard with a different ageing regime. The absence of the word reserva therefore does not indicate lower quality. In recent years, moreover, some designations have developed classifications based on origin, such as village wines or single-vineyard wines in Rioja, which emphasise place rather than length of ageing.

What each category suggests in the glass

Broadly speaking, a joven stands out for its fresh fruit. A crianza combines fruit with oak notes such as vanilla, toast or spice. A reserva usually shows more integration between fruit and oak and some developed aromas. A gran reserva, after years in bottle, tends to have a complex bouquet with notes of leather, tobacco, forest floor or nuts, and softened tannins. These are tendencies, not rules: the winery's style, the variety, the vintage and the type of oak matter as much as the category.

Wineries usually reserve the reserva and gran reserva categories for the best vintages or for grapes from their best plots, although this is not a general legal requirement.

Equivalents in other countries

Other countries use similar terms, but with different rules, and it is best not to assume they mean the same thing.

  • Italy: the term riserva is regulated by the disciplinare of each DOC or DOCG, which sets a minimum ageing period longer than that of the basic wine of the same appellation. Periods vary widely from area to area: in Barolo, Brunello di Montalcino or Chianti Classico, for example, each set of rules establishes its own requirements. Some appellations also use the term superiore, which usually refers to higher alcohol or stricter production conditions, not necessarily to longer ageing.
  • Portugal: the word reserva applies to wines with certain quality characteristics, and garrafeira designates wines with a longer minimum ageing period, part of it in bottle.
  • France: in general there are no equivalent legal ageing categories. Terms such as réserve or grande réserve are usually commercial and lack a common definition; ageing requirements, where they exist, are set by each appellation, as with Champagne's minimum periods on the lees.
  • South American countries: in Chile and Argentina there are terms such as reserva or gran reserva with their own legal definitions, different from Spain's, which in some cases are based more on alcohol level or other conditions than on long ageing.
  • In other countries, such as the United States or Australia, these terms are generally not regulated and their use depends on each producer.

How to use this information

Ageing terms are a useful clue to a wine's style and drinking window: a gran reserva has already spent years in the winery and is usually ready to drink, while a joven is best enjoyed in its first few years. But they are no substitute for information about the winery, the vintage or the variety. Read together with the rest of the label, they help you find the right wine for each occasion and each dish.
