Food pairing

Wine and Italian cuisine

Italian cooking is a mosaic of regional traditions that grew up alongside their wines; this article explains why the acidity of Italian reds suits tomato so well and surveys the classic pairings from north to south.

To speak of “Italian cuisine” is, in a sense, a simplification. Italy is a country of enormous gastronomic diversity, where each region, and even each city, keeps its own recipes, produce and customs. That diversity is mirrored in its wine: few countries grow so many different native grape varieties. For centuries, dishes and wines have developed together in the same places, which is why the advice most often repeated by Italian sommeliers remains simple and effective: with a regional dish, try a wine from the same region first.

Acidity: the key to Italian reds

Many of the great Italian reds, from sangiovese to nebbiolo or barbera, are marked by high acidity. This quality, which sometimes surprises those used to riper, softer reds, is precisely what makes them such good table companions. Tomato, ubiquitous in much of Italian cooking, is an acidic ingredient; a wine with little acidity seems flat and dull beside it, while a lively red keeps the balance. Acidity also offsets olive oil, aged cheeses and fatty meats, refreshing the palate between mouthfuls.

Pasta and pizza

There is no single wine for “pasta”: the sauce is what matters. A simple tomato sauce calls for a young, fresh red, such as a Chianti, a barbera or a Montepulciano d'Abruzzo. A Bolognese-style meat ragù can take a red with a little more structure. Sauces based on butter, cream or cheese, such as carbonara or cacio e pepe, are better suited to whites with some texture or to light reds without pronounced tannins. Genoese pesto, with basil, garlic, pine nuts and cheese, finds a classic partner in Vermentino from the Ligurian coast, whose herbal notes and saline touch accompany it naturally. For a pizza margherita, the combination of tomato, mozzarella and dough once again favours young, acidic reds, although a rosé or a white from the south also works.

The north: Piedmont, Lombardy and the Veneto

In Piedmont, autumn cooking revolves around mushrooms, game and the famous white truffle of Alba. Nebbiolo wines such as Barolo and Barbaresco, with their aromas of roses, tar and red fruit, their acidity and their firm tannins, are the traditional partners for tajarin with truffle, beef braised in wine or the local aged cheeses. For everyday meals, barbera and dolcetto accompany cured meats, pasta dishes and simple meat courses. Further east, in the Veneto, seafood risotto and fish from the Venetian lagoon go well with a Soave, made from garganega, while the corvina-based reds of Valpolicella, in their lighter versions, suit polenta and white meats.

Emilia-Romagna: the case of Lambrusco

Emilia-Romagna is a land of cured meats, cheeses and egg pasta: prosciutto di Parma, mortadella from Bologna, Parmigiano Reggiano, tortellini. In this context, so rich in fat and salt, the traditional wine is Lambrusco, a sparkling red, usually dry or slightly off-dry, with marked acidity. Its bubbles and freshness cleanse the palate, making it a perfect example of how a local wine answers the needs of its region's cooking.

The centre: Tuscany and its surroundings

Tuscan cooking is simple and built on its ingredients: pulses, bread, olive oil, grilled meats. Sangiovese is its great grape. A Chianti Classico goes very well with ribollita, pici with ragù or the region's salumi, and the famous bistecca alla fiorentina, cooked over embers, is traditionally served with a more structured sangiovese such as a Brunello di Montalcino. On the coast, the wines of Bolgheri, often made from Bordeaux varieties, suit red meat and game.

The south and the islands

Southern Italy offers warmer reds and whites of notable personality. In Campania, buffalo mozzarella and fish and seafood dishes call for whites such as Fiano, while aglianico, a robust and tannic red, accompanies braised meats and aged cheeses. In Apulia, ripe and generous primitivo goes well with orecchiette with ragù or with grilled meats. In Sicily, nero d'Avola accompanies aubergine dishes such as caponata or pasta alla Norma, and the fresh, mineral whites of Etna suit the island's fish and shellfish.

Aperitivo and dessert

The aperitivo is a deeply rooted ritual in Italy, and sparkling wines such as Prosecco from the Veneto or the traditional-method wines of Franciacorta and Trentino are regular protagonists. At the end of the meal, Moscato d'Asti, light, sweet and only gently sparkling, goes well with fruit desserts and simple pastries, and wines made from dried grapes, such as Tuscan vin santo, are traditionally enjoyed with almond biscuits. In every case, the spirit of the Italian table invites drinking alongside food and in moderation.
