Wine and rice dishes

Rice is a neutral canvas that takes on the character of its stock and ingredients; this article explains how to choose wine for paellas, seafood, black, soupy or baked rice dishes and for Italian risotto.

Rice is one of the most widely eaten foods in the world and, in itself, one of the most neutral. Its discreet flavour and its ability to absorb stocks, fats and aromas mean that every rice dish is, in reality, the sum of what goes with it. For pairing, this has a clear consequence: one does not choose a wine for “rice”, but for the stock, the main protein, the spices and the final texture of the dish. In Spain, where rice cookery is enormously varied, especially in the Valencia region, Murcia, Catalonia and Andalusia, and in Italy, with its risotto tradition, these differences are particularly evident.

Texture: dry, creamy and soupy

Before the ingredients, it is worth looking at the texture. A dry rice dish, such as paella, in which the grains remain separate and sometimes form a toasted layer at the bottom, the socarrat, has concentrated flavours and toasty notes. A creamy rice, meloso in Spanish, with a little more stock and a soft texture, is more enveloping. A soupy rice, caldoso, almost a soup, is lighter and more fluid. In general, the more concentrated and toasted the dish, the more body the wine can have; the lighter and soupier it is, the more a fresh, delicate wine is advisable.

Valencian paella

Traditional Valencian paella is made with chicken, rabbit, ferraura-type green beans and garrofó, a large white bean, and is flavoured with saffron, paprika and sometimes rosemary. It is a dish of meat and vegetables, not seafood, and its flavour is savoury without being heavy. It suits several styles of wine. Dry rosés from the Valencia region itself, such as those made from bobal in Utiel-Requena, are a very consistent choice: they have fruit, freshness and some structure. Fuller-bodied whites and young, fruity reds with moderate tannins that do not mask the saffron also work.

Fish and seafood rice dishes

Seafood rice dishes, such as seafood paella, arroz a banda, cooked in an intense fish stock and served with allioli, or arroz del senyoret, with the shellfish already peeled, generally call for white wines. The salinity and iodine of seafood go very well with Atlantic whites with good acidity, such as Albariño from Rías Baixas or Godello from Valdeorras, and also with rounder Mediterranean whites made from macabeo, xarel·lo or garnacha blanca. If the rice includes lobster or langoustines, a white with some lees ageing provides the necessary texture. Allioli, rich in garlic and oil, is softened by wines with marked acidity and, of course, by dry sparkling wines such as cava, which cleanse the palate of fat.

Black rice

Black rice owes its colour and deep flavour to cuttlefish or squid ink. It is a savoury dish, redolent of the sea, with a slight mineral note. As well as full-bodied whites, it goes very well with biologically aged wines such as fino or manzanilla, whose saline character and pungent notes converse with the ink. A rosé with some structure is another reasonable option.

Meat, mountain and baked rice dishes

Inland in the Valencia region, in Murcia and in Alicante there are meat rice dishes with rabbit, pork ribs, sausages or snails, and baked rice dishes with chickpeas, morcilla and pork belly. These are heartier dishes that call for red wines with some structure. Monastrell from Alicante and Jumilla, warm and ripe-fruited, suits them very well, as do Mediterranean garnachas. Arroz con costra from Elche, with its layer of egg set in the oven over a rice made with meat and sausages, is a typical example of a dish that goes well with a local red.

Vegetable and soupy rice dishes

Vegetable rice dishes, such as those made with artichokes, cauliflower or mushrooms, depend greatly on the main ingredient. Artichokes are difficult for wine, and dry, fresh whites or a fino work best with them. With mushrooms, a light red or a white with some ageing suit well. Soupy fish rice dishes, which are subtler, are best accompanied by light, aromatic whites, while meat versions, such as a soupy rice with pigeon, can take moderately aged reds.

Italian risotto

Risotto is made with rice varieties that release starch, and it is finished with butter and cheese, which gives it its characteristic creamy texture. Risotto alla milanese, with saffron and bone marrow, is traditionally accompanied by structured whites or light reds from northern Italy. Mushroom risotto, and truffle risotto in particular, finds a classic partner in the nebbiolo reds of Piedmont or the Pinot Noirs of Burgundy, while the seafood risotto of the Veneto prefers fresh whites such as Soave.

A simple rule

To sum up, it can be said that seafood rice dishes call for whites, meat rice dishes for rosés or reds, and vegetable rice dishes depend on their main ingredient. When the table brings together several rice dishes, or in case of doubt, a dry rosé with good acidity or a brut sparkling wine are usually the most versatile choices. And, as with all regional cooking, a wine from the same area often offers the most natural starting point.
