Rheinhessen

Rheinhessen is Germany’s largest wine region, a landscape of gentle hills within the bend formed by the Rhine between Worms, Mainz and Bingen. After decades associated with high-volume wines, it is now one of the country’s most dynamic regions.

History

Winegrowing in Rheinhessen has Roman roots, and in the Middle Ages the city of Mainz, seat of a powerful archbishopric, and numerous monasteries owned vineyards in the area. The name Liebfraumilch comes from the vineyard of the Church of Our Lady (Liebfrauenkirche) in Worms, whose wine was already renowned in the eighteenth century; in the twentieth century the name was applied to a soft, cheap white wine produced in large volumes in several Rhine regions, which for decades shaped the image of German wine abroad. In the early twentieth century, however, the Rieslings of Nierstein and Nackenheim were among the most highly regarded in Germany. Since the late 1990s a growing group of young producers has transformed the region’s reputation with dry wines from individual vineyards.

Territory and climate

The region occupies a rolling plateau bounded to the east and north by the Rhine and to the west by the river Nahe. The Hunsrück, the Taunus and the Palatinate Forest shelter it from wind and rain, so the climate is temperate and relatively dry. Several areas are usually distinguished: the Rheinterrasse, along the Rhine, with Nierstein, Nackenheim and Oppenheim; the Wonnegau, in the south, around Westhofen and Flörsheim-Dalsheim; the surroundings of Bingen, facing the Rheingau; and the so-called Rheinhessen Switzerland in the west, with higher hills. In the north, Ingelheim, where Charlemagne had an imperial palace built, has a long tradition of red wines, above all from Spätburgunder.

Soils

Loess, marl and limestone predominate. On the banks of the Rhine, between Nackenheim and Nierstein, the Roter Hang (“red slope”) stands out, a strip of red clay and sandstone soils from the Permian period (Rotliegend). In the west there are porphyries of volcanic origin, and at Bingen, quartzite and slate.

Grapes and styles

Rheinhessen grows a great variety of grapes. Riesling produces the most highly regarded wines, from those of the Roter Hang to those of the limestone vineyards of the Wonnegau. Müller-Thurgau and Silvaner, the region’s traditional variety, remain widespread, along with Dornfelder, Pinot Noir, Weissburgunder and Grauburgunder. Today simple, fruity wines coexist with highly ambitious dry wines, which have made the region a benchmark in the renewal of German wine.

Country
Germany
Climate
Temperate and relatively dry, sheltered by the hills surrounding the region.
Soils
Loess, marl and limestone; red Permian clay and sandstone on the Roter Hang.
Styles
Dry single-vineyard Rieslings, traditional Silvaners and a wide range of fruity whites and reds.
Grapes
Riesling, Silvaner, Müller-Thurgau, Pinot Noir, Pinot Gris
