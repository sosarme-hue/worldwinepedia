Kent

Kent, in the far south-east of England, is one of the country’s main wine areas. On the chalk hills of the North Downs and the soils of the Weald, it produces mainly traditional-method sparkling wines and still whites from Bacchus and Chardonnay.

History

Traditionally known as the “Garden of England” for its orchards and hop fields, Kent has a long agricultural vocation. There were already vineyards in the region in the Middle Ages, linked partly to religious institutions such as those of Canterbury, but modern viticulture began in the second half of the twentieth century with small plantings of early-ripening varieties. In the Tenterden area, in the Weald, vines were planted at the end of the 1970s, and Chapel Down, which has become one of the country’s largest producers, later established itself there.

With the rise of English sparkling wine, Kent has attracted significant investment in recent decades. New vineyards have been planted on the chalk slopes of the North Downs and in other parts of the county, and the Champagne house Taittinger chose Kent for its English project. Other well-known producers in the area include Gusbourne, near Appledore, and Simpsons, near Canterbury. Kent has no designation of origin of its own, and its wines use the national English designations.

Climate

Kent is among the warmest and driest parts of England, with maritime influence from the Thames estuary, the North Sea and the English Channel. The growing season is long and cool, with mild summers that allow slow ripening and retention of acidity. Spring frosts are the main risk in low-lying areas, and the choice of well-ventilated slopes is important to limit their effects.

Soils

The North Downs, which cross the county from west to east as far as the cliffs of Dover, are made of chalk, like the South Downs of Sussex and Champagne; their south-facing slopes are considered particularly suited to sparkling wine. To the south lies the Weald, with clay, silt and sand soils that are more fertile and retentive, where some of the region’s oldest vineyards are found. Near the south-east coast there are also clay and sandy soils of sedimentary origin.

Varieties and styles

As in the rest of southern England, most of the vineyard area is devoted to Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Pinot Meunier for traditional-method sparkling wines, made in non-vintage, vintage, Blanc de Blancs and rosé versions. Kent is also one of the areas most associated with Bacchus, which gives aromatic still whites with notes of elderflower, citrus and grass, often compared with Sauvignon Blanc. Still Chardonnay and Pinot Noir are also made, along with small quantities of other varieties.
