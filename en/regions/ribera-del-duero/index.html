Designation of Origin (DO)

Ribera del Duero

Ribera del Duero is a designation of origin in Castilla y León, created in 1982, that runs along the Duero river on a high plateau. It is known for its reds made from Tempranillo, called Tinto Fino or Tinta del País locally.

History

Viticulture along the banks of the Duero has ancient roots and in the Middle Ages was linked to monasteries, among them those of the Cistercian order, which spread vine growing across the area. The underground cellars dug beneath towns such as Aranda de Duero recall this long tradition, in which the stable temperature below ground was used to keep wine. Vega Sicilia was founded in 1864, and its wines brought prestige to the area for much of the twentieth century. The Designation of Origin was created in 1982, and since then the number of wineries has grown considerably, turning the region into one of the great benchmarks for Spanish red wine.

Territory and climate

The designation stretches along the Duero through four provinces of Castilla y León: Burgos, Valladolid, Segovia and Soria. Its best-known towns include Aranda de Duero, Peñafiel and Roa. The vineyards lie on a high plateau, roughly between 700 and just over 1,000 metres above sea level. The climate is continental, with long cold winters, hot dry summers, little rainfall and a wide difference between day and night temperatures, which helps preserve acidity and develop colour. Spring and autumn frosts are one of the main risks for the vineyards.

Soils

Soils are varied: layers of sand, silt and clay alternate with strata of limestone and marl, while the terraces near the river contain gravel and rounded stones. The limestone slopes and high moorlands known as páramos are considered especially suitable for reds with ageing potential.

Grapes and styles

The main variety is Tempranillo, known locally as Tinto Fino or Tinta del País, which must make up at least 75% of red wines. Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Malbec and Grenache are also permitted. For decades the designation covered only red and rosé wines, but a recent amendment to the regulations has added white wines made from the local Albillo Mayor variety. The reds tend to show deep colour, ripe black fruit and a firm tannic structure.

Ageing

In addition to young reds, the designation recognises the traditional ageing terms. Crianza requires at least two years, twelve months of them in oak barrels; Reserva three years, with a minimum of twelve months in barrel; and Gran Reserva five years, of which at least twenty-four months in barrel. Many wineries also make wines that do not fit these categories and are sold without an ageing term.
