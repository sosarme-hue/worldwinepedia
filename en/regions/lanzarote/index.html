Designation of Origin (DO)

Lanzarote

Lanzarote is the designation of origin of the Canary island of the same name, where vines are grown in pits dug into volcanic ash and protected by stone walls. Its emblematic variety is Malvasía Volcánica.

History

Lanzarote's wine history is marked by the volcanic eruptions of Timanfaya, which between 1730 and 1736 covered a large part of the island with lava and ash. Far from abandoning the land, farmers discovered that the layer of volcanic ash, called picón or rofe, made it possible to conserve the little moisture in the soil, and they developed a unique growing system. From then on the vine became one of the island's main crops, and Canary Malvasía, already known in European markets, found one of its production areas here. The designation of origin has helped protect and promote this exceptional vineyard.

Climate

The climate is subtropical and arid, with mild temperatures all year round, very scarce and irregular rainfall and the constant influence of the trade winds blowing from the north-east. The high temperatures on some summer days and the lack of water would make vine growing very difficult without the traditional techniques.

Soils and growing system

Beneath the layer of volcanic ash lies the original fertile soil. In the best-known area, La Geria, growers dig large pits in the picón down to that soil and plant one or more vines at the bottom of each. Each pit is protected from the wind by a small semicircular wall of dry volcanic stone called a zoco. The ash acts as a mantle that absorbs moisture from dew and the occasional rain and reduces evaporation. Other areas use trenches and linear walls, or enarenado, which covers the ground with a layer of picón. The result is a singular landscape, largely protected. As phylloxera never reached the islands, the vines are grown ungrafted.

Grapes and styles

Malvasía Volcánica is the most characteristic and most widely planted variety. It gives dry, off-dry and sweet whites with floral, ripe fruit and saline notes and good acidity. Listán Negro, the basis for reds and rosés, Listán Blanco, Muscat of Alexandria and Diego are also grown, among others. Producers make dry whites, sweet Malvasía and Moscatel wines, young and barrel-aged reds, rosés and some sparkling wines. Sweet Malvasía wines, made from very ripe or dried grapes, keep alive the island's historic tradition.

Viticulture

Cultivation is almost entirely manual, as the layout of the pits and walls rules out mechanisation. Yields are very low and each vine requires individual care, which makes Lanzarote's vineyard one of the most striking examples of viticulture adapting to extreme conditions.
