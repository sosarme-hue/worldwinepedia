Coonawarra

Coonawarra, in the south-east of South Australia, is famous for a narrow strip of terra rossa over limestone. It is the Australian region most closely associated with Cabernet Sauvignon.

History

Coonawarra lies in the Limestone Coast zone, about 350-400 kilometres south-east of Adelaide and close to the border with the state of Victoria. Its winegrowing history began in 1890, when the Scottish entrepreneur John Riddoch founded the Penola Fruit Colony and divided land among settlers to grow fruit trees and vines. In 1891 the triple-gabled winery was built that now belongs to Wynns Coonawarra Estate and has become a symbol of the region. During the first half of the twentieth century the area struggled because of its isolation and the dominance of fortified wines, and much of its fruit went to distillation or blends.

Recovery came from the 1950s, when the Wynn family bought Riddoch’s old winery and other companies began to invest in the region. Since then Coonawarra has established itself as one of Australia’s most prestigious sources of red wine. Its official boundaries, linked partly to the extent of the red earth, were the subject of long disputes before being fixed as a geographical indication.

Climate and soils

The region is flat and its climate is cool by Australian standards, influenced by the Southern Ocean, about 80 kilometres away. Spring can bring frost, summers are mild and dry and rainfall is concentrated in winter; irrigation draws on a shallow water table. What sets Coonawarra apart is its so-called terra rossa: a narrow strip of reddish earth, about fifteen kilometres long and less than two wide, lying over a layer of soft limestone. This soil offers good drainage and, at the same time, moisture reserves at depth. Around it lie darker, clay-rich soils, known locally as black soils, which are considered less suited to great reds.

Styles

Cabernet Sauvignon is the main variety and gives reds with black fruit and cassis, minty and leafy notes, marked acidity and firm tannins, capable of ageing for decades. Shiraz, historically the most widely planted variety, produces medium-bodied, spicy wines. Merlot, Cabernet Franc and Petit Verdot are also grown for Bordeaux-style blends, along with some Chardonnay and Riesling. Many wineries in other regions buy Coonawarra fruit for their blends.
