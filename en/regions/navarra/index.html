Designation of Origin (DO)

Navarra

Navarra is one of Spain's oldest designations of origin, with five subzones ranging from the pre-Pyrenean valleys to the Ebro plain. It was traditionally famous for its Grenache rosés and now produces a wide range of reds, whites and sweet Moscatel wines.

History

Navarra's viticulture has Roman roots and developed in the Middle Ages under the protection of monasteries and the Way of St James, which crosses the region and brought pilgrims, trade and exchanges with the other side of the Pyrenees. The old Kingdom of Navarre maintained significant wine production for centuries. After phylloxera, Grenache became the dominant variety, and for much of the twentieth century the region was synonymous with rosé. From the last decades of that century, vine and wine research promoted by the regional government and the arrival of varieties such as Tempranillo, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and Chardonnay profoundly diversified its output. Navarra is one of the oldest designations of origin in Spain.

Territory and subzones

The designation covers the southern half of the Chartered Community of Navarre and is divided into five subzones: Baja Montaña, Valdizarbe and Tierra Estella in the north, which are cooler and wetter; and Ribera Alta and Ribera Baja in the south, along the Ebro and its tributaries, which are warmer and drier. Part of Navarre's territory also belongs to the Rioja DOCa, and several Vinos de Pago are located in Navarre as well.

Climate and soils

The climate is transitional: the northern subzones are influenced by the Atlantic and the mountains, with more rain and moderate temperatures, while the Ribera has a dry continental climate with Mediterranean tendencies, hot summers and frequent cierzo, a strong, dry north-westerly wind. Soils are generally calcareous and brown, with areas of gravel and rounded stones on the river terraces and alluvial soils near the rivers.

Grapes and styles

The leading red grapes are Tempranillo, Grenache, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Graciano, Mazuelo (Carignan), Syrah and Pinot Noir. Among the whites are Chardonnay, Viura (Macabeo), White Grenache, Malvasía, Sauvignon Blanc and Moscatel de grano menudo (Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains). Grenache rosé remains an emblematic style; it is traditionally made by the saignée method, running off the juice after brief contact with the skins. Alongside it, producers make young and oak-aged reds, often blends of local and international varieties, fresh or barrel-fermented whites, and sweet Moscatel wines, including late-harvest examples.

Ageing

Reds may carry the terms Crianza, Reserva and Gran Reserva, according to the periods of barrel and bottle ageing laid down in the regulations, and wines with a brief spell in wood are also made.
