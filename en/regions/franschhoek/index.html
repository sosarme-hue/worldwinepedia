Franschhoek

Franschhoek, the “French corner”, is a mountain-ringed valley east of Stellenbosch where the Huguenots settled in the late seventeenth century. It is known for its old Semillon vines, its sparkling wines and its food scene.

History

From 1688 several groups of Huguenots, French Protestants who had left their country after the revocation of the Edict of Nantes, arrived at the Cape. The Dutch East India Company granted them land in a valley then called Olifantshoek, “elephants’ corner”, which soon came to be known as Franschhoek, “French corner”. The newcomers brought their farming and winegrowing knowledge, and many of the valley’s estates still bear the French names they received at that time, such as La Motte, La Provence and Bellingham. Although the French language was lost within a few generations, the Huguenot heritage is commemorated by the monument erected in the town in the twentieth century and by a museum devoted to their history.

For much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the valley combined vines with fruit orchards, and much of its grape harvest was sold to cooperatives. From the 1980s and 1990s many estates began bottling their own wines, and Franschhoek became one of the Cape’s great tourist destinations, with well-known restaurants, places to stay and a tourist tram that runs between the wineries.

Climate and soils

The valley is closed in by high mountains on three sides, which creates a Mediterranean climate that is somewhat warmer in summer than in the coastal areas, with higher winter rainfall and cool nights from the air that flows down from the ranges. Differences in altitude and aspect between the valley floor and the slopes are significant. On the valley floor, sandy alluvial soils of river origin predominate, while on the slopes there are soils of decomposed granite and Table Mountain sandstone, which are poorer and better drained.

Styles

Franschhoek preserves some of the oldest Semillon vines in South Africa, a variety that dominated Cape vineyards in the nineteenth century. They are used to make textured whites with notes of wax, citrus and honey that gain complexity with time. The valley is also an important centre for traditional-method sparkling wines, known as Méthode Cap Classique, based on Chardonnay and Pinot Noir. Among the reds, Cabernet Sauvignon and Syrah stand out, and Chenin Blanc, Chardonnay and Merlot are also grown. Some wineries in the area also buy grapes from other Cape regions, so not all the wines made in the valley carry the Franschhoek designation.
