Maipo Valley

The Maipo Valley, which surrounds Santiago, is the cradle of modern Chilean viticulture and the area most associated with the country’s Cabernet Sauvignon. Many of the historic estates founded in the nineteenth century are located there.

History

Its proximity to the capital explains the Maipo’s central role in the history of Chilean wine. In the mid-nineteenth century, families of the Santiago elite created estates inspired by the châteaux of Bordeaux, imported French vines and hired French winemakers. Emblematic estates such as Concha y Toro, Santa Rita, Santa Carolina and Cousiño Macul date from that period, some with historic manor houses and parks. In recent decades they have been joined by projects devoted to top-end wines, often with foreign partners, such as Almaviva.

Geography and climate

The valley follows the course of the Maipo River from the Andes to the coast and is part of the Central Valley region. The climate is Mediterranean, with dry, warm summers and mild, rainy winters. Proximity to the Andes strongly influences temperatures: cold air descending from the mountains at night cools the vineyards and prolongs ripening.

Subzones

Three sectors are usually distinguished. Alto Maipo, at the foot of the Andes, with areas such as Puente Alto and Pirque, between about 400 and 800 metres above sea level, is the coolest and the most highly regarded for Cabernet Sauvignon. The Middle Maipo, around Buin and Isla de Maipo, is warmer and flatter. The Lower Maipo or coastal sector, towards Talagante and Melipilla, receives greater Pacific influence. The terms Andes, Entre Cordilleras and Costa may appear on labels to reflect these differences.

Soils

In Alto Maipo, stony, poor alluvial and colluvial soils predominate, made up of gravel and stones from the mountains, which drain well and limit vigour. In the middle valley the soils are deeper, with clays and silts.

Grapes and styles

Cabernet Sauvignon is the emblematic variety: it gives reds with black fruit and cassis, often with notes of mint, eucalyptus and herbs, of firm structure and good ageing potential. Carmenère, Merlot, Syrah, Cabernet Franc and Petit Verdot are also grown and feature in many Bordeaux-style blends. Whites play a minor role, as the country’s cooler coastal areas are better suited to them. The urban growth of Santiago puts pressure on some historic vineyards.

Many of the valley’s historic estates retain their manor houses, old cellars and parks, and several welcome visitors, making the Maipo a wine tourism destination within easy reach of Santiago.
