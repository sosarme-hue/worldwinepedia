Geographical Indication (GI)

Adelaide Hills

A high-altitude region in the Mount Lofty Ranges, just east of Adelaide, in South Australia. Its cool climate has made it an Australian benchmark for Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and sparkling wine.

Territory

Adelaide Hills occupies a strip of the Mount Lofty Ranges running north to south east of the city of Adelaide, the capital of South Australia. It is a landscape of wooded hills, valleys, apple and cherry orchards, villages of German origin such as Hahndorf, and vineyards scattered on slopes at altitudes that are considerable by Australian standards. The region borders other famous areas: the Barossa and Eden Valley to the north and McLaren Vale to the south. Within the GI two subregions are recognised, Piccadilly Valley and Lenswood, both among the coolest parts.

History

There were vineyards in these hills in the nineteenth century, but most disappeared in the early twentieth. Modern winegrowing developed mainly from the 1970s and 1980s, when producers seeking cool climates planted Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Sauvignon Blanc. Since then the region has become one of Australia’s most important sources of quality fruit for cool-climate wines, and many wineries, large and small, buy grapes here.

Climate and soils

Altitude is the key factor: temperatures are clearly lower than on the plains around Adelaide, with cool nights and slow ripening. Rainfall is relatively high in winter and spring, and the highest sectors are at risk of frost. Differences in elevation, aspect and exposure create a mosaic of mesoclimates, from cold sites suited to sparkling wine to warmer slopes where Shiraz ripens well. The soils are varied: loams and silts over clay, sandstone, schist and slate, generally of moderate fertility.

Grapes and styles

Sauvignon Blanc was the variety that made Adelaide Hills famous, with whites showing tropical and citrus fruit and lively acidity. Chardonnay produces some of Australia’s most highly regarded whites, often taut and elegant in style, with restrained barrel ageing. Pinot Noir gives light- to medium-bodied reds and, together with Chardonnay, is the basis for traditional-method sparkling wines. Shiraz from the warmer sectors shows a spicy, fresh profile, different from that of the neighbouring valleys. In recent decades varieties such as Grüner Veltliner, Pinot Gris, Nebbiolo and Sangiovese have been successfully planted, reflecting a region open to experimentation, where many small producers welcome visitors in cellar doors scattered among the hills and villages.
