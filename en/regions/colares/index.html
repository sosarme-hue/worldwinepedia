Controlled Designation of Origin (DOC)

Colares

A tiny Atlantic appellation near Sintra, west of Lisbon, famous for its ungrafted vines planted in sand, which withstood phylloxera. Its Ramisco reds and Malvasia de Colares whites are a historic rarity.

Viticulture in the sand

Colares lies on the coastal strip between the Serra de Sintra and the Atlantic Ocean, not far from Cabo da Roca, the westernmost point of mainland Europe. What makes it unique is its chão de areia: vines planted in deep layers of sand covering a clay subsoil. Phylloxera, the insect that devastated European vineyards in the late nineteenth century, cannot survive in such sandy soils, so the vines of Colares went on growing on their own roots and today are among the few ungrafted vineyards in Europe. During that period of crisis, Colares wines were in great demand and enjoyed considerable prestige, and the region was officially demarcated in the early twentieth century.

A laborious way of growing

Planting a vineyard in sand requires considerable work: traditionally, deep trenches were dug down to the clay, where the plant took root, and were then gradually filled in as it grew. The vines are trained to sprawl very close to the ground and are protected from the salty Atlantic wind by cane fences and low walls. This effort, together with the pressure of building and tourism around Sintra, has reduced the vineyard to a very small area. The appellation also includes a zone of chão rijo, with firmer soils, but it is the wines from the sand vineyards that define the identity of Colares.

Climate

The climate is cool and Atlantic, with mild summers, frequent mists, high humidity and constant sea winds. The grapes ripen slowly and keep high acidity, and yields are low.

Grapes and styles

The emblematic red grape is Ramisco, a local variety that gives deeply coloured wines with very lively acidity and firm tannins, often austere in youth and needing many years in bottle to reveal their complexity. The white is Malvasia de Colares, which produces saline, well-structured whites. Traditionally the wines were aged for a long time before release, and the Adega Regional de Colares, a local cooperative, has played a central role in making and selling them. Today, alongside it, a handful of producers keep this appellation alive, and its wines are regarded as one of the most singular curiosities of European wine.
