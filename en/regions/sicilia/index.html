Wine region

Sicily

Sicily, the largest island in the Mediterranean, has one of the largest vineyard areas in Italy. Its production ranges from Nero d'Avola reds and the volcanic wines of Etna to fortified Marsala and the sweet wines of Pantelleria and the Aeolian Islands.

History

Vines have been grown in Sicily since antiquity, and the Greek colonists who founded cities on the island spread their vine-growing and winemaking techniques. Over the centuries Phoenicians, Romans, Arabs, Normans and Spaniards left their mark on the island's agriculture. In 1773 the English merchant John Woodhouse began shipping wine from the Marsala area, fortified with alcohol, to England, and in the nineteenth century houses such as Ingham and Florio made Marsala an internationally known wine. For much of the twentieth century the island mainly produced bulk wine, often used to strengthen wines from other regions. Since the late 1980s and the 1990s, a new generation of wineries has promoted bottled wines from native varieties, and in 2011 the Sicilia DOC was created, covering the whole island.

Climate and territory

The climate is Mediterranean, with hot, dry summers, mild winters and abundant sunshine; winds such as the sirocco and the dry air reduce disease pressure in the vineyard. Differences are nonetheless considerable: the altitude of inland areas and of the slopes of Etna, as well as the influence of the sea on the coast, create very diverse conditions. The region also includes archipelagos with their own wine traditions, such as the Aeolian Islands and Pantelleria.

Soils

There are limestone, clay and sand over much of the island, white calcareous soils in the south-east and around Marsala, and volcanic soils on Etna, the Aeolian Islands and Pantelleria.

Grapes and styles

Nero d'Avola is the most widespread red variety and gives deeply coloured wines with ripe fruit. Nerello Mascalese is the leading grape on Etna, and light, aromatic Frappato is blended with Nero d'Avola in Cerasuolo di Vittoria, the island's only DOCG. Leading whites include Catarratto, Grillo, Inzolia, Grecanico and, on Etna, Carricante. Zibibbo, the local name for Muscat of Alexandria, gives Moscato and Passito di Pantelleria on that island; Pantelleria's traditional practice of growing vines as low bush vines was inscribed by UNESCO on the List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2014. Malvasia delle Lipari is made in the Aeolian Islands. International varieties such as Syrah and Chardonnay are also grown.

Designations

In addition to the Sicilia DOC and the Terre Siciliane IGT, the main designations include the Cerasuolo di Vittoria DOCG and the Etna, Marsala, Pantelleria, Malvasia delle Lipari, Faro and Vittoria DOCs, among others.

Climate
