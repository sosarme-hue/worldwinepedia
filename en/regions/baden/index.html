Baden

Baden is Germany’s southernmost wine region, a long strip stretching from the Tauber valley to Lake Constance, largely facing Alsace. It is the German home of the Pinot varieties, with Spätburgunder as its main grape.

History

Winegrowing in Baden has Roman roots and developed in the Middle Ages under monasteries and feudal lords. In the nineteenth century the region suffered, like the rest of Europe, from powdery mildew and phylloxera, and many small growers later organised themselves into cooperatives. These still carry great weight in the region: they make a considerable share of Baden’s wine, and Breisach, beside the Rhine, is home to one of the largest cooperative wineries in Europe. Alongside them, many family estates have given the region great prestige, particularly for its Spätburgunder and its Pinot-family whites.

Territory and climate

Baden stretches for some four hundred kilometres, mostly along the eastern side of the Upper Rhine valley, at the foot of the Black Forest. It is the warmest and sunniest region in Germany, which is why European regulations place it in a different wine-growing zone from the rest of the country, the same as Alsace. It is divided into several districts: Tauberfranken, in the north; the Badische Bergstrasse and Kraichgau; the Ortenau, around Durbach; the Breisgau; the Kaiserstuhl, an ancient volcanic massif rising alone from the Rhine plain, which is the warmest area; the Tuniberg; the Markgräflerland, between Freiburg and Basel; and the Bodensee, on the shores of Lake Constance, with vineyards at higher altitude.

Soils

The range of soils is wide: volcanic rock and thick layers of loess in the Kaiserstuhl, granite and gneiss in the Ortenau, limestone and loess in the Markgräflerland and Tuniberg, shell limestone in the Kraichgau and Tauberfranken, and glacial deposits around Lake Constance.

Grapes and styles

Spätburgunder (Pinot Noir) is the most widely planted variety in Baden and produces reds ranging from fruity styles to highly ambitious, structured, barrel-aged wines, especially in the Kaiserstuhl. It is followed by Müller-Thurgau, Grauburgunder (Pinot Gris), Weissburgunder and Gutedel (Chasselas), a speciality of the Markgräflerland. In the Ortenau, Riesling, known locally as Klingelberger, has a long tradition, and Chardonnay, Silvaner, Gewürztraminer and Sauvignon Blanc are also grown. On Lake Constance a traditional Spätburgunder rosé called Weissherbst is made. Baden wines are usually dry, with good body and more moderate acidity than in the northern regions.
