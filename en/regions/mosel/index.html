Mosel

The Mosel region follows the winding course of this river and its tributaries the Saar and Ruwer, with slate vineyards on extremely steep slopes. It is the birthplace of some of the most delicate and long-lived Rieslings in the world.

History

Winegrowing on the Mosel dates back to Roman times, when Trier was one of the capitals of the Empire. In the fourth century the poet Ausonius described in his poem Mosella the vines covering the river’s slopes, and Roman wine presses have been found in excavations in the area. During the Middle Ages the monasteries and the archbishopric of Trier were the great vineyard owners. In the nineteenth century the region’s finest wines fetched very high prices on international markets. Until 2007 the region was officially called Mosel-Saar-Ruwer.

Territory and climate

The river forms very tight loops between the Hunsrück and Eifel hills, and the best vineyards lie on south- or south-west-facing slopes, often with extraordinary gradients; the Calmont, near Bremm, is frequently cited as one of the steepest vineyards in Europe. The climate is cool and ripening is slow. The river and the slate store and reflect heat, which allows Riesling to ripen at this latitude. The region is usually divided into several sections: the Upper Mosel, beside Luxembourg; the Middle Mosel, with famous villages such as Piesport, Bernkastel, Graach, Wehlen, Zeltingen, Ürzig and Erden; the Lower Mosel or Terrassenmosel, down to Koblenz; and the cooler Saar and Ruwer valleys.

Soils

Devonian slate predominates, bluish grey in most of the region and reddish in some vineyards, as at Ürzig. This poor, stony soil drains well and warms up easily. In the Upper Mosel, by contrast, there are limestone soils, where the old Elbling variety is traditionally grown.

Grapes and styles

Riesling is by far the leading grape, followed by Müller-Thurgau and Elbling; Pinot Noir has also grown in recent years. Mosel Rieslings are known for their lightness, low alcohol, vibrant acidity and notes of white fruit, flowers and wet stone. Traditionally they were made mostly with some sweetness, in the Kabinett, Spätlese and Auslese categories, which remain emblematic, and in great years Beerenauslese, Trockenbeerenauslese and Eiswein are produced. Today more and more dry (trocken) and off-dry (feinherb) wines are also made. Vineyards such as the Bernkasteler Doctor, the Wehlener Sonnenuhr and the Scharzhofberg, on the Saar, are among the best known in Germany.

Country
Germany
Climate
Cool and northerly, moderated by the river and by the heat stored in the slate slopes.
Soils
Blue and red Devonian slate on very steep slopes; limestone in the Upper Mosel.
Styles
Light Rieslings of vibrant acidity, from dry to Kabinett, Spätlese and Auslese with sweetness, and nobly sweet wines.
Grapes
Riesling, Müller-Thurgau, Pinot Noir
