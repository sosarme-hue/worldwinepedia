Thracian Valley

The great plain of southern Bulgaria, between the Balkans and the Rhodopes, is the country’s warmest wine region and the home of Mavrud. It produces mainly red wines, from both native and international varieties.

The Thracian Valley occupies the broad plain crossed by the Maritsa river in southern Bulgaria, bounded to the north by the Balkan range and Sredna Gora and to the south by the Rhodope mountains. It covers the surroundings of towns such as Plovdiv, Pazardzhik, Asenovgrad, Stara Zagora and Haskovo and extends south-east to the Sakar hills, near the borders with Greece and Turkey. For legal purposes, its wines fall under the Thracian Lowlands protected geographical indication, shared by several local designations of origin such as Asenovgrad, Brestnik, Perushtitsa, Sakar and Stara Zagora.

History

The region was part of ancient Thrace, whose peoples placed wine at the centre of their rites, and treasures of gold and silver vessels intended for ceremonial drinking have been found here. Viticulture continued in Roman and Byzantine times and, under Ottoman rule, mainly around monasteries and Christian communities. In the second half of the twentieth century, the valley was one of the bases of the large state production geared to export. Since the 1990s, and especially after Bulgaria joined the European Union in 2007, many new wineries with their own vineyards have been established, some with foreign investment.

Climate and soils

It is the warmest part of the country. The climate is continental with Mediterranean influence, which arrives through the Maritsa valley: summers are long, warm and dry, autumns mild and winters less harsh than north of the Balkans, which act as a barrier against cold winds. Rainfall is moderate. Soils are varied: alluvial on the flood plains of the Maritsa and its tributaries, brown forest soils on the slopes, dark, heavy clays in some areas and sandier, stonier ground in the hills.

Grapes and styles

The emblematic native variety is Mavrud, a late-ripening red associated above all with the Asenovgrad area, south of Plovdiv, which gives deeply coloured wines with notable acidity and good ageing potential. Rubin, a Bulgarian cross of Nebbiolo and Syrah, and Pamid, which gives light wines, are also grown. Most of the vineyard area, however, is planted with international varieties: Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Syrah and Cabernet Franc among the reds, and Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc and the local Red Misket among the whites. The dominant style is ripe, structured reds, often barrel-aged and made as varietals or in blends in which Mavrud is combined with Bordeaux varieties or Syrah. Rosés and whites are also made, and are increasingly common.
