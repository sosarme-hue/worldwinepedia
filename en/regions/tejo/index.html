DOC do Tejo and Vinho Regional Tejo (GI)

Tejo

A region of central Portugal that follows the course of the Tagus river north-east of Lisbon. Formerly known as Ribatejo, it has moved from bulk production towards fresh, fruity wines offering good value.

History

The valley of the Tagus, the river the Portuguese call the Tejo, has been vine country for centuries, and its proximity to Lisbon and the ease of river transport turned the region into a great wine larder for the capital. For much of the twentieth century its name was Ribatejo, associated with plentiful wines sold in bulk or through large cooperatives. In the early twenty-first century the appellation changed its name to Tejo, at a time when producers were working to lower yields, replant vineyards on the most suitable sites and bottle wines with an identity of their own. Today the DOC do Tejo, with stricter rules, coexists with Vinho Regional Tejo, which allows greater freedom of grape varieties and under which most of the production is sold.

The three zones of the Tejo

The region is usually described in terms of three quite distinct landscapes. The Campo, or lezíria, occupies the alluvial plains along the river, with deep, fertile soils that used to flood periodically; here the vines are productive and the wines tend to be light. The Bairro, on the north bank, is an area of gentle hills with poorer clay-limestone and schist soils that give more structured wines. The Charneca, south of the river, has dry, sandy soils and a slightly warmer climate reminiscent of the neighbouring Alentejo; it is the zone where quality vineyards have expanded most.

Climate

The climate is Mediterranean, with warm, dry summers and mild winters, but the relatively nearby Atlantic and the river itself moderate temperatures and bring some humidity. Ripening is generally even, making it possible to obtain ripe fruit without losing all freshness.

Grapes and styles

The most characteristic white grape is Fernão Pires, widely planted in the region, which gives aromatic wines with floral and citrus notes and is also used for sparkling and late-harvest wines. It is joined by Arinto, which contributes acidity, and Talia, the local name for Trebbiano. Among the reds, Castelão and Trincadeira are important, together with Touriga Nacional, Alicante Bouschet and Aragonez (Tempranillo), as well as international varieties such as Syrah and Cabernet Sauvignon. The dominant style is fruity, approachable and moderately priced, although a growing number of quintas make oak-aged reds and more ambitious whites.

Country
Portugal
Climate
Mediterranean, with warm, dry summers moderated by Atlantic influence and by the river itself.
Soils
Fertile alluvium by the river (Campo), clay-limestone and schist to the north (Bairro) and sand to the south (Charneca).
Styles
Aromatic Fernão Pires whites and fruity reds, with a growing band of more ambitious oak-aged wines.
Grapes
Fernão Pires, Arinto, Trebbiano, Castelão, Trincadeira, Touriga Nacional, Alicante Bouschet, Tempranillo, Syrah (Shiraz), Cabernet Sauvignon
