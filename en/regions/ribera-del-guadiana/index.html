Ribera del Guadiana

Ribera del Guadiana is the designation of origin of Extremadura, divided into six subzones spread across the provinces of Badajoz and Cáceres. Tierra de Barros is the largest, and the region produces Tempranillo reds, whites from local varieties such as Cayetana Blanca and Pardina, and other styles.

History

Vines have been grown in Extremadura since Roman times, when Emerita Augusta, today's Mérida, was one of the great cities of Hispania. During the Middle Ages and the early modern period monasteries, such as that of Guadalupe, helped to keep the vineyards going. Many Extremaduran villages preserve the tradition of vino de pitarra, made at home and fermented in large earthenware jars. During the twentieth century the region became a major producer of bulk wine, largely destined for other parts of Spain. At the end of that century several specific district designations were recognised, and in 1999 they were merged into a single regional designation of origin, Ribera del Guadiana.

Subzones and territory

The designation is divided into six subzones. Tierra de Barros, in the centre of the province of Badajoz around Almendralejo, is the largest and most productive. Ribera Baja and Ribera Alta stretch along the river plains of the Guadiana. Matanegra lies south of Tierra de Barros, near Zafra. In the province of Cáceres are Montánchez, a hilly area of small plots, and Cañamero, on the slopes near Guadalupe, the highest subzone. The climate is Mediterranean with continental and Atlantic influences: summers are very hot and dry, winters mild and rainfall moderate, heavier in the hill areas.

Soils

In Tierra de Barros deep clay soils predominate, the barros that give the district its name, with a good capacity to retain water. The Guadiana plains have plenty of alluvial soils, while Montánchez and Cañamero have poorer soils of slate and granite.

Grape varieties

The leading reds are Tempranillo, Garnacha, Garnacha Tintorera, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Syrah and Graciano. The whites include traditional varieties of the region such as Cayetana Blanca, Pardina, Alarije and Eva (or Beba), alongside Macabeo, Verdejo, Chardonnay, Pedro Ximénez and Moscatel.

Styles

The designation produces young and aged reds, mainly from Tempranillo, with ripe fruit and medium to full body, as well as whites from local and international varieties, rosés and sparkling wines. Extremadura also produces cava: the municipality of Almendralejo is part of the production area of that designation. The region remains a major producer of bulk wine, but the number of wineries bottling under the designation has grown in recent decades.
