Bío-Bío Valley

A subregion in southern Chile, around the Biobío river, cooler and wetter than the central valleys. With a long tradition of País and Muscat, it now also stands out for aromatic whites and cool-climate Pinot Noir.

Territory

The Bío-Bío Valley lies in the south of winegrowing Chile, some five hundred kilometres south of Santiago, around the Biobío river, one of the country’s largest. Together with the Itata Valley to the north and the Malleco Valley to the south, it forms the Southern wine region. Towns such as Mulchén, Yumbel, Negrete and Los Ángeles account for much of its vineyard. The landscape, greener than that of the central valleys, alternates crops, pasture and forestry plantations, and vines cover a modest area compared with the country’s major regions.

History

Vines reached this area with the Spanish colonists, and for centuries the vineyard was devoted mainly to the País variety and to Muscats, dry-farmed by smallholders to produce wines for local consumption. From the late twentieth century several wineries from the central regions, seeking cooler climates, began planting varieties such as Pinot Noir, Chardonnay, Riesling, Gewürztraminer and Sauvignon Blanc here. At the same time, renewed interest in old vines has led to the revival of old plots of País and Muscat.

Climate

Bío-Bío is cooler and wetter than the Central Valley. Rainfall, concentrated in autumn and winter, is much higher, and the growing season is shorter, with a greater risk of spring frost and of rain at harvest. On the other hand, moderate temperatures and a wide diurnal range favour wines with marked acidity and clear-cut aromas. Differences in altitude and distance from the sea create contrasts between sectors.

Soils

The soils are mainly of volcanic and alluvial origin: black volcanic sands, gravel deposits carried by the rivers and, in some areas, red clays of granitic origin. They tend to be deep and retain water well, which allows many plots to be farmed without irrigation.

Grapes and styles

Cool-climate varieties give some of their best Chilean results here. Pinot Noir produces light, fragrant reds, and Chardonnay taut whites that also serve as a base for sparkling wine. Riesling and Gewürztraminer give aromatic whites, dry or slightly sweet, and Sauvignon Blanc fresh, herbaceous wines. The old País and Muscat vines contribute rustic, light reds and perfumed whites. Altogether, the valley offers a fresher and less familiar face of Chilean wine.
