Champagne

Champagne, north-east of Paris, is the region that made sparkling wines with a second fermentation in bottle famous around the world. Its wines are based on Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Meunier, grown over a chalk subsoil.

History

For centuries Champagne produced mainly still wines, appreciated at the French court. Effervescence arose spontaneously when fermentation, halted by the winter cold, resumed in spring, and for a long time it was considered a fault. Dom Pérignon, a Benedictine monk at the abbey of Hautvillers who died in 1715, is traditionally credited with inventing Champagne, but his main contribution is now thought to have been improving blending and winemaking. Sparkling Champagne developed in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, thanks to stronger bottles and the founding of trading houses in Reims and Épernay. In the early twentieth century, disputes over the boundaries of the area led to the riots of 1911; the area was delimited in 1927 and recognised as an AOC in 1936. In 2015 the Champagne hillsides, houses and cellars were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

Territory, climate and soils

The main areas are the Montagne de Reims, the Vallée de la Marne, the Côte des Blancs and the Côte de Sézanne, in the Marne département, and the Côte des Bar, in the Aube. The climate, at the northern limit of vine growing, combines oceanic and continental influences, with low average temperatures and a risk of frost. The grapes ripen with difficulty and retain high acidity, ideal for sparkling wine. Much of the Marne vineyard lies over a chalk subsoil, which drains well and retains moisture; in the Côte des Bar, Kimmeridgian marl predominates. The old chalk quarries, the crayères, are used as cellars.

Grapes

The three main varieties are Pinot Noir, which brings body and structure; Meunier, which gives fruity wines and is widely grown in the Vallée de la Marne; and Chardonnay, dominant in the Côte des Blancs, which contributes finesse and freshness. A blanc de blancs is made solely from white grapes and a blanc de noirs solely from black ones.

Winemaking

A base wine is made first, often blended with reserve wines from earlier years. After bottling with the liqueur de tirage, a mixture of wine, sugar and yeast, a second fermentation takes place in the bottle, producing carbon dioxide. The wine then rests on its lees: at least 15 months from bottling for non-vintage Champagne, 12 of them on the lees, and at least three years for vintage Champagne. After riddling and disgorgement, which remove the sediment, the liqueur d'expédition is added, and its sugar content determines terms such as brut nature, extra brut, brut, extra dry, sec, demi-sec and doux.

Other wines

The region also produces still wines under the Coteaux Champenois appellation and a still rosé, Rosé des Riceys. Seventeen villages hold grand cru status.

Country
France
Climate
Cool, transitional between oceanic and continental, at the northern limit of viticulture.
Soils
Chalk across much of the Marne; Kimmeridgian marl in the Côte des Bar.
Styles
Traditional-method sparkling wines, vintage and non-vintage, white and rosé, from brut nature to doux; still Coteaux Champenois wines.
Grapes
Pinot Noir, Chardonnay
