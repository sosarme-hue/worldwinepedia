Designation of Origin (DO)

Toro

Toro is a designation of origin in Zamora and Valladolid, on the banks of the Duero, recognised in 1987. Its reds from Tinta de Toro, the local form of Tempranillo, are noted for their deep colour, structure and ripeness.

History

Toro wine has a long-standing reputation. As early as the Middle Ages, the town of Toro and its surroundings were known for their wines, which were sold in other parts of Castile and enjoyed royal privileges. Part of the vineyard area survived phylloxera at the end of the nineteenth century thanks to its sandy soils, in which the insect struggles to thrive, and as a result old ungrafted vines planted on their own roots can still be found. The Designation of Origin was recognised in 1987. Since the 1990s, the arrival of wineries from other regions and the revival of old vineyards have given Toro considerable prominence.

Territory and climate

The designation covers land in the province of Zamora, where most of the vineyards are, and a few municipalities in the west of the province of Valladolid, on both sides of the Duero river. The vineyards lie at roughly 600 to 750 metres above sea level. The climate is continental and dry, with little rainfall, cold winters and very hot summers with many hours of sunshine. These conditions favour full ripening, giving wines that are often high in alcohol, while cooler nights help preserve some freshness.

Soils

Sandy soils predominate, sometimes over clay subsoils that retain water, along with areas of rounded stones on the river terraces. The poverty of these soils keeps yields low.

Grapes and styles

The main variety is Tinta de Toro, a local adaptation of Tempranillo with thick skins and great colouring capacity. It is accompanied by Grenache, used in reds and rosés. White varieties include Malvasía and Verdejo. The most characteristic style is red, with deep colour, ripe black fruit, firm tannins and a full body, suitable both for drinking young and for barrel ageing. Rosés and whites are also produced in smaller quantities.

Ageing

The designation uses the traditional ageing terms: alongside young reds, which may spend a short time in wood, producers make Crianza, Reserva and Gran Reserva wines, which must meet the minimum periods of barrel and bottle ageing laid down in the regulations. Many wineries work with old vines and low yields to make wines for laying down.
