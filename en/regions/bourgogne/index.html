Burgundy

Burgundy, in east-central France, is the home of Pinot Noir and Chardonnay expressed through precisely defined vineyard plots, the climats. Its hierarchy of appellations culminates in the grands crus of the Côte d'Or and Chablis.

History

Vines were already grown in Burgundy in Gallo-Roman times, but it was the medieval monasteries that shaped its viticulture. The Benedictine abbey of Cluny, founded in 910, and the Cistercian abbey of Cîteaux, founded in 1098, acquired vineyards and observed over centuries which plots produced the best wines; the Cistercians created, for example, the Clos de Vougeot. The Valois dukes of Burgundy promoted the quality of their wines, and in 1395 Philip the Bold ordered Gamay to be uprooted in favour of Pinot Noir. The Hospices de Beaune were founded in 1443, and their annual wine auction is held every November. After the French Revolution, Church lands were sold off as national property, and inheritance laws later broke up the estates, so that many vineyards are now shared among numerous owners. This is why domaines, which make wine from their own grapes, coexist with négociants, which buy grapes, must or wine. In 2015 the climats of the Burgundy vineyards were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

Areas

The region stretches from north to south from the Yonne département to the south of Saône-et-Loire. It comprises Chablis and the Grand Auxerrois; the Côte d'Or, divided into the Côte de Nuits and the Côte de Beaune; the Côte Chalonnaise, with Rully, Mercurey, Givry, Montagny and Bouzeron; and the Mâconnais, with appellations such as Pouilly-Fuissé, Saint-Véran and Viré-Clessé. Beaujolais, further south, is often associated with Burgundy, although it has its own identity.

Climate and soils

The climate is semi-continental, with cold winters, warm summers and a risk of spring frost and hail, so that harvests vary greatly from year to year. The soils derive mainly from Jurassic limestone and marl, whose composition and depth change over very short distances along the slopes.

Hierarchy of appellations

The Burgundian system is based on place rather than ownership. At the base are the regional appellations, such as Bourgogne, Bourgogne Aligoté, Coteaux Bourguignons and Crémant de Bourgogne. Above them are the communal or village appellations, such as Meursault or Gevrey-Chambertin; within these, the premiers crus, delimited plots whose name may appear on the label alongside that of the village; and at the top the 33 grands crus, each with its own appellation, such as Chambertin, Musigny, Romanée-Conti, Corton-Charlemagne and Montrachet. Some vineyards belong to a single owner and are known as monopoles.

Grapes and styles

Pinot Noir is the variety of the great reds and Chardonnay that of the great whites. Aligoté has its own regional appellation as well as that of Bouzeron; Gamay is used in some regional wines, and Sauvignon Blanc is grown in Saint-Bris, in the Yonne. Burgundy also produces traditional-method sparkling wines under the Crémant de Bourgogne appellation.
