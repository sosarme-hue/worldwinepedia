Central Valley

Chile’s great wine region, a depression between the Andes and the Coastal Range stretching south of Santiago. It groups the Maipo, Rapel, Curicó and Maule valleys and holds most of the country’s vineyards.

A region that groups valleys

In the Chilean system of appellations, the Central Valley is one of the main wine regions, together with Atacama, Coquimbo, Aconcagua, the Southern region and the Austral region. It occupies the intermediate depression separating the Andes from the Coastal Range, from the outskirts of Santiago southwards, and comprises four subregions: the Maipo Valley, the Rapel Valley, itself divided into Cachapoal and Colchagua, the Curicó Valley and the Maule Valley. Each has its own appellation and zones. It is by far the country’s most important region in terms of vineyard area and production volume.

A broad appellation

When a wine bears the words Central Valley on its label, it means the grapes may come from any of these subregions, and often from several at once. This flexibility allows wineries to blend grapes from different valleys to make wines of consistent style at affordable prices, which is why the appellation appears mainly on high-volume varietal wines intended for export. The more ambitious wines, by contrast, usually state a subregion or a more precise zone, such as Alto Maipo, Apalta or Cauquenes.

History

The Spanish brought vines to Chile in the sixteenth century, and the País variety was for centuries the basis of the Central Valley’s vineyards. In the nineteenth century landowners around Santiago imported French vines such as Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and Carmenère, before phylloxera reached Europe. Chile has never suffered this pest, so many vines are still planted on their own roots. The modernisation of the wineries from the 1980s onwards turned the Central Valley into the engine of the growth of Chilean wine exports.

Climate, soils and styles

The climate is Mediterranean, with dry, sunny summers, rainfall concentrated in winter and a wide diurnal range thanks to cold air flowing down from the Andes at night; areas close to the Coastal Range also receive some marine influence. Irrigation with water from the Andean rivers is common. The soils are mostly alluvial, with gravel, silt and clay, and of volcanic or granitic origin on the slopes. The main varieties are Cabernet Sauvignon, Carmenère, Merlot and Syrah among the reds, and Sauvignon Blanc and Chardonnay among the whites; the wines are usually fruity, ripe and easy to drink.

Country
Chile
Climate
Mediterranean, with dry, sunny summers, winter rain and cool nights from Andean air.

Alluvial (gravel, silt and clay) on the plain, volcanic and granitic on the slopes.
