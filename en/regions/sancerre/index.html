Sancerre

Sancerre, on the left bank of the Loire in the Cher département, is one of the benchmark appellations for Sauvignon Blanc. It also produces reds and rosés from Pinot Noir.

Territory

The appellation takes its name from the town of Sancerre, built on a hill overlooking the Loire, and covers several municipalities in the Cher département in central France. Among its best-known wine villages are Chavignol, Bué, Verdigny, Sury-en-Vaux and Saint-Satur. Across the river, on the right bank, lies Pouilly-sur-Loire, with the Pouilly-Fumé appellation, also devoted to Sauvignon Blanc. Together with Menetou-Salon, Quincy and Reuilly, these appellations make up the Centre-Loire vineyards, geographically closer to Chablis than to the mouth of the Loire.

History

Vines have been grown in the area since the Middle Ages, encouraged by the local abbeys. Before the phylloxera crisis at the end of the nineteenth century, red varieties such as Pinot Noir and Gamay predominated in the Sancerre vineyards. When the vines were replanted, Sauvignon Blanc took on the leading role, and in the twentieth century Sancerre whites grew in popularity, first in the bistros and restaurants of Paris and then on international markets.

Climate and soils

The climate is semi-continental, with cold winters, warm summers and a risk of spring frost. The landscape of hills and valleys offers a wide range of exposures. Three main soil types are distinguished: the terres blanches, Kimmeridgian marl rich in marine fossils, similar to that of Chablis, which gives structured wines; the caillottes, stony, shallow soils over Portlandian limestone, associated with more aromatic and lighter wines; and flint soils, found mainly towards the Loire, associated with taut wines of mineral character.

Grapes and styles

The whites, which account for most of the production, are made exclusively from Sauvignon Blanc. They are usually dry, fresh and aromatic, with notes of citrus, gooseberry, grass or white flowers, and lively acidity. Most are fermented in stainless steel tanks to preserve the fruit, although some producers use barrels or large casks for their single-plot cuvées, which can develop in bottle for several years.

The reds and rosés are made from Pinot Noir. The reds are generally light to medium-bodied, with red fruit and fine tannins, and some producers have worked to give them more structure through plot selection and ageing in wood. Fresh, delicate rosés complete the range.

Food

The area is also known for crottin de Chavignol, a goat's cheese with a protected designation of origin. Pairing this cheese with a white Sancerre is one of the most frequently cited regional matches.
