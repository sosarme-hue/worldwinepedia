Nahe

The Nahe region follows this tributary of the Rhine, which joins it at Bingen, and stands out for an exceptional diversity of soils within a small area. Its Rieslings combine the finesse of the Mosel with a little more body and a marked mineral character.

History

Winegrowing in the Nahe valley is old, as in the rest of the Rhine basin, and Bad Kreuznach, the region’s main town, was for centuries a wine trading centre. For a long time, however, the area’s wines were often sold under the names of other neighbouring regions, and the Nahe did not become a separate wine region until the wine law of 1971. In 1902 the Prussian state created a model wine estate at Niederhausen and Schlossböckelheim, planted on slopes previously covered in scrub and cleared partly with convict labour, which helped to reveal the potential of those vineyards; today that estate, now privately owned, is known as Gut Hermannsberg. Since the late twentieth century several family producers have placed the Nahe among the regions most admired by Riesling lovers in Germany.

Territory and climate

The river rises in the Hunsrück and runs through a winding valley to Bingen. The vines grow on often steep slopes, sheltered to the north by the Hunsrück, which gives a temperate, sunny and relatively dry climate. Among the best-known villages are Monzingen, Schlossböckelheim, Niederhausen, Oberhausen, Norheim, Traisen, Bad Kreuznach and Münster-Sarmsheim. At Traisen rises the Rotenfels, one of the highest rock cliffs in central Europe north of the Alps, at whose foot lies the small Bastei vineyard.

Soils

The geology of the Nahe is highly complex, the result of intense volcanic and tectonic activity. Within a few kilometres there is a succession of porphyries and melaphyres of volcanic origin, slates, quartzites, sandstones, conglomerates, loess and clays. This variety explains the marked differences in character between neighbouring vineyards, such as the Hermannshöhle at Niederhausen or the Felsenberg at Schlossböckelheim.

Grapes and styles

Riesling is the most important variety and gives wines of great precision, with stone fruit, citrus, floral notes and a strong mineral accent; they are made both dry and with sweetness, and in great years nobly sweet wines and Eiswein. Müller-Thurgau, Dornfelder, Silvaner, Weissburgunder and Grauburgunder are also grown. As in other German regions, dry wines from individual vineyards have gained ground in recent decades, without the traditional styles with residual sugar disappearing. The region produces a relatively small quantity of wine, but its reputation among German wines is high.
