Wine-producing province

Salta

The province of Salta, in north-west Argentina, is home in the Calchaquí Valleys to some of the highest vineyards in the world. Cafayate is the cradle of Torrontés and also produces intense reds from Malbec and Cabernet Sauvignon.

History

Viticulture reached the valleys of north-west Argentina in colonial times, brought by missionaries and by the estates that supplied the trade routes to Upper Peru. In the nineteenth century wineries were founded that still exist, and Cafayate became the province’s main wine centre. For a long time its wines were drunk mainly in the north of the country, but since the late twentieth century Salta’s Torrontés and its high-altitude reds have gained international recognition.

Geography and climate

Most of the vineyards lie in the Calchaquí Valleys, a succession of valleys following the course of the Calchaquí River between arid mountains and colourful rock formations. Vineyards range roughly from 1,700 metres, around Cafayate, to more than 3,000 metres on the highest estates, near Molinos and Cachi. Despite the low latitude, altitude brings cold nights and very marked diurnal temperature ranges. The climate is dry and the very intense solar radiation encourages thick skins, deep colour and firm tannins in the reds. The scant rainfall is concentrated in summer, and irrigation water comes from mountain rivers and streams.

Soils

The soils are mostly sandy and stony, of alluvial and colluvial origin, poor in organic matter and very well drained, which limits the vigour of the vines.

Zones

Cafayate, at the southern end of the valleys, accounts for most of the vineyard area and wineries. Further north lie San Carlos, Molinos, with very high-altitude estates such as those of Colomé, and Cachi. The Calchaquí Valleys also extend into the neighbouring provinces of Tucumán and Catamarca.

Grapes and styles

Torrontés Riojano, a natural cross between Muscat of Alexandria and the Criolla Chica grape, gives very aromatic whites here, with notes of white flowers, rose, citrus and stone fruit, generally dry and made without oak. Among the reds, Malbec and Cabernet Sauvignon stand out, producing wines of great colour, concentration and structure, along with, to a lesser extent, Tannat, which also has its own tradition here.

Landscape and wine tourism

The Calchaquí Valleys Wine Route, which links Cafayate with Molinos and Cachi, crosses landscapes of great beauty, such as the Quebrada de las Conchas, and brings together family wineries with long traditions and recent projects. Tourism has become an important complementary activity for many producers in the area.
