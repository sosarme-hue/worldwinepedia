Itata Valley

The Itata Valley, in Chile’s Southern Region, is one of the cradles of the country’s viticulture. Its old dry-farmed vineyards of País, Cinsault and Muscat of Alexandria, worked by smallholders, have been rediscovered over the last two decades.

History

Spanish conquerors and missionaries planted vines around Concepción and Chillán as early as the sixteenth century, and during colonial times Itata was one of Chile’s main producing areas, with wines shipped by sea to other parts of the viceroyalty. With the development of the great Central Valley estates in the nineteenth century, the region lost prominence and remained in the hands of small farmers who went on growing vines in the traditional way and selling their wine or grapes in bulk. This marginalisation preserved an exceptional heritage of old vines, many of them over a century old and planted on their own roots. Since the early twenty-first century, independent winemakers and well-known estates have begun working with these growers to make wines that express the tradition of the south.

Geography and climate

The valley follows the course of the Itata River and its tributaries, such as the Ñuble, to its mouth on the Pacific, north of Concepción. The climate is Mediterranean with greater oceanic influence than in the centre of the country: rainfall is more abundant, especially in winter, and temperatures are more moderate. Most of the vineyards are dry-farmed, without irrigation, on the gentle hills of the Coastal Range, often as bush vines worked by hand or with horses.

Soils

Decomposed granite soils predominate, sandy and poor, light-coloured or reddish, with good drainage. In some areas near the Andes there are also soils of volcanic origin.

Grapes and styles

País, the historic grape, gives light, fresh reds with red fruit and rustic tannins. Cinsault, widely planted in the area, produces aromatic, floral reds of light to medium body, which have become one of the hallmarks of the new Itata. Among whites, Muscat of Alexandria gives very aromatic wines, sometimes made with skin contact, and there is also Sémillon and Corinto. Many producers use traditional methods and old vessels, such as clay tinajas, alongside wooden and concrete vats.

Challenges

As in Maule, many Itata growers are elderly smallholders, and the expansion of pine and eucalyptus forestry plantations has reduced the vineyard area. Forest fires in recent years have also affected the area. The growing appreciation of its wines is seen as key to keeping this winegrowing landscape alive.
