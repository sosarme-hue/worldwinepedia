Maule Valley

The Maule Valley, at the southern end of the Central Valley, is the area with the largest vineyard surface in Chile. Alongside large-scale production, it retains old dry-farmed Carignan and País vines that have given rise to some of the country’s most original wines.

History

Maule is one of Chile’s oldest wine areas. During colonial times and much of the nineteenth century the País grape was planted above all, grown as bush vines without irrigation on the hills of the so-called dry interior. After the 1939 earthquake, which destroyed many vineyards, Carignan was planted more widely to add colour and acidity to the wines. During the twentieth century the region was synonymous with bulk wine and large volumes, but since the early twenty-first century small producers and well-known estates have recovered the old vines and used them to make quality wines. A group of producers created the VIGNO association, which brings together Carignan-based reds from old dry-farmed vineyards in Maule.

Geography and climate

The valley is organised around the Maule River and its tributaries, such as the Claro, the Loncomilla and the Tutuvén, with the city of Talca as its main centre. The climate is Mediterranean, slightly cooler and wetter than in the valleys further north, with wet winters and dry summers. Two areas are distinguished: the irrigated sector of the central valley, flat and productive, and the dry interior (secano interior), in the hills of the Coastal Range, where the vines depend solely on rainfall. To the east, in the Andean foothills, night-time temperatures are cooler.

Soils

In the central valley, deep and fertile alluvial and volcanic soils dominate. In the dry interior, granitic soils of decomposed granite predominate, poor and with little water retention, well suited to low-yielding old vines.

Grapes and styles

In the irrigated sector, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Carmenère, Sauvignon Blanc and Chardonnay are grown, with a large share going into high-volume wines. In the dry interior, old-vine Carignan gives deeply coloured reds with red and black fruit, lively acidity and good structure, while País offers light, rustic and fresh wines, often made using traditional methods. There are also old vineyards of Cinsault, Malbec and Muscat. These dry-farmed wines have helped renew the image of Chilean wine.

Growers and heritage

A considerable part of the dry-farmed vineyard area belongs to smallholders who for generations sold their grapes to large companies. The growing value placed on old vines has opened up new opportunities for them, although rural depopulation and the replacement of vineyards with forestry plantations remain threats to this viticultural heritage.
