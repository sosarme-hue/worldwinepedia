Controlled and Guaranteed Designation of Origin (Asti / Moscato d'Asti DOCG)

Asti

The Asti DOCG, in south-eastern Piedmont, covers the sweet, aromatic wines made from Moscato Bianco: sparkling Asti and the delicate, gently fizzy, low-alcohol Moscato d'Asti.

Territory

The Asti appellation covers a wide group of hills spread across the provinces of Asti, Cuneo and Alessandria, in south-eastern Piedmont. The historic heart of Moscato is the area around Canelli and the nearby villages, as well as the hills bordering the Langhe and Monferrato. It should not be confused with other appellations bearing the town's name, such as the Barbera d'Asti DOCG, devoted to Barbera, which is a separate appellation. The underground cellars of Canelli, dug into the tuff of the hills to keep the wines, are part of the Piedmont vineyard landscape recognised as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2014.

Climate and soils

The climate is continental, with cold winters and warm summers, tempered by the shelter of the Alps and the Apennines. The vineyards occupy slopes that are often steep, on calcareous marls and sandstones that are poor and well drained. These conditions allow Moscato to ripen while keeping its acidity and aromas, which are the essential feature of its wines.

The grape and its history

The variety is Moscato Bianco, also called Moscato di Canelli, one of the oldest and finest members of the Muscat family. Its cultivation in the area has been documented for centuries. In the nineteenth century some Canelli houses began making sweet sparkling wines from Moscato, giving rise to an industry that made the name of Asti famous around the world. The DOC was established in 1967 and the DOCG in 1993, bringing sparkling Asti and Moscato d'Asti together under a single umbrella.

Styles

Asti, traditionally known as Asti Spumante, is a sparkling wine usually made by fermentation in a sealed tank. Fermentation is stopped before all the sugar is converted, so the wine keeps natural sweetness and a moderate level of alcohol. Moscato d'Asti is more delicate: it has a light fizz, with more pressure than a still wine but less than a fully sparkling one, and low alcohol. Both are known for aromas of fresh grapes, peach, sage, acacia blossom and citrus, and are best drunk young. The DOCG also provides for subzone names, such as Canelli, and late-harvest versions. In recent years less sweet versions of Asti have also been authorised, broadening a range that has long been associated with dessert and celebration.
