Columbia Valley

A large AVA in Washington State, with a small part in Oregon, lying east of the Cascades in a semi-arid climate with sunny summers. It contains most of Washington’s vineyards.

History

The first vineyards in Washington State were planted in the nineteenth century, and after Prohibition production focused for decades on table grapes, juice and wines from American varieties such as Concord. Quality-oriented Vitis vinifera viticulture took off in the 1960s and 1970s, with companies such as the one that would become Chateau Ste. Michelle and with small pioneering wineries in Walla Walla and Yakima. Yakima Valley was the state’s first AVA, and shortly afterwards the large Columbia Valley AVA was recognised, encompassing most of the others. Since the 1990s the number of wineries has grown rapidly, and Washington has established itself as the country’s second-largest wine-producing state, after California. Many wineries are based near Seattle, west of the Cascades, where they make wine from grapes trucked in from the east of the state.

Climate and soils

The Cascade Range blocks Pacific storms and leaves the east of the state in a rain shadow: the climate is semi-arid, with very little rain, hot, dry summers and cold winters. The northerly latitude gives very long summer days, and cool nights help preserve acidity. Irrigation, drawn from the Columbia River and its tributaries such as the Yakima and the Snake, is essential. The main hazard is severe winter freezes, which can damage the vines. The sandy, silty soils derive largely from loess and from sediments deposited by the Missoula floods at the end of the last Ice Age; they are low in organic matter and well drained. Because phylloxera has barely established itself in these soils, many vines are grown on their own roots.

Sub-regions

The AVA contains numerous sub-zones, among them Yakima Valley, Walla Walla Valley (shared with Oregon), Red Mountain, Horse Heaven Hills, Wahluke Slope, Rattlesnake Hills, Snipes Mountain, Ancient Lakes and Lake Chelan. Red Mountain, one of the smallest, is known for powerful Cabernet Sauvignon reds.

Styles

Washington offers reds with ripe fruit yet marked acidity, above all from Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and Syrah, as well as Bordeaux-style blends. Among the whites, Riesling stands out, in styles from dry to sweet, along with Chardonnay. Cabernet Franc, Sauvignon Blanc and, to a lesser extent, other varieties are also grown.
