Anjou

Anjou, around the city of Angers, is one of the most varied areas of the Loire: it produces dry and sweet Chenin Blanc whites, Cabernet Franc reds and a large quantity of rosé, including Rosé d'Anjou and Cabernet d'Anjou.

Territory

Anjou corresponds to the former province of the same name, in the Maine-et-Loire department, with Angers as its capital. Its vineyards are spread on both sides of the Loire and along its tributaries, such as the Layon, the Aubance and the Loir. To the east it borders Saumur, often grouped with Anjou under the name Anjou-Saumur, and to the west the Pays Nantais. Geologically two zones are distinguished: Anjou noir, to the west, on the edge of the Armorican massif, with schist and dark volcanic and metamorphic rocks, and Anjou blanc, to the east, where the Paris basin begins and limestone and the chalky tuffeau appear.

Appellations

The Anjou AOC covers whites, reds and sparkling wines; Anjou Villages and Anjou Villages Brissac are reserved for more structured reds. Among the rosés, Rosé d'Anjou, medium-sweet and made mainly from Grolleau, and Cabernet d'Anjou, also with residual sugar and based on the two Cabernets, account for very large volumes; Rosé de Loire is the dry version. For sweet Chenin whites, the leading names are Coteaux du Layon, Bonnezeaux, Quarts de Chaume, the only Loire appellation with the Grand Cru designation, and Coteaux de l'Aubance. For dry whites, the most highly regarded appellation is Savennières.

Climate

The climate is temperate with an oceanic influence, relatively dry for the region thanks to the shelter of the Vendée hills to the west. In the Layon and Aubance valleys, autumn mists followed by sunny days favour the development of noble rot, Botrytis cinerea, which concentrates the sugars in the grapes.

Grapes

Chenin Blanc is the great white variety, capable of giving very taut dry wines and long-lived sweet ones; Chardonnay and Sauvignon Blanc play a secondary role. Among the reds, Cabernet Franc is the main variety, followed by Cabernet Sauvignon, Grolleau, Gamay and Pineau d'Aunis.

Styles and trends

For much of the twentieth century Anjou was known mainly for its medium-sweet rosés. Since the end of that century many growers have focused on dry Chenin whites from schist, more ambitious Cabernet Franc reds and the great sweet wines of the Layon, and the area has a notable proportion of organically farmed vineyard.
