Jura

The Jura, between Burgundy and Switzerland, is a small wine region with a strong personality. It is known for vin jaune, a Savagnin white aged under a veil of yeast, and for its light Poulsard and Trousseau reds.

Location

The Jura vineyard occupies a narrow strip of hills in the department of the same name, in eastern France, at the foot of the first slopes of the Jura mountains, which separate it from Switzerland. To the west lies the Saône plain, which separates it from Burgundy. It runs north to south for some eighty kilometres, from around Salins-les-Bains and Arbois to south of Lons-le-Saunier. It is one of the smallest wine regions in France.

History

Viticulture in the area is very old. The Jura is linked to Louis Pasteur, born in Dole and raised in Arbois, who carried out part of his studies on fermentation in his family vineyard. Arbois was one of the first appellations of origin recognised in France, in 1936.

Appellations

There are four geographical appellations: Arbois, the largest, with the designation Arbois-Pupillin for the village of Pupillin; Côtes du Jura, which covers most of the region; L'Étoile, small and devoted to whites; and Château-Chalon, reserved exclusively for vin jaune. To these are added three product appellations: Crémant du Jura, a traditional-method sparkling wine; Macvin du Jura, a liqueur wine made from grape must and pomace brandy from the region; and Marc du Jura, for the brandy.

Climate and soils

The climate is continental, with cold and sometimes snowy winters, warm summers and abundant rainfall. The soils are mainly Triassic and Jurassic marls, grey, bluish or reddish in colour, and limestone. The cool, clay-rich marls are very well suited to Savagnin.

Grapes

Five main varieties are grown. Among the whites, Chardonnay, the most widely planted, and Savagnin, the region's emblematic variety. Among the reds, Poulsard, also called Ploussard, thin-skinned and very pale in colour; Trousseau, more structured; and Pinot Noir.

Styles

Vin jaune is made from Savagnin and aged in barrels that are not topped up for at least six years and three months from the harvest. During this time a veil of yeast forms on the surface of the wine, protecting it from excessive oxidation and giving it characteristic aromas of walnut, curry and spice. It is bottled in the clavelin, a 62-centilitre bottle. Whites can also be made in the ouillé style, with topped-up barrels to prevent the veil, or in the traditional oxidative style. The region also produces vin de paille, a sweet wine from dried grapes, and light, pale reds with aromas of red berries and spice.
