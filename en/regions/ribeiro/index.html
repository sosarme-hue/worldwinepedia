Ribeiro

Ribeiro, in the province of Ourense, is one of the oldest designations of origin in Galicia and in Spain. Its wines, mainly Treixadura whites, were already exported in the Middle Ages, and it preserves the traditional sweet wine known as Tostado.

History

Ribeiro is one of the wine areas with the longest tradition in Galicia. In the Middle Ages monasteries, including the Cistercian houses of San Clodio and Oseira, developed the vineyards, and the town of Ribadavia became an important trading centre. Ribeiro wines were shipped to other European ports, particularly to England, and gained a considerable reputation. After the crisis caused by powdery mildew and phylloxera in the nineteenth century, many vineyards were replanted with productive varieties such as Palomino and Garnacha Tintorera. Since the late twentieth century the area has recovered its native varieties. It is one of the oldest designations of origin in Spain.

Territory and climate

The designation lies in the west of the province of Ourense, where the Miño river meets its tributaries the Avia, Arnoia and Barbantiño, around Ribadavia, Leiro, Castrelo de Miño and other municipalities. The vineyards are planted on slopes and terraces in the valleys, known locally as socalcos. The climate is transitional between Atlantic and continental: warmer and less rainy than the Galician coast but with enough humidity, with mild to warm summers and a marked difference between day and night temperatures during ripening.

Soils

Granite-derived soils predominate: sandy, acidic and shallow, known in Galicia as xabre, together with areas of schist and alluvial deposits on the terraces near the rivers.

Grapes and styles

Treixadura is the main white variety, contributing ripe fruit aromas, floral notes and good structure. It is usually blended with other native varieties such as Torrontés, Loureira, Godello, Albariño, Lado and Caíño Blanco, which makes Ribeiro a land of blends. Red varieties include Caíño Tinto, Sousón, Brancellao, Ferrón and Mencía. Production focuses on fresh, aromatic whites, although light, fresh reds are also made. In recent decades many growers have replaced Palomino, still found in old vineyards, with these native varieties and have brought abandoned hillside plots back into cultivation.

Tostado

Tostado is a traditional sweet wine made from grapes left to dry after harvest, often hung or spread out in dry, well-ventilated places, until their sugars are concentrated. The very dense must ferments slowly, and the wine is aged for a long time before release. It is a niche style but one deeply rooted in the history of the area.
