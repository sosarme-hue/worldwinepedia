Villány

Hungary’s southernmost wine region, near the Croatian border, is the warmest in the country and is devoted mainly to full-bodied reds from Bordeaux varieties, especially Cabernet Franc.

Villány lies in Baranya county, in the far south of Hungary, around the Villány hills, a small limestone range that rises above the plain and shelters the vineyards from northerly winds. Its main wine villages are Villány, Villánykövesd and Palkonya, together with the Siklós area, which has traditionally focused more on white wines.

History

Vines were already grown in Roman times, and after the wars with the Ottomans the region was resettled in the eighteenth century by German-speaking colonists, the Danube Swabians, who helped shape the local wine tradition. The cellars dug or built in rows along the streets of Villánykövesd and Palkonya bear witness to that period. After the socialist era, in the 1990s, a group of grower families began bottling quality wines under their own names, and Villány became one of the country’s most highly regarded red wine regions. It was also one of the first areas in Hungary to organise a wine route.

Climate and soils

It is the warmest region in Hungary, with a continental climate with sub-Mediterranean influence, hot summers, many hours of sunshine and winters that are relatively mild for the country. The south-facing slopes of the Villány hills combine loess, red clays and limestone fragments, which favour good ripening and structured reds.

Grapes and styles

Traditionally the main grapes were Kékoportó (Portugieser), which gave light young reds, and Kékfrankos (Blaufränkisch). Since the late twentieth century the Bordeaux varieties have come to the fore: Cabernet Franc, Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot, on their own or blended, often aged in barrel. Cabernet Franc is now regarded as the region’s emblematic variety, and the appellation has created the Villányi Franc category, reserved for wines from this grape made under stricter rules on yield and ageing. Villány’s rules also distinguish between classic wines, in a fruitier style, and premium or higher-tier wines. Rosés are also made and, above all in Siklós, whites from Olaszrizling (Welschriesling), Chardonnay and other varieties.

The most ambitious reds are usually aged in oak barrels for an extended period and are made both as varietals and as Bordeaux-style blends, often with Cabernet Franc as their backbone. The region’s small size and the concentration of family wineries in a handful of villages have also made it one of Hungary’s leading wine tourism destinations.
