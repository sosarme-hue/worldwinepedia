Qualified Designation of Origin (DOCa)

Rioja

Rioja, in the Ebro valley, became Spain's first Qualified Designation of Origin in 1991. It is best known for its Tempranillo-based reds and its long tradition of ageing in oak barrels.

History

Vine growing in Rioja dates back to Roman times and is well documented in the Middle Ages, when the area's monasteries helped to sustain and spread viticulture. In the nineteenth century, figures such as Luciano de Murrieta and Camilo Hurtado de Amézaga, Marquis of Riscal, introduced winemaking and ageing techniques inspired by Bordeaux. The arrival of phylloxera in France in the second half of that century attracted French merchants and encouraged the growth of wineries, particularly around the railway station in Haro. The designation of origin was recognised in 1925, and in 1926 its Regulatory Council was set up, the first in Spain. In 1991 Rioja became the country's first Qualified Designation of Origin.

Territory and climate

The designation stretches along the Ebro valley through La Rioja, Álava and Navarra, sheltered to the north by the Sierra de Cantabria and to the south by the ranges of the Iberian System. It is divided into three zones: Rioja Alta, Rioja Alavesa and Rioja Oriental, known as Rioja Baja until 2018. The first two, further west and higher up, receive more Atlantic influence, with moderate temperatures and more rain; Rioja Oriental is warmer and drier, with a Mediterranean character. The tributaries of the Ebro, such as the Oja, Najerilla, Iregua, Leza, Cidacos and Alhama, form valleys with their own microclimates.

Soils

Three main soil types predominate: calcareous clays, common in Rioja Alavesa and parts of Rioja Alta; reddish ferrous clays; and alluvial soils near the rivers, with plenty of rounded stones.

Grapes and styles

Tempranillo is the main variety, bringing structure and ageing potential. It is complemented by Grenache, more widespread in Rioja Oriental; Graciano, valued for its acidity and colour; Mazuelo, the local name for Carignan; and Maturana Tinta. The leading white grapes are Viura (Macabeo), Malvasía, White Grenache and Tempranillo Blanco, and others such as Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc and Verdejo are also permitted. Red, white and rosé wines are made, and in recent years sparkling wines as well.

Ageing and classifications

Rioja is known for its tradition of ageing in 225-litre oak barrels. Red Crianza wines must be aged for at least two years, one of them in barrel; Reserva wines for three years, with a minimum of one in barrel; and Gran Reserva wines for five years, with at least two in barrel and two in bottle. Alongside these terms, since 2017 the regulations have recognised zone wines, village wines and Viñedos Singulares (single vineyards), which emphasise geographical origin rather than length of ageing.

Country
Spain
Climate
A transition between the Atlantic influence of Rioja Alta and Alavesa and the warmer, drier Mediterranean character of Rioja Oriental.
Soils
Calcareous clays, ferrous clays and alluvial soils with rounded stones.
Styles
Young and oak-aged reds (Crianza, Reserva, Gran Reserva), as well as whites, rosés and sparkling wines.
