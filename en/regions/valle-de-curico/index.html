Curicó Valley

A subregion of Chile’s Central Valley, between Colchagua and Maule, watered by the Teno and Lontué rivers. It is one of the country’s most productive areas, with a wide diversity of white and red varieties.

Territory

The Curicó Valley lies some two hundred kilometres south of Santiago, in the Maule region, and forms part of the large Central Valley wine region, between the Colchagua Valley to the north and the Maule Valley to the south. Its hub is the city of Curicó, and it is divided into two main zones that follow the course of its rivers: the Teno Valley in the north and the Lontué Valley in the south, with wine towns such as Molina, Sagrada Familia and Lontué. As in other Chilean valleys, the vineyards stretch from the Andean foothills in the east to the Coastal Range in the west, and in recent years vineyards have been planted both in the foothill sectors and in areas closer to the sea, such as Licantén.

History

Curicó has a long winegrowing tradition, with large areas of vineyard long devoted to everyday wines. In the late 1970s the arrival of the Spanish winery Miguel Torres, which introduced techniques such as temperature-controlled fermentation in stainless steel tanks, is regarded as a milestone in the modernisation of Chilean wine. Today the valley is home to large companies, cooperatives and medium-sized wineries, and a significant share of the grapes goes into wines sold under the broader Central Valley appellation.

Climate and soils

The climate is Mediterranean, with warm, dry summers, rainy winters and a marked difference between day and night temperatures, accentuated by cold air descending from the Andes. Rainfall is somewhat higher than in the valleys further north. The soils are mainly alluvial and of volcanic origin, loamy and clay-based in the centre of the valley and stonier on the river terraces and slopes.

Grapes and styles

Curicó is one of Chile’s most diverse valleys. Among the reds, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and Carmenère dominate, together with Syrah and Malbec, giving fruity wines of medium to full body. Among the whites, Sauvignon Blanc is very important and Chardonnay also covers a significant area. In the unirrigated western lands old País vines survive, which some producers have revived to make light, fresh reds, and sparkling wines are also made. The valley thus offers both large-volume, good-value wines and more ambitious single-vineyard labels.
