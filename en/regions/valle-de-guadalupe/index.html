Wine valley

Valle de Guadalupe

The Valle de Guadalupe, in the municipality of Ensenada (Baja California), is Mexico’s best-known wine region. With a semi-arid Mediterranean climate and granitic soils, it produces mainly ripe blended reds and has become a leading destination for wine tourism and gastronomy.

History

The vine reached the area with the missionaries: at the beginning of the nineteenth century the Dominicans founded the mission of Nuestra Señora de Guadalupe del Norte in the valley, the last of the peninsula’s missions, which gave the place its name. After it was abandoned, the valley remained sparsely populated until, in the early twentieth century, Russian families from the Molokan religious community settled there, growing wheat and vines; their heritage can still be seen in the village of Francisco Zarco. In the following decades large wine companies set up in the region, such as L.A. Cetto, founded by Italian immigrants, and for years much of the fruit went into brandy.

At the end of the 1980s a new stage began with the appearance of smaller wineries focused on quality wine, among them Monte Xanic. In the 1990s winemakers such as Hugo D’Acosta, founder of Casa de Piedra, drove a generation of personal projects and the training of new producers. Since then the number of wineries has grown rapidly, along with restaurants and accommodation, and the so-called Baja California Wine Route attracts a growing number of visitors. Tourist and urban growth and water use have also opened a debate on preserving the valley’s agricultural landscape.

Climate

The climate is semi-arid Mediterranean, with mild winters that bring scant rainfall and dry, warm, very sunny summers. The nearby Pacific Ocean, its waters cooled by the California Current, brings breezes and morning fogs that moderate temperatures and cool the nights. The main constraint is water: aquifers are limited and, in some places, slightly saline, and many vineyards are drip-irrigated. Some vineyards are dry-farmed.

Soils

Sandy and sandy-clay soils derived from the decomposed granite of the surrounding ranges predominate; they are poor, loose and well drained. On the valley floor, along the Guadalupe creek bed, alluvial deposits and deeper soils are common, while on the slopes the soils are stonier.

Areas

The valley runs north-east to south-west, between the village of Francisco Zarco and the El Porvenir area, and continues south through San Antonio de las Minas towards Ensenada. Other nearby wine valleys of Baja California, such as Santo Tomás, San Vicente and Ojos Negros, are usually considered together with it as part of the Ensenada wine region.

Varieties and styles

The valley produces mainly red wine, often as blends of several varieties. Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Tempranillo, Syrah, Grenache, Carignan, Petite Sirah, Malbec and Zinfandel are grown, and Nebbiolo, introduced by Italian immigrants, has become one of its most characteristic varieties. The reds tend to be ripe, with intense fruit and full body, often with a saline streak. Among whites, Chenin Blanc, Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay and Colombard stand out. There is no regulated designation of origin: wineries state the valley of origin on the label.
