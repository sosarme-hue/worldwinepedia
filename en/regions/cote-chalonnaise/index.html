Côte Chalonnaise

The Côte Chalonnaise extends the Côte de Beaune southwards, west of Chalon-sur-Saône. Its five village appellations, Bouzeron, Rully, Mercurey, Givry and Montagny, offer Burgundian-style Pinot Noir, Chardonnay and Aligoté at more moderate prices.

Location

The Côte Chalonnaise lies in the Saône-et-Loire department, south of Santenay and the Côte de Beaune, and takes its name from the town of Chalon-sur-Saône, for centuries the river port from which its wines were traded. Unlike the Côte d'Or, it does not form a continuous escarpment: the vineyards are spread across hills and valleys separated by woods, meadows and other crops, giving the landscape a more scattered appearance.

Appellations

The area has the regional Bourgogne Côte Chalonnaise AOC and five village appellations, from north to south. Bouzeron is the only village appellation in Burgundy devoted to Aligoté, which here gives whites of greater depth than elsewhere in the region. Rully produces whites and reds, with the former predominating, and is also an important centre of Crémant de Bourgogne production. Mercurey, the largest, is known above all for its firm, fruity Pinot Noir reds. Givry is also mainly red and, according to a much-repeated tradition, its wines were among the favourites of King Henri IV. Montagny, at the southern end, produces only Chardonnay whites. Rully, Mercurey, Givry and Montagny have vineyards classified as premier cru; there are no grands crus in the area.

Climate and soils

The climate is moderately continental, with cold winters, warm summers and a risk of frost and hail similar to that of the Côte d'Or, although the vineyards, somewhat higher and more scattered, sometimes ripen a little later. The soils are Jurassic limestone and marl, with clay on the lower slopes, in geological continuity with the Côte de Beaune.

Grapes and styles

Pinot Noir gives reds of medium colour, with aromas of cherry and raspberry and a structure somewhat more rustic and less dense than that of the great villages of the Côte d'Or. Chardonnay produces fresh, mineral whites, sometimes aged in barrel, and the Aligoté of Bouzeron stands out for its liveliness and its citrus and floral notes. Crémant de Bourgogne, made by the traditional method, has a notable presence here.

Appeal

For many wine lovers, the Côte Chalonnaise is a more affordable way to discover the Burgundian style. Some Côte d'Or houses own vineyards or buy grapes in the area, and a new generation of local producers has helped to improve its reputation.
