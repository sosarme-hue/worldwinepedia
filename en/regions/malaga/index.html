Designation of Origin (DO)

Málaga

Málaga is a historic designation of origin devoted mainly to sweet wines from Moscatel and Pedro Ximénez, ranging from naturally sweet wines to long-aged liqueur wines. It shares a regulatory council with the Sierras de Málaga DO, which covers dry still wines.

History

Málaga's viticulture dates back to the Phoenician presence on the coast and continued in Roman and Al-Andalus times, when grapes were also used to make raisins. Between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries Málaga wine, often called Mountain in England, enjoyed great fame in the markets of northern Europe and the Americas. In 1878 phylloxera was detected in Málaga, the first outbreak of the pest in Spain, devastating much of the vineyard and causing a long decline. Málaga is one of the oldest designations of origin in Spain. In 2001 the Sierras de Málaga DO was created to cover the province's still, non-sweet wines; both share a regulatory council with the designation for Pasas de Málaga raisins.

Territory and climate

The production area covers several districts of the province: the Axarquía, to the east, with Moscatel vineyards on very steep slate slopes; the Montes de Málaga, north of the city; the Norte zone, around Antequera, with a more continental climate; the Costa Occidental, around Manilva; and the Serranía de Ronda, where mainly Sierras de Málaga wines are made. The climate ranges from the mild Mediterranean of the coast to the continental climate of the inland uplands.

Soils

Slate soils, poor and steep, predominate in the Axarquía and the Montes de Málaga, while the Norte zone has mostly deeper limestone and clay soils.

Grapes

The main varieties are Muscat of Alexandria, Moscatel de grano menudo (Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains) and Pedro Ximénez. Other traditional white grapes, such as Doradilla and Lairén, are also permitted, and in the Sierras de Málaga DO various red varieties, such as Romé, Tempranillo, Grenache, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and Syrah.

Types of wine

The designation covers naturally sweet wines, made without added alcohol, often from sun-dried grapes; vinos dulces naturales and liqueur wines, to which grape spirit is added; and some dry wines. Liqueur wines are also classified by colour, from white and golden to dark and black, partly through the addition of arrope, a cooked, concentrated must. The ageing terms are Málaga; Málaga Noble, two to three years; Málaga Añejo, three to five years; and Málaga Trasañejo, more than five years. Other traditional terms are Lágrima, made from must that runs off without mechanical pressing, and Pajarete.
