Wine region (Harei Yehuda, the Judean Hills)

Judea (Judean Hills)

A region of limestone hills around Jerusalem, one of Israel’s five main wine regions. Its high-altitude vineyards, on land with a winegrowing history going back millennia, give Bordeaux-style and Mediterranean reds and fresh whites.

Territory

In Israel’s winegrowing division, which distinguishes the regions of Galilee, Shomron, Shimshon (Samson), the Judean Hills and the Negev, the Judean Hills region (Harei Yehuda) comprises the heights surrounding Jerusalem, mainly to the west and south-west of the city, towards the coastal plain. It is a landscape of hills and narrow valleys, with pine forests, stone terraces, olive groves and small vineyard plots, often at altitudes of several hundred metres. The lower foothills descending westwards, known as the Shfela or Judean Foothills, are frequently associated with the region in everyday usage, although officially they belong to the Shimshon region.

History

The Judean Hills preserve abundant evidence of very ancient winegrowing: presses cut into the rock, terraces and remains of winemaking installations dating back to antiquity, and wine appears frequently in the biblical texts associated with this land. After centuries of decline, quality winegrowing reappeared in the region in the late twentieth century. One of the pioneering wineries was Domaine du Castel, founded by the Ben Zaken family near Jerusalem, whose Bordeaux-style reds helped bring Israeli wine to international attention. Since then numerous small and medium-sized wineries have been established, many of them in local moshavim and kibbutzim.

Climate and soils

The climate is Mediterranean, with rainfall concentrated in winter and long, warm, dry summers. Altitude moderates the heat: nights are cool even in summer, and the wide difference between day and night temperatures helps preserve acidity and aromas. In winter there can be frost in the highest areas. The soils are mainly limestone, with the characteristic terra rossa, a reddish clay formed by the weathering of limestone rock, and shallow stony soils on the slopes, which ensure good drainage.

Grapes and styles

As in the rest of Israel, international varieties predominate. Among the reds, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Cabernet Franc and Petit Verdot stand out, often in barrel-aged Bordeaux-style blends, alongside Mediterranean varieties such as Syrah and Carignan, prized from old vines. Among the whites, Chardonnay and Sauvignon Blanc give fresh, elegant wines. Most wineries make kosher wines, though not all of them, and many welcome visitors, given the region’s proximity to Jerusalem and to the country’s main cities.
