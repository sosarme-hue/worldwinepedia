Colchagua Valley

The Colchagua Valley, in the southern part of the Rapel Valley, is one of Chile’s best-known red wine areas. Apalta, with its horseshoe-shaped slopes, is its most famous sector.

History

Colchagua is an area with a long tradition of farming and cattle raising, with large estates around towns such as Santa Cruz, San Fernando and Peralillo. Vines have been grown here since colonial times, but the valley’s reputation for quality wine was built mainly from the 1990s, when many Chilean and foreign wineries invested in vineyards and modern facilities. The creation of a wine route, museums and hotels has made Colchagua one of the country’s main wine tourism destinations.

Geography and climate

The valley follows the Tinguiririca River and, together with Cachapoal, forms the Rapel Valley subregion within the Central Valley. It is a valley open to the west, stretching from the Andean foothills to the coast. The climate is Mediterranean, warm inland and in the centre of the valley, with dry, bright summers that favour good ripening of late-ripening red varieties. To the west, in areas such as Marchigüe, Lolol and, on the coast itself, Paredones, Pacific influence lowers temperatures.

Subzones

Apalta, near Santa Cruz, is an amphitheatre of north-facing slopes, sheltered by hills and open towards the Tinguiririca River, which receives plenty of light but benefits from cool afternoon breezes. Some of Chile’s most acclaimed reds are made there. Other notable areas are Los Lingues, in the Andean foothills; Marchigüe and Lolol, in the western sector; and Paredones, by the sea, where cool-climate varieties are grown.

Soils

The soils on the valley floor are alluvial, made up of clay, silt and sand, fertile and deep. On the slopes of Apalta and the Coastal Range, poor, decomposed granitic soils dominate, favouring more concentrated wines.

Grapes and styles

Colchagua is above all red wine country. Carmenère finds one of its finest expressions here, alongside Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah, Malbec and Merlot. The wines tend to be ripe, with generous black fruit and ample body, often barrel-aged, and many of the country’s great blends come from Apalta. In the coastal strip, Sauvignon Blanc and Chardonnay whites and fresher-styled Pinot Noir reds are made.

Wine tourism

Santa Cruz is the centre of the valley’s tourist life, with museums, hotels and wineries open to the public. The Colchagua Valley Wine Route, one of the first in the country, organises visits and activities around the harvest.
