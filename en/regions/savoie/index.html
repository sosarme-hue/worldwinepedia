Savoie

Savoie, in the French Alps, brings together scattered mountain vineyards between Lake Geneva and the Isère valley. It produces mainly fresh whites from Jacquère, Altesse and Roussanne, and Mondeuse reds.

Territory

The Savoie vineyard is spread across the Savoie and Haute-Savoie departments, and small areas of the Isère and the Ain. It does not form a continuous region, but a series of islands on Alpine slopes and valleys: on the shores of Lake Geneva, around Lac du Bourget, in the Rhône valley near Seyssel and, above all, in the Combe de Savoie, the Isère valley south-east of Chambéry. The vineyards generally lie on sunny slopes, between the valley floors and the mountain areas.

Appellations

The main appellation is Vin de Savoie or Savoie, which may be followed by the name of one of its crus, among them Apremont, Abymes, Chignin, Chignin-Bergeron, Arbin, Jongieux, Ripaille, Marignan, Crépy and Marin. Roussette de Savoie is reserved for Altesse whites, and Seyssel produces still and sparkling whites. There is also Crémant de Savoie, a traditional-method sparkling wine. The names Apremont and Abymes recall the great landslide of Mont Granier in 1248, whose debris now forms part of the soil of these vineyards.

Climate and soils

The climate is continental and mountainous, with cold winters, warm summers and a strong influence from the lakes and altitude. The lakes moderate temperatures, and south-facing slopes make the most of the light. The soils are varied: limestone scree at the foot of the mountains, glacial moraines, molasse and alluvial deposits.

Grapes

White varieties dominate. Jacquère is the most widely planted and gives light whites with lively acidity and floral and citrus notes, typical of Apremont and Abymes. Altesse, also called Roussette, produces more aromatic wines capable of ageing. Roussanne, known here as Bergeron, is the basis of Chignin-Bergeron. Chasselas is grown by Lake Geneva, in crus such as Ripaille, Marin and Crépy. Among the reds, Mondeuse Noire stands out, giving deeply coloured wines with marked acidity and notes of black fruit and pepper, especially in Arbin. Gamay and Pinot Noir are also grown.

Styles

Savoie wines are mostly dry whites, fresh and moderate in alcohol, which are drunk young and traditionally accompany Alpine cooking such as fondue and raclette. Mondeuse reds and Altesse and Bergeron whites can have more structure and develop well for several years. In recent decades a group of producers has championed single-plot wines and the revival of little-known local varieties.
