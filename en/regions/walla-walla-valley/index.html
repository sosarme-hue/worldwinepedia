Walla Walla Valley

An AVA in south-eastern Washington State that also extends into north-eastern Oregon. With a dry continental climate, it is known for Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and Syrah reds and for the cobblestone soils of The Rocks District.

Territory

The Walla Walla Valley lies in south-eastern Washington State, around the city of the same name, and its southern part crosses the border into Oregon, near Milton-Freewater. The AVA is part of the large Columbia Valley AVA, which covers much of eastern Washington. To the east rise the Blue Mountains, whose slopes influence the area’s climate and rainfall patterns. Its name comes from an expression of the region’s Native peoples usually interpreted as “many waters”, a reference to the streams flowing down from the mountains.

History

Although there were vineyards in the area in the nineteenth century, modern winegrowing in Walla Walla began in the 1970s and 1980s, when a small group of pioneering wineries such as Leonetti Cellar, Woodward Canyon and L’Ecole No. 41 began making reds that caught the attention of critics. Since then the number of wineries has grown considerably, and the city of Walla Walla has become an important wine tourism destination. Many wineries from other parts of Washington also buy grapes from the valley.

Climate and soils

The climate is continental and dry, with hot, sunny summers, cool nights and cold winters in which severe frosts can damage the vines. Rainfall increases as the land rises towards the Blue Mountains, so some eastern vineyards can be farmed with little irrigation, while in the west irrigation is essential. The soils are largely made up of loess, a fine wind-blown silt, laid down over sediments from the Missoula Floods, the gigantic floods that swept through the region at the end of the last Ice Age. In the south, on the Oregon side, the sub-AVA The Rocks District of Milton-Freewater sits on an ancient alluvial fan covered with basalt cobblestones, which store heat and give the wines a very particular character.

Grapes and styles

Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot are the most widely planted varieties and give reds with ripe fruit, firm tannins and good acidity, often in Bordeaux-style blends with Cabernet Franc and Petit Verdot. Syrah has gained great prestige, especially from The Rocks District, with notes of olive, meat, spice and earth. Malbec and Grenache are also grown, together with small amounts of white varieties such as Chardonnay.
