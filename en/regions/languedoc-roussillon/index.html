Wine region

Languedoc-Roussillon

Languedoc-Roussillon, along the Mediterranean between the Rhône and the Pyrenees, is France's largest wine area. Once known for table wine, it now produces Mediterranean appellation reds, varietal wines under the IGP Pays d'Oc, sparkling wines from Limoux and vins doux naturels.

History

The vine arrived early in this Mediterranean strip, and in Roman times the region was part of Gallia Narbonensis, whose capital was Narbonne. In the nineteenth century the railway made it possible to supply cheap wine to the industrial cities of northern France, and the vineyards spread across the plains with high-yielding varieties such as Aramon. Overproduction and fraud led to the severe crisis of 1907, when a revolt of the Midi winegrowers gave rise to mass demonstrations. For much of the twentieth century the region remained associated with table wine. From the 1970s and 1980s many plains vineyards were uprooted, hillsides were replanted with better varieties, and appellations and the vin de pays category, now IGP, were developed. Administratively, Languedoc-Roussillon has been part of the Occitanie region since 2016.

Climate and soils

The climate is Mediterranean, warm and dry, with abundant sunshine. Winds such as the tramontane, blowing from the north-west, and the mistral in the eastern part dry out the vineyards. Inland, in the foothills of the Massif Central and the Pyrenees, altitude and, in areas such as Limoux, Atlantic influences bring more freshness. The soils are very diverse: schist in Faugères, Saint-Chinian, parts of Corbières and Banyuls; limestone in areas such as La Clape and Pic Saint-Loup; terraces of pebbles, clay and sandstone elsewhere.

Languedoc appellations

The regional Languedoc appellation covers a wide area. Among the best-known appellations are Corbières, Minervois, with its cru Minervois-La Livinière, Fitou, Saint-Chinian, Faugères, Pic Saint-Loup and Terrasses du Larzac, mainly for reds; and Picpoul de Pinet, a white from the Picpoul grape near the Thau lagoon. Limoux produces sparkling wines such as Blanquette de Limoux, based on Mauzac, and Crémant de Limoux, with Chardonnay and Chenin Blanc.

Roussillon and vins doux naturels

Roussillon, in the Pyrénées-Orientales département, produces reds under the Côtes du Roussillon, Côtes du Roussillon Villages and Collioure appellations, and is France's main area for vins doux naturels: Banyuls, Maury, Rivesaltes and Muscat de Rivesaltes. These wines are made by mutage, the addition of grape spirit during fermentation, which stops it and preserves part of the grapes' natural sugar; tradition attributes the technique to Arnau de Vilanova, a thirteenth-century physician. Languedoc also has sweet fortified Muscats, such as Muscat de Frontignan.

Grapes and styles

The reds are based on Grenache, Syrah, Mourvèdre, Carignan and Cinsault; white varieties include White Grenache, Macabeo, Rolle (Vermentino), Picpoul and Muscat. The IGP Pays d'Oc, one of the most important in France, allows varietal wines to be made from Chardonnay, Merlot, Cabernet Sauvignon, Sauvignon Blanc and Viognier, among others.
