Los Carneros

An AVA at the southern end of the Napa and Sonoma valleys, beside San Pablo Bay. Cool and windy, it is a classic area for Pinot Noir, Chardonnay and base wines for sparkling wine.

Territory

Los Carneros, often simply called Carneros, occupies a strip of low, rolling hills north of San Pablo Bay, the northern part of San Francisco Bay. It is one of the few Californian AVAs spanning two counties: its eastern part belongs to Napa and its western part to Sonoma, so it overlaps both the Napa Valley AVA and the Sonoma Valley AVA. Its name, which in Spanish refers to sheep, recalls that for a long time this land was used for grazing and dairy farming.

Climate

Carneros is one of the coolest parts of Napa and Sonoma. The proximity of the bay and the almost constant afternoon winds blowing in from the ocean keep temperatures moderate even in midsummer, and morning fog is frequent. The wind also reduces vine vigour and thickens the grape skins. Rainfall is relatively low, so irrigation is common. These conditions extend the ripening period and allow high acidity to be retained.

Soils

The soils are generally shallow and clay-based, with limited drainage and low fertility, which helps keep yields moderate. Some areas have loamy and alluvial soils, but compact clay is the most characteristic feature.

Grapes and styles

Pinot Noir and Chardonnay dominate the vineyards. Carneros Pinot Noir tends to be medium-bodied, with bright red fruit, spicy notes and fresh acidity, while the Chardonnays combine citrus and apple fruit with good tension. From the late twentieth century several sparkling wine houses arrived in the area, some linked to major Champagne and Cava firms, drawn by the cool climate; as a result a significant share of the grapes goes into base wines for traditional-method sparkling wine. Carneros is also a valued source of fruit for Napa and Sonoma producers who do not own vineyards there, and there are plots of Merlot and Syrah in the somewhat warmer sectors.

One of the first AVAs

Carneros was one of the first AVAs recognised in California, and its boundaries were drawn on the basis of climate and landscape rather than administrative borders. Today it is a reference point for understanding how the maritime influence of the bay shapes the character of the region’s wines.
