Loire

The Loire valley brings together a succession of vineyards along France's longest river, from the Atlantic coast to the centre of the country. It is known for the diversity of its styles, with Melon de Bourgogne, Chenin Blanc, Sauvignon Blanc and Cabernet Franc in the leading roles.

History

Viticulture spread through the Loire valley in Gallo-Roman times and developed in the Middle Ages thanks to the abbeys and to the river itself, which allowed wine to be carried inland and to the Atlantic ports. Dutch merchants played an important part in creating demand for white and sweet wines, and the presence of the royal court in the Loire châteaux during the Renaissance added to the region's prestige. In 2000 the Loire valley between Sully-sur-Loire and Chalonnes-sur-Loire was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as a cultural landscape.

Climate and soils

The climate shifts from mild, humid and oceanic near the Atlantic to semi-continental in central France, with colder winters and a greater risk of frost. Overall it is a cool climate, giving wines with marked acidity. The soils reflect two major geological groups: in the west, the ancient rocks of the Armorican Massif, such as schist, granite and gneiss; further east, the sedimentary rocks of the Paris Basin, in particular the white chalky limestone known as tuffeau, into which many cellars have been dug, as well as clay with flint and the Kimmeridgian marl of Sancerre.

Areas

Four main areas are usually distinguished. The Pays Nantais, near the river mouth, is the home of Muscadet, a dry white from Melon de Bourgogne that is often aged on its lees over the winter and bottled with the term sur lie. Anjou and Saumur produce Chenin Blanc whites, dry as in Savennières or sweet as in Coteaux du Layon, Bonnezeaux and Quarts de Chaume; Cabernet Franc reds such as Saumur-Champigny; rosés such as Rosé d'Anjou and Cabernet d'Anjou; and sparkling wines. Touraine offers the Chenin wines of Vouvray and Montlouis-sur-Loire, in styles ranging from dry to sweet and sparkling, and the Cabernet Franc reds of Chinon, Bourgueil and Saint-Nicolas-de-Bourgueil. The Centre-Loire, further east, is Sauvignon Blanc country, with Sancerre, Pouilly-Fumé, Menetou-Salon, Quincy and Reuilly.

Grapes and styles

Chenin Blanc, known locally as Pineau de la Loire, is the region's most versatile variety, capable of producing long-lived dry, off-dry, sweet and sparkling wines. Sauvignon Blanc dominates in the Centre-Loire and parts of Touraine, and Melon de Bourgogne in the Pays Nantais. Among the reds, Cabernet Franc leads, accompanied by Cabernet Sauvignon, Gamay, Pinot Noir and Côt, the local name for Malbec. Chardonnay is used mainly in sparkling wines such as Crémant de Loire.

Country
France
Climate
Oceanic near the Atlantic and increasingly semi-continental towards the east; cool overall.
Soils
Schist, granite and gneiss of the Armorican Massif in the west; tuffeau, clay with flint and limestone marl in the east.
Styles
Dry, off-dry and sweet whites, light to medium-bodied Cabernet Franc reds, rosés and sparkling wines such as Crémant de Loire.
Grapes
Chenin Blanc, Sauvignon Blanc, Melon de Bourgogne, Cabernet Franc, Cabernet Sauvignon, Gamay, Pinot Noir, Malbec, Chardonnay
