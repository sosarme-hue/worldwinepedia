Sardinia

Sardinia, the second-largest island in the Mediterranean, has a winegrowing tradition of its own, shaped by varieties such as Cannonau, Vermentino and Carignano. Its only DOCG is Vermentino di Gallura.

An island apart

Sardinia lies in the middle of the western Mediterranean, south of Corsica. Its isolation and its history, with Phoenician, Roman, Pisan, Genoese, Aragonese and Piedmontese influences, have produced a winegrowing culture distinct from that of the rest of Italy. Several of its main varieties probably arrived or spread during the long period of rule by the Crown of Aragon and Spain, although the exact origin of some of them is still debated. For much of the twentieth century production focused on bulk wine, and in recent decades many wineries, including cooperatives, have turned to higher-quality bottled wines.

Climate and soils

The climate is Mediterranean, with hot, dry summers, mild winters and little rain. Winds, especially the mistral, known here as the maestrale, are frequent and help keep the grapes healthy. The island is largely mountainous and its soils are very varied: granite in Gallura, in the north-east; volcanic soils in some parts of the centre and west; sands in the Sulcis, in the south-west; and limestones and clays in other areas.

Appellations and grapes

The island's only DOCG is Vermentino di Gallura, recognised in 1996, which produces whites on granite soils in the north-east. Vermentino is also made throughout the island under the Vermentino di Sardegna DOC. Cannonau di Sardegna is the most widespread red appellation; Cannonau is the same variety as Grenache, and it gives warm, spicy reds, above all in the province of Nuoro, with subzones such as Oliena, Jerzu and Capo Ferrato. In the south-west, the Carignano del Sulcis DOC covers reds from Carignano, or Carignan, often from old bush vines on sand. Other traditional appellations include Monica di Sardegna, Nuragus di Cagliari, Moscato di Sardegna, Malvasia di Bosa and Vernaccia di Oristano, a white wine aged oxidatively under a veil of yeast and made from a local variety of the same name.

Styles

Vermentino whites are usually aromatic, with notes of flowers, white fruit and Mediterranean herbs, and a savoury, slightly bitter finish; those from Gallura tend to show more structure. Cannonau and Carignano reds combine ripe fruit, spice and often high alcohol. The island also keeps alive a tradition of sweet and oxidatively aged wines, such as Vernaccia di Oristano and Malvasia di Bosa, which form part of its wine identity.
