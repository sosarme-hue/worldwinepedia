Algarve

The southernmost wine region of mainland Portugal, on the country’s south coast. After decades of decline, its small wineries now make ripe reds, rosés and fresh whites shaped by a sunny climate tempered by the sea.

History

Vines have been grown in the Algarve since antiquity, and Phoenicians, Romans and Arabs all left their mark on the agriculture of this coast. During the twentieth century, however, viticulture declined sharply: mass tourism transformed the coastline from the 1960s onwards, land became more profitable for building than for vines, and much of the production was concentrated in cooperatives making wines of little interest. In recent decades the picture has changed with the arrival of small producers, many of them new to the industry, who have replanted vineyards, modernised wineries and committed to bottled wines with an identity of their own.

Appellations

The Algarve has four DOCs, named after as many towns: Lagos, Portimão, Lagoa and Tavira. Lagoa has traditionally been the most important wine centre. Today, however, most wines are sold as Vinho Regional Algarve, a geographical indication that covers the whole region and allows a wider range of grape varieties and styles.

Climate and soils

Sheltered to the north by the Monchique and Caldeirão ranges, which block cold winds, the Algarve enjoys a very sunny Mediterranean climate, with hot, dry summers and mild winters. The nearby Atlantic moderates temperatures, especially along the coastal strip, and sea breezes help keep the grapes healthy. The soils are varied: sandy near the coast, clay-limestone in the so-called barrocal, the intermediate belt of limestone hills, and schist towards the mountains.

Grapes and styles

Among the reds, the most characteristic traditional variety is Negra Mole, pale in colour and soft in tannin, which lends itself to light reds and rosés and which some producers have begun to value again. Castelão, Trincadeira, Touriga Nacional, Aragonez (Tempranillo) and Syrah are also grown, the last being very well suited to the region’s heat. Among the whites, Arinto, which keeps its acidity well, stands out, as does Síria, often called Crato Branco here, alongside some international varieties. The reds tend to be ripe and generous in fruit; the rosés are closely tied to summer drinking; and the whites, when picked at the right time, are fresh with a saline character. A significant share of the production is sold within the region itself, often to the visitors who travel through it all year round.
