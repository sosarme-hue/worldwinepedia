La Mancha

La Mancha, in Castilla-La Mancha, is Spain's largest designation of origin by area and one of the largest wine regions in the world. Traditionally dominated by the white Airén, it now also produces a growing share of reds from Tempranillo and international varieties.

History

The plain of La Mancha, a land associated with Cervantes's novel, has a long winemaking tradition. For centuries its wines supplied Madrid and other inland cities, and the arrival of the railway in the nineteenth century facilitated trade. For much of the twentieth century production focused on volume: bulk wine and wine for distillation, largely made by large cooperatives. The earthenware jars known as tinajas, still visible in many wineries, bear witness to that era. From the last decades of the twentieth century, vineyard conversion, investment in technology, temperature control during fermentation and an increase in red varieties have transformed production and given rise to a wider range of bottled wines.

Territory and climate

The designation extends across a vast plateau in the centre of the peninsula, in the provinces of Albacete, Ciudad Real, Cuenca and Toledo, at altitudes of around 500 to 700 metres. The climate is extreme continental: cold winters, very hot dry summers, scarce and irregular rainfall and many hours of sunshine. The dry air reduces the incidence of vine diseases, which favours organic viticulture. The territory of Castilla-La Mancha is also home to several Vinos de Pago and other designations of origin such as Valdepeñas.

Soils

Reddish-brown soils predominate, with a clayey or sandy-clay texture over limestone subsoils, sometimes with a calcareous crust close to the surface. They are generally poor in organic matter.

Grapes and styles

Airén, one of the most widely planted white grapes in the world, remains the most widespread variety. Well adapted to drought, it gives light, fresh young whites when fermented at low temperatures. Among the reds, Tempranillo, known locally as Cencibel, stands out, alongside Grenache, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Syrah and Petit Verdot. Other permitted white grapes are Macabeo, Verdejo, Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay and Moscatel. Producers make young whites, rosés and reds, barrel-aged reds with the traditional terms Crianza, Reserva and Gran Reserva, and sparkling wines.

A region in transformation

The sheer size of the vineyard area, its diversity of soils and its wide range of varieties allow La Mancha to make very different wines, from young everyday wines to aged ones. The growing importance of wineries that bottle their own wines and of organic viticulture are among the most visible changes of recent decades.
