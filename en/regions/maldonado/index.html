Maldonado

Maldonado, in south-eastern Uruguay, is a young wine area set on hills close to the Atlantic Ocean. Its weathered granite soils and cool, windy climate give Albariño, Tannat and other varieties with a taut profile and marked freshness.

History

The department of Maldonado, best known for its resorts such as Punta del Este and for its Atlantic coastline, long had only a marginal winegrowing presence. Its development as a quality wine area is recent: it began at the end of the twentieth century and accelerated in the first decades of the twenty-first, when several producers turned to the inland hills near the sea in search of a cooler climate and soils different from the clays of the country’s south. Among the first projects were vineyards in the Sierra de la Ballena and around Pan de Azúcar. The best-known project is Bodega Garzón, created near the village of Garzón, which has planted an extensive vineyard divided into numerous plots on the local slopes and has helped make the region’s potential known abroad. Alongside it, other wineries of varying size have emerged, often linked to tourism and gastronomy.

Climate

The influence of the Atlantic Ocean is decisive. The climate is cooler than in Canelones, with constant sea breezes and winds that ventilate the vineyards, reduce humidity around the bunches and limit the risk of disease. Moderate temperatures during ripening help retain acidity and aromatic freshness. Rainfall is spread throughout the year, as in the rest of the country, although good air flow and slope drainage help to manage it.

Soils

Unlike the clay-rich south, Maldonado is dominated by granitic soils and old rocks of the Uruguayan crystalline shield. In many plots a shallow layer of soil rests on decomposed granite and rock fragments, known locally as balasto, which drain well and limit vine vigour. The relief is rolling, with hills and low ranges offering varied exposures, and in some areas there are also soils with a higher proportion of clay and silt.

Areas

Vineyards are spread across several parts of the department, including the Garzón area, near the lagoon of the same name; the Sierra de la Ballena, between Piriápolis and Punta Ballena; the surroundings of Pan de Azúcar; and the hills near San Carlos. As in the rest of the country, wineries state the area or vineyard of origin on the label.

Varieties and styles

Albariño has found particularly favourable conditions in Maldonado, giving fresh, saline whites with citrus and floral aromas. Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay and Pinot Noir, well suited to the cool climate, are also grown, and among reds Tannat, which here gives wines with firm tannins but often tauter and fresher than those of the south, along with Cabernet Franc, Merlot and Petit Verdot. Rosés and sparkling wines are also made, frequently from Pinot Noir and Chardonnay.
