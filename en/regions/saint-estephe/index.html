Saint-Estèphe

Saint-Estèphe is the northernmost of the great communal appellations of the Haut-Médoc. Its soils, richer in clay than those of its neighbours, give firm, fresh reds in which Merlot often plays an important role.

Location

Saint-Estèphe extends to the north of Pauillac, from which it is separated by the Jalle du Breuil stream; just across it lies Château Lafite Rothschild. It is a large commune on the left bank of the Gironde, with several hamlets scattered among the vineyards and a small harbour on the estuary. Further north begins the Médoc appellation, which corresponds to the lower part of the peninsula.

History and estates

Viticulture developed here, as in the rest of the Médoc, from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries onwards. In the 1855 classification Saint-Estèphe received five classified growths: Château Cos d'Estournel and Château Montrose, second growths; Château Calon-Ségur, third; Château Lafon-Rochet, fourth; and Château Cos Labory, fifth. Cos d'Estournel is known for its oriental-inspired building, erected in the nineteenth century, and Montrose for its vineyards lying very close to the estuary. Outside the 1855 classification, the appellation has a large number of crus bourgeois and an important cooperative, and some of its unclassified estates enjoy great prestige.

Climate and soils

The climate is temperate oceanic, moderated by the estuary, although the more northerly position means that ripening is sometimes slightly later than in Margaux. The big difference lies in the soils: over the gravels, which remain present on the best mounds, there is a higher proportion of clay, and in some areas the subsoil is limestone. These soils retain more water, warm up more slowly and are very well suited to Merlot, while also protecting the vines better in dry years.

Grapes and style

The appellation is for red wine only. Cabernet Sauvignon remains the main variety, but Merlot generally accounts for a larger share than in Pauillac or Saint-Julien, and the blends are completed with Cabernet Franc, Petit Verdot and Malbec. Traditionally Saint-Estèphe wines had a reputation for being austere and tannic in their youth, with marked acidity and slow development. Today they tend to show more fruit and roundness, without losing the freshness and firmness that set them apart within the Médoc.

Ageing and cellaring

The wines are aged in French oak barrels and the best of them need several years in bottle to open up. Their structure and acidity give them considerable ageing potential.
