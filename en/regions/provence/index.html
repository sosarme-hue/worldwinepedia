Provence

Provence, in south-eastern France between the Rhône and the Côte d'Azur, is the French region most closely associated with rosé, which makes up the great majority of its production. It also has appellations for distinctive reds and whites, such as Bandol and Cassis.

History

Provence lays claim to one of the oldest viticultural traditions in France: the Greeks of Phocaea founded Massalia, present-day Marseille, around 600 BC and spread vine growing along the coast. The Romans, who gave the region its name, the Provincia, later extended the vineyards. For centuries its wines were drunk mainly locally. In the twentieth century the growth of tourism on the Côte d'Azur helped to popularise Provençal rosé, and in recent decades its pale, dry style has become widely known internationally. In 1955 a classification of crus classés was established for a group of Côtes de Provence estates.

Climate and soils

The climate is Mediterranean, with hot, dry summers, mild winters and very high levels of sunshine. The mistral, blowing down the Rhône valley, keeps the vines healthy. The soils fall into two broad groups: limestone predominates in the west and north, while in the east, around the Maures and Esterel massifs, there are crystalline rocks such as schist and granite, as well as volcanic soils in some areas. The Mediterranean scrubland known as garrigue surrounds many vineyards.

Appellations

Côtes de Provence is the region's largest appellation and lies mainly in the Var département, with several additional geographical designations such as Sainte-Victoire, Fréjus, La Londe, Pierrefeu and Notre-Dame des Anges. To the west are Coteaux d'Aix-en-Provence and Les Baux-de-Provence, and in the centre Coteaux Varois en Provence. Alongside them are small historic appellations: Bandol, west of Toulon, known for its Mourvèdre reds, which must contain at least 50% of the variety and be aged for a minimum of eighteen months in wood; Cassis, beside the coastal cliffs, devoted mainly to whites; Palette, near Aix-en-Provence; and Bellet, in the hills above Nice.

Grapes and styles

The rosés, which make up most of the production, are made mainly from Grenache, Cinsault and Syrah, sometimes with Mourvèdre, Tibouren or Carignan. They are usually produced by direct pressing or after a very short maceration, giving pale, dry, fresh and fruity wines. The reds are based on the same Mediterranean varieties, with Cabernet Sauvignon in some areas, and in Bandol they achieve remarkable structure and ageing potential. Among the white grapes, the most notable are Rolle, the local name for Vermentino, Ugni Blanc, Clairette and Marsanne.

Country
France
Climate
Mediterranean, warm and dry, with abundant sunshine and the influence of the mistral.
Soils
Limestone in the west and north; schist, granite and some volcanic soils in the east.
Styles
Mainly pale, dry rosés; Mediterranean reds, including the Mourvèdre-based wines of Bandol, and whites such as those of Cassis.
Grapes
Grenache, Cinsault, Syrah (Shiraz), Monastrell (Mourvèdre), Carignan, Cabernet Sauvignon, Vermentino, Trebbiano
