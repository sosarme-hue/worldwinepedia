Lambrusco of Emilia

Lambrusco is the sparkling or semi-sparkling red wine typical of Emilia, above all of the provinces of Modena and Reggio Emilia, where several DOCs cover the different types of Lambrusco, dry or with some sweetness.

Territory

Lambrusco is produced mainly on the plain and first hills of Emilia, the western part of the Emilia-Romagna region, south of the Po river. The provinces of Modena and Reggio Emilia are its heartland, and it is also made in the provinces of Parma and Bologna, as well as in the neighbouring Lombard province of Mantua, which has its own appellation. The name Lambrusco does not refer to a single grape but to a family of native red varieties related to the wild vines that Latin authors called labrusca.

Climate and soils

The climate is continental, with cold, often foggy winters and hot, humid summers. On the plain, deep and fertile alluvial soils of silt, clay and sand laid down by the Po and its tributaries predominate, while the hills at the foot of the Apennines have more clayey and calcareous soils. Vineyards on the plain were traditionally trained on high, productive trellises.

Appellations and grapes

The main DOCs are Lambrusco di Sorbara, north of Modena, from the Lambrusco di Sorbara variety, which gives pale wines with floral aromas and high acidity; Lambrusco Grasparossa di Castelvetro, in the hills south of Modena, deeply coloured and with firmer tannins; and Lambrusco Salamino di Santa Croce, around Carpi, of medium body. There are also the broader Modena or Lambrusco di Modena DOC and the Reggiano DOC, in the province of Reggio Emilia, where varieties such as Lambrusco Maestri, Lambrusco Marani and Lambrusco Montericco are grown, often with a share of Ancellotta, a deeply coloured red grape. Other Emilian DOCs, such as Colli di Parma, also include types of Lambrusco.

Winemaking and styles

Most Lambrusco is made as a semi-sparkling wine (frizzante), with a second fermentation in a sealed tank; there are also fully sparkling versions and others fermented in bottle, including the ancestral method, in which the wine keeps its lees and may be cloudy. Depending on residual sugar, Lambrusco can be secco, amabile or dolce; the dry versions are the most prized in their home area. There are also rosé versions, especially from Sorbara. Lambrusco is drunk young and slightly chilled, and its acidity and bubbles make it the traditional partner for Emilian cooking, rich in cured meats and filled pasta.
