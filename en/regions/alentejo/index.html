Alentejo

The Alentejo, the great warm plain of southern Portugal, produces mainly ripe, fruity reds and broad whites, and keeps alive the ancient tradition of fermenting wine in clay amphorae.

History

Vines have been grown in the Alentejo since Roman times, and from that era comes the custom of making wine in large clay amphorae, the talhas, which has survived to this day. For much of the twentieth century the region was devoted mainly to cereals, cork oak and olives, and wine was left to cooperatives and small producers. From the 1980s and 1990s winegrowing developed strongly, with new plantings, modern wineries and investment from large companies, and Alentejo wines became very popular in the Portuguese market.

Territory and climate

The Alentejo covers about a third of Portugal’s territory, south of the Tagus river, in a landscape of rolling plains dotted with cork and holm oaks. The climate is Mediterranean with continental features: very hot, dry summers, mild winters and scant rainfall concentrated in the cooler months, so irrigation is common in many vineyards. The Alentejo DOC is divided into eight subregions: Portalegre, Borba, Redondo, Reguengos, Vidigueira, Évora, Granja-Amareleja and Moura. Portalegre, at the foot of the São Mamede range, is higher, cooler and wetter than the rest. Besides the DOC there is the Vinho Regional Alentejano geographical indication, which covers the whole region and allows greater freedom in the choice of varieties.

Soils

Soils are varied: schist, granite, clay and limestone areas, with marble around Borba and Estremoz.

Grapes and styles

The leading reds are Aragonez (Tempranillo), Trincadeira, Alicante Bouschet, which has gained a prestige here that is rare in other regions, Castelão and Touriga Nacional, alongside international varieties such as Syrah and Cabernet Sauvignon. Among the whites, Antão Vaz, typical of Vidigueira, Arinto, Roupeiro and Fernão Pires stand out. The reds tend to be ripe, with generous fruit and soft tannins; the whites are broad and medium-bodied, often with Arinto to add acidity. Talha wines, fermented on their skins in clay amphorae, have a specific designation within the DOC. The regional wine commission also runs a sustainability programme for the area’s producers.

Cooperatives still play an important role in the region, alongside large estates, or herdades, and smaller producers. In recent years interest has grown in higher-altitude vineyards, such as those of Portalegre, in old vines and in talha wines, which many producers have revived using traditional methods.
