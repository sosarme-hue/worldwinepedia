Santa Cruz Mountains

A mountain AVA south of San Francisco, between the Pacific and the bay, defined by the elevation of its vineyards. Small and scattered among forests, it is one of the historic cradles of fine Californian wine, with long-lived Cabernet Sauvignon, Pinot Noir and Chardonnay.

A mountain AVA

The Santa Cruz Mountains rise between the Pacific Ocean and San Francisco Bay, south of the city and west of Silicon Valley. The Santa Cruz Mountains AVA was one of the first in the United States to be defined by terrain: its boundaries follow contour lines, excluding valleys and low-lying areas and including only slopes above a certain elevation, on both sides of the range. It extends over parts of Santa Cruz, San Mateo and Santa Clara counties. The vineyard area is relatively small and highly fragmented: plots are cut into clearings in a landscape dominated by redwood, oak and conifer forests, with many small wineries.

History

Winegrowing reached these mountains in the nineteenth century, and vineyards were planted on their slopes in the decades that followed. During the twentieth century the area attracted quality-minded producers who believed in high-elevation vineyards. Notable historic wineries include Ridge Vineyards, whose Monte Bello, a mountain Cabernet Sauvignon, featured in the famous 1976 Paris tasting, and Mount Eden Vineyards, known for its Pinot Noir and Chardonnay.

Climate and soils

The climate varies greatly from one slope to another. The western side, facing the Pacific, is more exposed to fog and cool winds and is well suited to Pinot Noir and Chardonnay. The eastern side, facing the bay, is warmer and drier and favours Cabernet Sauvignon and other Bordeaux varieties. Many vineyards lie above the fog line, with sunny days and cool nights. Winter rainfall is higher than in other Californian wine areas. The San Andreas Fault runs through the region, and the soils are very varied, with shale, sandstone, marl and shallow stony soils of diverse origin.

Grapes and styles

Cabernet Sauvignon from the eastern slopes tends to be structured, with firm tannins, lively acidity and great ageing potential, often more restrained than that of warmer valleys. Pinot Noir from the western side shows red fruit, earthy notes and freshness, while Chardonnay combines richness and tension. Merlot, Syrah and old Zinfandel vines are also grown. Overall, the area is valued for balanced, long-lived wines, and its remote hillside sites, often reached by narrow forest roads, continue to attract small producers in search of distinctive terroirs.
