Wine region (Galil), with the Upper Galilee, Lower Galilee and Golan Heights subregions

Galilee

Israel’s northernmost wine region and its most highly regarded for quality wine. Its high-altitude vineyards, on limestone and volcanic soils, give structured reds and fresh whites.

Territory

Galilee occupies northern Israel, between the Jezreel Valley to the south and the Lebanese border to the north, and from the hills near the Mediterranean coast to the Jordan Valley and the Sea of Galilee to the east. In the Israeli winegrowing classification it is the Galil region, which comprises three subregions: Upper Galilee, mountainous and high, around Mount Meron; Lower Galilee, with gentler hills and valleys, around Mount Tabor; and the Golan Heights, the volcanic plateau east of the Jordan, which because of its importance has an entry of its own. Taken together, it is the region that holds many of the country’s most highly valued vineyards.

History

Winegrowing in Galilee is very old, as shown by the presses and other archaeological remains scattered across the region. Modern quality viticulture developed from the 1970s and 1980s, when agronomic research showed that the northern heights, cooler than the rest of the country, were well suited to French varieties. The success of the Golan wineries was followed by projects in Upper Galilee, such as Dalton, on the Alma plateau, and Galil Mountain Winery, at Kibbutz Yiron, while in Lower Galilee Tabor Winery, near Mount Tabor, stands out. Many large Israeli wineries based in other regions also buy grapes here.

Climate and soils

The climate is Mediterranean, with rainfall concentrated in winter, more abundant than in central and southern Israel, and dry summers. Altitude is the decisive factor: in Upper Galilee many vineyards lie at several hundred metres, with cool nights, cold winters and occasional snow, while Lower Galilee is warmer. The soils are very varied: limestone and terra rossa across much of Upper and Lower Galilee, and volcanic, with basalt and tuff, in some northern areas and on the Golan.

Grapes and styles

International varieties predominate. Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot give structured reds with ripe fruit and firm tannins, often in Bordeaux-style blends with Cabernet Franc and Petit Verdot. Syrah and Carignan, the latter from old vines, bring a Mediterranean profile. Among the whites, Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc, Viognier and Gewürztraminer stand out, keeping remarkable freshness thanks to the altitude. Traditional-method sparkling wines and some sweet wines are also made. Much of the production is kosher.
