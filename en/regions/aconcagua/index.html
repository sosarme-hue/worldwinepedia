Aconcagua Valley

The Aconcagua Valley, north of Santiago, takes its name from the Aconcagua River, which flows down from the Andes not far from the highest mountain in the Americas. It combines a warm inland sector with a red wine tradition and recent cool-climate coastal vineyards.

History

Viticulture in Aconcagua has its roots in the nineteenth century. In 1870 Maximiano Errázuriz founded an estate in Panquehue, in the inland part of the valley, which became one of the largest of its time and gave rise to Viña Errázuriz. For much of the twentieth century the area had a modest profile on the national scene, but since the 1990s it has gained recognition thanks to top-end reds and, more recently, to new plantings near the sea.

Geography and climate

The Aconcagua River rises in the Andes, on Chilean soil, not far from Mount Aconcagua, which stands across the border in Argentina, and flows from east to west into the Pacific near Concón. In the Chilean appellation system, Aconcagua designates both a wine region, which also includes the Casablanca and San Antonio valleys, and the subregion of the Aconcagua Valley itself. The climate is Mediterranean, with strong contrasts depending on distance from the ocean: the interior, around San Felipe, Panquehue and Los Andes, is one of the warmest sectors of central Chile, with sunny days and nights cooled by mountain air; the western part receives sea breezes and fogs.

Subzones

The terms Andes, Entre Cordilleras and Costa are especially useful here. Aconcagua Andes and the central sector contain the traditional red wine vineyards. Aconcagua Costa, planted mainly from the first decade of the twenty-first century a few kilometres from the sea, has proved to be a cool area suited to Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and cool-climate Syrah.

Soils

Inland, alluvial and colluvial soils predominate, with gravel and stones on the hillsides. In the coastal sector there are soils of metamorphic origin, with schists and slates, and others of decomposed granite, generally poor and well drained.

Grapes and styles

In the inland valley, Cabernet Sauvignon, Carmenère, Syrah and Cabernet Franc stand out, giving ripe, structured reds often destined for long-lived blends; some of Chile’s most acclaimed reds are made on the slopes of Panquehue. On the coast, Chardonnay and Sauvignon Blanc whites show marked acidity and a saline profile, while Pinot Noir and Syrah offer lighter, spicier reds. The availability of irrigation water is a growing concern throughout the valley.
