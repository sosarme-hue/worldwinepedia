McLaren Vale

McLaren Vale, south of Adelaide and next to Gulf St Vincent, is one of South Australia’s historic regions. It stands out for its old-vine Shiraz and Grenache and for great geological diversity.

History

McLaren Vale stretches between the Mount Lofty Ranges and the coast of Gulf St Vincent, about 35-40 kilometres south of Adelaide. Winegrowing began very early: John Reynell planted vines in the area in the late 1830s, and shortly afterwards Thomas Hardy, who had arrived from England, founded a company that would become one of the largest in the country. For much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the region produced robust reds and fortified wines, many of which were shipped in bulk to Britain. Like the rest of South Australia it escaped phylloxera, so it still has century-old Shiraz and Grenache vines.

From the 1970s and 1980s family wineries and cellar doors multiplied, and the region became a wine tourism destination close to the capital. In recent decades McLaren Vale has distinguished itself through its commitment to sustainability, with a regional programme of its own and a high proportion of organic or biodynamic vineyards, and through experimentation with Mediterranean varieties.

Climate and soils

The climate is Mediterranean, with warm, dry summers and mild, rainy winters. The proximity of the sea moderates temperatures: breezes from the gulf cool the afternoons and lengthen ripening. The higher areas, towards the hills, and those near the coast are somewhat cooler than the valley interior. The geology is extraordinarily varied, with rocks of very different ages in a small area: sands, clays, limestones, gravels, schists and soils derived from very ancient rocks. Local producers have mapped this diversity and increasingly use it to distinguish their single-site wines.

Styles

Shiraz is the main variety and gives reds with ripe fruit, notes of chocolate and Mediterranean herbs and soft tannins, often with a little more freshness than those of the Barossa. Old-vine Grenache has gained great prestige and is increasingly made in light, aromatic styles. Cabernet Sauvignon and Mourvèdre, locally known as Mataro, which features in Rhône-style blends, are also important. In recent years varieties of Italian and Spanish origin have thrived, such as Fiano, Vermentino, Tempranillo and Nero d’Avola, well suited to heat and drought. Many producers also make single-vineyard or single-district wines to show the differences between the region’s soils, and the proximity of Adelaide has encouraged the opening of numerous cellar doors.
