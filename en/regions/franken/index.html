Franken

Franken (Franconia), in northern Bavaria, follows the course of the river Main around Würzburg. It is the region of Silvaner par excellence, with traditionally dry wines bottled in the distinctive flattened bottle known as the Bocksbeutel.

History

Winegrowing in Franken is documented from the early Middle Ages, and for centuries the Prince-Bishop of Würzburg, the monasteries and the city’s great charitable institutions were its main promoters. Historic estates are still active in Würzburg, such as the Juliusspital, founded in 1576 by Prince-Bishop Julius Echter as a hospital, the Bürgerspital, of medieval origin, and the Bavarian state winery, successor to that of the prince-bishops, with its cellars beneath the Residenz. In the Middle Ages the Franconian vineyard was much larger than today. Silvaner has been documented in the region since the seventeenth century and has become its emblematic variety. The Bocksbeutel bottle, rounded and flattened in shape, has been associated with Franken for centuries, and its use is reserved by law for quality wines from the region and a few neighbouring areas.

Territory and climate

The vineyards are concentrated on the slopes bordering the Main, which follows a very winding course here, and in its side valleys. There are three main districts: the Maindreieck (“Main triangle”), around Würzburg, with villages such as Randersacker, Escherndorf and Sommerach; the Mainviereck (“Main square”), to the west, with Bürgstadt and Klingenberg; and the Steigerwald, to the east, with Iphofen. The climate is more continental than in the Rhine regions, with warm, dry summers and cold winters, and spring and autumn frosts are a frequent risk.

Soils

Each district has a characteristic soil: shell limestone (Muschelkalk) in the Maindreieck, home to the famous Würzburger Stein vineyard; red sandstone (Buntsandstein) in the Mainviereck; and Keuper marl and gypsum in the Steigerwald. The Stein, a broad south-facing slope overlooking the city of Würzburg, is one of the largest and best-known vineyards in Germany, and its wine, traditionally called Steinwein, enjoyed great fame in past centuries.

Grapes and styles

Silvaner and Müller-Thurgau are the most widely planted varieties, followed by Bacchus and Riesling, which is reserved for the best sites. Franconian Silvaner is usually dry and medium-bodied, with herbal and white-fruit notes and a mineral, earthy profile; it is an excellent partner for the regional cuisine. In the Mainviereck, on sandstone, Spätburgunder of growing reputation is made. Franconian wines are traditionally dry, and the region retains a sober, food-oriented style that sets it apart from other German regions.
