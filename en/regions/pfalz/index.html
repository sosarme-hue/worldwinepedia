Pfalz

The Pfalz (Palatinate) is one of Germany’s largest wine regions and one of its warmest and sunniest, a natural continuation of Alsace north of the French border. It produces outstanding dry Rieslings alongside a wide range of whites and reds.

History

As in the rest of the Rhine valley, winegrowing in the Pfalz dates back to Roman times. In the Middle Ages the bishoprics of Speyer and Worms and numerous monasteries owned vineyards in the area. In the nineteenth century some large family estates in the Mittelhaardt, around Deidesheim, Forst and Wachenheim, became highly renowned for their Rieslings, and their owners played an important role in the economic and political life of the region. In 1935 the Deutsche Weinstrasse, the German Wine Route, was created, running through the region from north to south, from Bockenheim to the French border at Schweigen. For much of the twentieth century the Pfalz was known above all for high-volume wines, but since the end of the century a generation of producers has given the region a reputation for quality, especially with dry wines.

Territory and climate

The vineyards stretch along a strip some eighty kilometres long at the foot of the Haardt hills, the eastern edge of the Palatinate Forest (Pfälzerwald), which continues the Alsatian Vosges to the north. These mountains shelter the region from damp westerly winds, resulting in a dry, sunny climate that is relatively warm for Germany; almond trees, figs and even lemon trees grow in sheltered spots. The region is divided into two districts: the Mittelhaardt-Deutsche Weinstrasse, in the north, with villages such as Bad Dürkheim, Ungstein, Wachenheim, Forst and Deidesheim, and the Südliche Weinstrasse, in the south, around Landau, Birkweiler and Schweigen.

Soils

Geological diversity is remarkable: red sandstone (Buntsandstein) at the foot of the Haardt, limestone, marl, loess and clay, and outcrops of basalt, as in the famous Pechstein vineyard at Forst. In the Südliche Weinstrasse there is also shell limestone and soils of volcanic origin.

Grapes and styles

Riesling is the main variety and in the Mittelhaardt produces dry wines of medium to full body, ripe and spicy, with good acidity; vineyards such as Kirchenstück, Jesuitengarten and Ungeheuer at Forst are among the most highly regarded in Germany. The region also stands out for the Pinot family: Spätburgunder (Pinot Noir), Weissburgunder and Grauburgunder (Pinot Gris), especially in the south. Dornfelder, Müller-Thurgau, Silvaner, Gewürztraminer and other varieties complete a very wide range, which also includes Sekt and sweet wines in favourable years.
