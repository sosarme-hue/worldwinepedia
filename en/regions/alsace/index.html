Alsace

Alsace, between the Vosges and the Rhine, is a region of aromatic white wines which, unlike most of France, are identified by grape variety. Riesling, Gewürztraminer, Pinot Gris and Muscat are its most prestigious grapes.

History

Alsatian viticulture dates back to Roman times and enjoyed great prosperity in the Middle Ages, when its wines were exported down the Rhine to northern Europe. The region later suffered the devastation of the Thirty Years' War. Its political history has been marked by changes of sovereignty between France and Germany: annexed to the German Empire from 1871 to 1918, a period when much of its output went into everyday wines, it was occupied again during the Second World War. After 1945 a quality policy took hold: the Alsace appellation was recognised in 1962, Alsace Grand Cru in 1975 and Crémant d'Alsace in 1976. The Alsace Wine Route, which runs through the wine villages, opened in 1953.

Climate and soils

The vineyards occupy the eastern slopes of the Vosges, at roughly 200 to 400 metres, in the Bas-Rhin and Haut-Rhin départements. The mountains shelter the region from westerly winds and rain, so the climate is semi-continental, sunny and relatively dry; Colmar is one of the driest cities in France. Warm summers and long autumns allow slow ripening. The geology is highly varied as a result of the formation of the Rhine rift: within a few kilometres granite, gneiss, schist, sandstone, limestone, marl and volcanic soils succeed one another, which explains the diversity of the grands crus.

Appellations

The Alsace AOC covers most of the production and usually states the grape variety on the label; Edelzwicker is a blend of several varieties. The Alsace Grand Cru AOC comprises 51 delimited sites, such as Schlossberg, Rangen, Brand and Kaefferkopf, the last added in 2007. The Crémant d'Alsace AOC covers traditional-method sparkling wines, made mainly from Pinot Blanc, Auxerrois, Riesling, Pinot Gris, Chardonnay and Pinot Noir. The terms Vendanges Tardives and Sélection de Grains Nobles designate late-harvest wines, the latter made from grapes often affected by noble rot. Alsace wines must be bottled in the tall, slender bottle known as the flûte.

Grapes and styles

The varieties regarded as noble are Riesling, which gives dry whites with firm acidity and great longevity; Gewürztraminer, highly aromatic, with notes of lychee, rose and spice; Pinot Gris, full-bodied; and Muscat, which in Alsace is usually made dry. Sylvaner, Pinot Blanc and Pinot Noir, the only significant red variety, used for reds and rosés, complete the picture. The level of sweetness in the whites varies with producer and vintage, from fully dry wines to others with noticeable residual sugar.
