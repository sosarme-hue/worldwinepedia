Beira Interior

A mountain region in Portugal’s eastern interior, along the Spanish border and around the Serra da Estrela. Its high-altitude vineyards give fresh whites and lively reds that are still little known outside the country.

Territory

Beira Interior occupies the eastern strip of the old province of Beira, between the Douro valley to the north and the Tagus to the south, with the Spanish border to the east and the Serra da Estrela, the highest range in mainland Portugal, to the west. It is a land of plateaus, mountains and deep valleys, of fortified villages and of traditional farming in which vines share the land with olive trees, chestnuts and livestock. Many vineyards lie at altitudes that are high by Portuguese standards, and this shapes the character of the wines.

Subregions

The Beira Interior DOC brings together three subregions that once had appellations of their own. Castelo Rodrigo, in the north near the Douro, is the driest and has partly schist soils. Pinhel, in the centre, is known for a long tradition of whites and of base wines for sparkling wine. Cova da Beira, in the south, stretches along the Zêzere valley at the foot of the Serra da Estrela and the Serra da Gardunha and has a somewhat milder climate. Outside the DOC, the area’s wines may use the Terras da Beira geographical indication.

Climate and soils

The climate is clearly continental: long, cold winters with frost and sometimes snow, and hot, dry summers with large differences between day and night temperatures. This wide diurnal range helps the grapes keep their acidity and aromas. Poor, well-drained granite soils predominate, with areas of schist, especially in the north.

Grapes and styles

The great white grape of the region is Síria, known elsewhere in Portugal as Roupeiro, which here gives fresh wines with citrus and floral aromas. It is joined by Fonte Cal, a local variety, and by Arinto, valued for its acidity. Among the reds, traditional varieties such as Rufete and Marufo are grown alongside Touriga Nacional, Tinta Roriz (Tempranillo), Trincadeira and Jaen (Mencía). The reds tend to be medium-bodied, fresh and red-fruited, livelier than those of warmer regions, while the whites stand out for their tension. For decades much of the production passed through cooperatives and was sold without much profile, but in recent years producers have emerged who are exploring old vines and the potential of altitude, and the region is beginning to attract attention as one of the cool frontiers of Portuguese wine.
