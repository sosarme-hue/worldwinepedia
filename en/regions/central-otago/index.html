Central Otago

Central Otago, in the mountainous interior of the southern South Island, is one of the world’s most southerly wine regions and the only one in New Zealand with a continental climate. It is famous for its Pinot Noir.

History

Central Otago was settled in the 1860s following the gold rush, and it was then that the Frenchman Jean Désiré Féraud planted vines near Clyde and made wines that were shown at exhibitions. Winegrowing did not continue, however, and for a century the region was devoted to mining, livestock and fruit growing. In the late 1970s and the 1980s a small group of pioneers planted vines again in places such as Gibbston, Lake Wanaka and Alexandra, even though many experts considered the area too cold.

The first commercial wines appeared in the second half of the 1980s and, during the 1990s, the region’s Pinot Noir began to attract attention. Since then the vineyard area has grown considerably, and Central Otago has become one of the great names in New World Pinot Noir, as well as a tourist destination linked to Queenstown and its mountain scenery.

Climate and soils

Lying at roughly 45 degrees south and surrounded by mountain ranges, the region has a continental climate, with short, hot, very bright summers, dry autumns and cold winters. The difference between day and night temperatures is large, and spring and autumn frosts are a constant risk. Rainfall is low, so many vineyards need irrigation. Soils are poor and derived mainly from schist, with wind-blown loess and gravels of glacial and river origin. The best-known sub-areas include Gibbston, the coolest and highest; Bannockburn, one of the warmest, beside the Kawarau river; the Cromwell basin, with Lowburn and Pisa; Bendigo; Alexandra; and Wanaka.

Styles

Pinot Noir covers most of the vineyard area and gives reds that are deeply coloured for the variety, with very pure red and black fruit, notes of herbs and spice, good acidity and fine tannins. Differences between sub-areas are noticeable, and many producers make single-vineyard or single-valley wines. Among the whites, Riesling, in styles ranging from dry to sweet, Pinot Gris and Chardonnay stand out, all with marked acidity. Rosés and some sparkling wines are also produced. The region has widely adopted sustainable viticulture practices, and a significant share of its vineyards is farmed organically or biodynamically. Many wineries welcome visitors, and wine tourism is closely tied to the scenery of the surrounding lakes and mountains.
