Pauillac

Pauillac, in the heart of the Haut-Médoc, is home to three of the five first growths of the 1855 classification. Its Cabernet Sauvignon reds are powerful, firm and very long-lived.

Location

Pauillac is a small port town on the left bank of the Gironde, lying between Saint-Julien to the south and Saint-Estèphe to the north, from which it is separated by the Jalle du Breuil stream. The communal appellation covers most of the commune and a few plots in neighbouring villages. It is a landscape of gentle gravel mounds sloping towards the estuary, almost entirely given over to vines.

The first growths

No other Bordeaux appellation gathers so many illustrious names. In the 1855 classification Château Lafite Rothschild, at the northern end, and Château Latour, in the south next to the border with Saint-Julien, were recognised as premiers crus. Château Mouton Rothschild, originally classified as a second growth, was promoted to first growth in 1973, the only major change that classification has undergone. To these can be added the two Pichons, Château Pichon Baron and Château Pichon Longueville Comtesse de Lalande, both second growths, and a long list of fifth growths, among them Château Pontet-Canet, Château Lynch-Bages and Château Grand-Puy-Lacoste. The local cooperative and several crus bourgeois complete the picture.

Climate and soils

As elsewhere in the Médoc, the climate is temperate oceanic and the estuary acts as a thermal regulator. The best land consists of croupes of deep gravel mixed with sand and quartz pebbles, resting on layers of clay, marl or limestone. Their excellent drainage and their ability to store heat are highly favourable to Cabernet Sauvignon, a late-ripening variety that finds here one of its benchmark terroirs.

Grapes and style

The appellation is for red wine only. Cabernet Sauvignon accounts for a particularly high share of the vineyard and of the blends, completed by Merlot and, to a lesser extent, Cabernet Franc, Petit Verdot and Malbec. Pauillac wines are often described as the most powerful and structured reds of the Médoc: deep colour, aromas of blackcurrant, cedar, tobacco and graphite, and firm tannins that need time to integrate. The differences between the great estates are marked; Latour is usually associated with an imposing structure and Lafite with greater delicacy.

Ageing and cellaring

The top wines are aged in French oak barrels, a large part of them new, and almost every estate makes a second wine, some of them highly regarded. The best Pauillacs rank among the longest-lived red wines in the world and can develop favourably over several decades.
