Vaud

The canton of Vaud, on the shores of Lake Geneva, is the great territory of Chasselas. Its Lavaux terraces, a World Heritage Site, and areas such as La Côte and the Chablais give delicate whites that clearly express their place of origin.

History

The Vaud vineyards have Roman origins, but their present landscape owes much to the Cistercian monks who, from the twelfth century, cleared and terraced the steep slopes that drop down to Lake Geneva between Lausanne and Vevey. For centuries wine was an essential source of wealth for the lakeside communities, which built kilometres of stone walls to support the terraces. In 2007 UNESCO inscribed the Lavaux vineyard terraces on the World Heritage List as a cultural landscape. For a long time the canton’s wines were named after the village rather than the variety, a tradition that still carries weight on labels.

Climate

The climate is temperate and somewhat wetter than in the Valais. Lake Geneva acts as a thermal regulator, softening the winters and extending ripening into the autumn. In Lavaux people traditionally speak of the three suns: direct sunlight, the light reflected by the surface of the lake and the heat stored and released by the stone walls.

Areas and soils

The canton is divided into several regions. La Côte, between Geneva and Lausanne, is the largest, with gentle slopes on moraines and glacial deposits. Lavaux, east of Lausanne, occupies very steep terraces on molasse, conglomerate and moraine; here are the Dézaley and Calamin appellations, both Grand Cru, and villages such as Epesses, Cully, Lutry, Saint-Saphorin and Chardonne. The Chablais, in the Rhône valley upstream of the lake, includes Aigle, Yvorne, Ollon and Bex, with soils of limestone scree. Smaller areas such as Côtes de l’Orbe, Bonvillars and the Vaud part of the Vully, near the lakes of Neuchâtel and Murten, complete the canton.

Grapes and styles

Chasselas dominates the vineyard and gives dry whites with light to medium body, moderate acidity and notes of lime blossom, white flowers and white fruit, with hints of honey and hazelnut as they age. It traditionally goes through malolactic fermentation and is aged on its lees in tank or large casks, which adds texture without masking the character of the soil. Dézaley wines tend to be broader and suited to ageing, those from the Chablais firmer. Among the reds, Pinot Noir, Gamay and the Swiss crosses Gamaret and Garanoir stand out; the canton’s traditional red blend is called Salvagnin. Whites are also made from Chardonnay, Pinot Gris and Doral, another Swiss cross, as well as small quantities of sparkling and sweet wines.

Country
Switzerland
Climate
Temperate, moderated by Lake Geneva, with good sunshine on the south-facing slopes.
Soils
Moraines and glacial deposits, molasse and conglomerate in Lavaux and limestone scree in the Chablais.
Styles
Mainly light, terroir-driven dry Chasselas whites; Pinot Noir and Gamay reds and the Salvagnin blend.
Grapes
Chasselas, Pinot Noir, Gamay, Chardonnay, Pinot Gris
