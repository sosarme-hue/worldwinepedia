Württemberg

A region in south-west Germany, around Stuttgart and the Neckar valley, where red grapes predominate. It is the home of Trollinger and Lemberger, with a strong cooperative tradition and largely local consumption.

Territory

Württemberg occupies the eastern half of the federal state of Baden-Württemberg. Its vineyards are spread along the river Neckar and its tributaries, such as the Rems, the Enz, the Kocher and the Jagst, from around Tübingen to Heilbronn, with some plots in the Tauber valley and a small area on the shores of Lake Constance. Stuttgart, the regional capital, is one of the few large European cities with vineyards within its municipal boundaries, and the slopes overlooking the Neckar are part of its urban landscape. The region is divided into several districts (Bereiche), the most important of which is the Württembergisches Unterland, around Heilbronn.

Climate and soils

The climate is continental, warmer in the sheltered valleys, where south-facing slopes and the influence of the rivers help ripening. The soils are very varied: those of the Keuper, coloured marls and sandstones of the Triassic, predominate, together with shell limestone or Muschelkalk, which forms spectacular terraces above the meanders of the Neckar, alongside areas of loess.

A red-wine region

Together with the Ahr, Württemberg is the German region with the highest proportion of red grapes. The most traditional is Trollinger, a variety of Tyrolean origin that gives pale, light, fruity reds, very popular in the region itself, where they are often drunk from handled glasses in taverns. Lemberger, the local name for Blaufränkisch, gives wines with more colour, structure and spice and is the basis of many of the more ambitious reds; the blend of Trollinger and Lemberger is a regional classic. The picture is completed by Schwarzriesling (Pinot Meunier), widely planted here, Spätburgunder (Pinot Noir) and crossings such as Dornfelder. Among the whites, Riesling is the main variety, followed by Müller-Thurgau, Kerner and Silvaner.

Cooperatives and consumption

A very large share of the grapes is made into wine by cooperatives, which bring together many growers with small holdings, often farmed part-time. Much of the wine is drunk within the region, which has one of the highest per capita consumption levels in Germany, so its wines are relatively little known outside it. In recent decades, however, a group of private estates has shown the potential of Lemberger and Spätburgunder for age-worthy, barrel-aged reds, as well as for top-quality dry Riesling.
