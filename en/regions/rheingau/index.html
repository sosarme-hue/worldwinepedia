Rheingau

The Rheingau occupies the right bank of the Rhine where the river flows from east to west, with south-facing slopes sheltered by the Taunus hills. It is one of the historic cradles of Riesling and of late harvesting.

History

The Rheingau is a small region but one of enormous historical weight. In the twelfth century Cistercian monks founded Kloster Eberbach, which became one of the largest wine estates in Europe and whose walled vineyard, the Steinberg, is still famous. At Schloss Johannisberg, according to tradition, the late arrival of the courier who was to authorise the harvest in 1775 meant that the grapes were picked late and affected by noble rot, with excellent results: this is said to be how the value of Spätlese was discovered. In the nineteenth century Rheingau wines, known in England as hock after the town of Hochheim, were among the most prestigious in the world. Geisenheim is home to one of the best-known institutions for wine education and research, founded in 1872.

Territory and climate

Between Wiesbaden and Rüdesheim the Rhine turns westwards, leaving on its northern bank a strip of south-facing slopes. The wooded Taunus range shelters them from cold northern winds, and the broad surface of the river reflects light and moderates temperatures. To the east, Hochheim overlooks the river Main; to the west, from Rüdesheim and Assmannshausen onwards, the valley narrows and the slopes become very steep. Well-known villages include Rauenthal, Kiedrich, Erbach, Hattenheim, Oestrich, Winkel, Johannisberg and Rüdesheim. The western end of the region, around Assmannshausen and Lorch, is part of the Upper Middle Rhine Valley, inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2002, a landscape of steep slopes, castles and riverside villages where terraced viticulture requires almost entirely manual work.

Soils

Soils are varied: slate and quartzite on the upper slopes and on the Rüdesheimer Berg, and loess, marl, sand and gravel in the lower areas near the river. This diversity is reflected in the character of the wines from each village.

Grapes and styles

Riesling accounts for the great majority of the vineyard area and is the region’s hallmark. Its wines tend to have a little more body than those of the Mosel, with firm acidity and notes of peach, citrus and spice; they are made in every style, from dry to great nobly sweet wines. Spätburgunder (Pinot Noir) has a long tradition in Assmannshausen, where it is grown on slate soils. In recent decades the region’s producers have championed dry Rieslings from classified vineyards.
