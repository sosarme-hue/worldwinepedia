Constantia

Constantia, on the Cape Peninsula south of Cape Town, is South Africa’s oldest wine area. It was famous in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries for its sweet wines and today also stands out for its cool-climate whites.

History

In 1685 Governor Simon van der Stel was granted land at the foot of the Constantiaberg mountain, south of the Cape settlement, and founded a farming and winegrowing estate there. After his death in 1712 the property was divided into several parts, from which present-day estates such as Groot Constantia and Klein Constantia descend. In the late eighteenth century, under the Cloete family, Constantia’s sweet wines, made mainly from Muscat, achieved extraordinary fame in Europe. They were exported to the courts and great houses of the continent, are mentioned in literary works such as Jane Austen’s Sense and Sensibility and, according to tradition, were requested by Napoleon during his exile on St Helena.

In the second half of the nineteenth century the loss of tariff advantages on the British market, powdery mildew and then phylloxera brought that golden age to an end, and sweet wine production disappeared. In the 1980s Klein Constantia revived the style with its Vin de Constance, and Groot Constantia also makes a sweet wine of its own. Today the area is surrounded by Cape Town’s residential suburbs, which limits the vineyard area.

Climate and soils

Constantia is a ward within the Wine of Origin system. Its climate is among the coolest in the Cape, thanks to the proximity of False Bay to the south-east and the Atlantic to the west. The south-easterly wind, the so-called Cape Doctor, blows strongly in summer and cools the vineyards, and the cloud that builds up over the mountains reduces the hours of heat. Rainfall is relatively high. The vineyards occupy the eastern slopes of the mountain, often facing the sea, on soils of decomposed granite with Table Mountain sandstone in the higher parts.

Styles

Sauvignon Blanc is the most widespread variety and gives fresh whites with lively acidity and citrus and herbal aromas, often with a more mineral character than in warmer areas. Semillon is used on its own or in Bordeaux-inspired blends with Sauvignon Blanc, sometimes barrel-fermented. Small-berried Muscat, known locally as Muscat de Frontignan, remains the basis of the natural sweet wines that recall the historic tradition. Chardonnay, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and traditional-method sparkling wines are also made.
