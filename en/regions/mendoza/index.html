Mendoza

Mendoza is Argentina’s leading wine province and accounts for most of the country’s vineyard area. Its irrigated oases at the foot of the Andes are the heartland of Malbec.

History

The city of Mendoza was founded in 1561 by expeditions from Chile, and vines were grown from early colonial times thanks to a system of irrigation channels inherited in part from the indigenous Huarpe people. In 1853 an agricultural school, the Quinta Normal, was created in the city, where the French agronomist Michel Aimé Pouget introduced French varieties, Malbec among them. The arrival of the railway in 1885 and European immigration turned Mendoza into a major winemaking centre geared to the national market. Since the 1990s the province has been the main stage for the modernisation of Argentine wine.

Climate

The climate is continental and desert-like, with scant rainfall, concentrated in summer, and very high levels of sunshine. Viticulture depends on snowmelt from the Mendoza, Tunuyán, Diamante and Atuel rivers, distributed through a network of channels. The altitude of the vineyards, from about 500 to more than 1,500 metres, tempers the heat and accentuates the difference between day and night temperatures. Hail, frost and the zonda wind are the main hazards.

Soils

Poor, permeable alluvial soils of sand, silt, gravel and boulders predominate, and their composition varies greatly from one estate to another depending on proximity to old riverbeds. In some high-altitude areas calcareous crusts and deposits appear.

Zones

Five broad areas are usually distinguished: the Northern zone, near the capital; the Primera Zona or upper Mendoza River zone, with Luján de Cuyo and Maipú; the Uco Valley, to the south and at higher altitude, with Tupungato, Tunuyán and San Carlos; the Eastern zone, the largest and most productive, with departments such as San Martín, Junín and Rivadavia; and the Southern zone, with San Rafael and General Alvear. Within them there are numerous Geographical Indications and two Controlled Designations of Origin, Luján de Cuyo and San Rafael.

Grapes and styles

Malbec is the star variety and is made in every style, from young, fruity reds to single-plot wines aged in barrel or concrete. Bonarda, Cabernet Sauvignon, Cabernet Franc and Syrah are also important, as are whites from Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc and Torrontés. Criolla grapes such as Cereza and Criolla Grande remain widespread in the Eastern zone for everyday wines and grape must.

Country
Argentina
Climate
Continental desert climate, very sunny and dry, with wide diurnal temperature ranges and essential irrigation with snowmelt.
Soils
Poor, permeable alluvial soils of sand, silt, gravel and boulders, with calcareous patches at altitude.
Styles
Malbec reds in every register, blends with Bordeaux varieties and high-altitude Chardonnay whites.
Grapes
Malbec, Bonarda, Cabernet Sauvignon, Cabernet Franc, Syrah (Shiraz), Chardonnay, Torrontés
