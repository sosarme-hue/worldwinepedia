AOC (Provence)

Bandol

Bandol, on the Provençal coast west of Toulon, is the benchmark appellation for Mourvèdre. Its structured, long-lived reds are joined by well-structured rosés and some whites.

Location

Bandol lies in the Var department, on the Mediterranean coast of Provence, between Marseille and Toulon. The appellation takes its name from the small port from which the wines were once shipped, and covers several communes: Bandol, Sanary-sur-Mer, La Cadière-d'Azur, Le Castellet, Saint-Cyr-sur-Mer, Le Beausset, Évenos and Ollioules. The vineyards form an amphitheatre of hills facing the sea, sheltered to the north by limestone massifs such as the Sainte-Baume.

Landscape

Much of the vineyard is cultivated on terraces supported by dry-stone walls, known locally as restanques, built on slopes that are often steep. The work is largely manual and yields are low. The landscape combines vines, pine woods, olive trees and garrigue vegetation.

History

Vines have been grown on this coast since Antiquity, and in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries Bandol wines were exported by sea. After phylloxera, replanting favoured more productive varieties and Mourvèdre lost ground. Its recovery is linked to the creation of the AOC in 1941 and to the work of families such as the Peyrauds of Domaine Tempier, who championed the variety as the backbone of the appellation's reds.

Climate and soils

The climate is Mediterranean, very sunny and dry, with mild winters. The proximity of the sea moderates temperature extremes and brings breezes, while the mistral sometimes blows from the north-west. The soils are poor and stony, mainly limestone, with sandy marls and, in some sectors, siliceous ground. Mourvèdre, a very late-ripening variety, needs abundant heat and sun but also some water; conditions in Bandol are considered particularly well suited to it.

Grapes and styles

The reds must contain a majority of Mourvèdre, completed by Grenache and Cinsault, and to a lesser extent by Syrah and Carignan. The rules require at least eighteen months of ageing in wood before bottling. They are dark reds with aromas of blackberry, plum, leather, garrigue, liquorice and gamey notes with age, firm tannins and great ageing potential. The rosés, which account for a significant share of production, are made from the same varieties; they have more body and structure than many Provençal rosés and can develop in bottle. The whites, a minority, are based on Clairette, Bourboulenc and Ugni Blanc, with some Sauvignon Blanc in certain cases.
