Ribeira Sacra

Ribeira Sacra, in inland Galicia, is a designation of origin recognised in 1996, famous for its terraced vineyards above the canyons of the Miño and Sil. Its main variety is Mencía.

History

The name Ribeira Sacra refers to the great concentration of monasteries and hermitages established along the banks of the Miño and Sil during the Middle Ages, among them Santo Estevo de Ribas de Sil. Tradition traces the beginning of vine growing on these slopes to Roman times, and it was later the monastic communities that developed viticulture on the terraces. After a long period of decline and abandonment of plots, the area was recognised as a Designation of Origin in 1996, which helped bring many vineyards back into production.

Territory and subzones

The designation extends across the south of the province of Lugo and the north of Ourense, along the Miño, Sil and Bibei rivers. It comprises five subzones: Amandi, Chantada, Quiroga-Bibei, Ribeiras do Miño and Ribeiras do Sil. Conditions vary with the aspect and altitude of the slopes.

Climate and soils

The climate combines Atlantic influence with continental features. At the bottom of the canyons summer temperatures can be high and south-facing slopes receive a great deal of sunshine, while rainfall is moderate compared with the Galician coast. The soils are shallow and mainly slate and granite, often on very steep slopes. South-facing slopes, such as many in the Sil canyon, are the most highly valued because they allow full ripening in an otherwise cool setting. Old vines are also common, often with several varieties mixed in the same plot.

Heroic viticulture

Much of the vineyard is grown on narrow stone terraces called socalcos, which drop towards the rivers with gradients that are at times extreme. The work is almost entirely manual, and in some areas the grapes are carried by small rail-mounted machines or even by boat. This is why people speak of heroic viticulture, a term that sums up the human effort needed to maintain this landscape.

Grapes and styles

Mencía is the dominant variety, giving reds with bright colour, red and black fruit aromas, floral and mineral notes and fresh acidity. It is joined by local red varieties such as Brancellao, Merenzao, Sousón and Caíño, as well as Garnacha Tintorera. White varieties include Godello, Albariño, Treixadura, Loureira, Doña Blanca and Torrontés. Most production is of young reds, although single-plot and wood-aged wines are increasingly common, as are Godello whites.
