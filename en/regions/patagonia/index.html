Wine region

Patagonia

Argentine Patagonia, centred on the provinces of Neuquén and Río Negro, is the country’s southernmost wine region. Its cool, windy climate gives fresher-styled reds from Pinot Noir and Malbec and whites with good acidity.

History

Patagonian viticulture developed mainly in the Upper Río Negro Valley, following irrigation works carried out in the early twentieth century, which turned the steppe into a farming area devoted to apples, pears and vines. European immigrants planted vineyards, some of which, several decades old, still survive. After a long period of decline, the region enjoyed a revival from the late 1990s and early 2000s, when new projects were created in San Patricio del Chañar, in Neuquén, and old Pinot Noir and Malbec vineyards were recovered in Río Negro.

Geography and climate

The vineyards lie roughly between 37° and 40° south, at low altitude, generally between 200 and 400 metres, in river valleys that cut across the Patagonian plateau. The climate is continental, dry and cool, with a long ripening season, bright days and cold nights. The constant and often strong wind reduces the risk of fungal disease and helps produce thick skins, but vineyards must be protected by rows of poplars. Late frosts are a risk, and irrigation with water from the Neuquén, Limay and Negro rivers is essential.

Soils

Alluvial soils of sand, silt and gravel predominate, poor and well drained, with boulders on the river terraces and more clayey soils in some parts of the valley.

Zones

In Neuquén, the main area is San Patricio del Chañar, with recently established wineries and large vineyards. In Río Negro, the Upper Valley, around towns such as General Roca and Mainqué, and the Middle Valley stand out. Further south, in Chubut, small experimental and commercial vineyards have appeared at even higher latitudes, such as in the Andean area of Trevelin.

Grapes and styles

Pinot Noir is the variety that has brought the region most prestige, with fresh, delicate red-fruited wines, especially from old vines in Río Negro. Malbec gives deeply coloured wines with more acidity and tension than in the warm areas of Mendoza, and Merlot, Cabernet Franc, Cabernet Sauvignon, Sauvignon Blanc and Chardonnay are also grown, as well as base wines for sparkling wine.

Distance from the major centres of consumption and the smaller scale of production mean that many Patagonian projects are small and strongly focused on expressing specific vineyards.
