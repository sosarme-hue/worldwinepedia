Controlled Designation of Origin (DOC)

Dão

The Dão is a granite plateau in north-central Portugal, ringed by mountains, producing fresh, elegant Touriga Nacional reds and Encruzado whites with ageing potential.

History

The Dão, named after the river that crosses it, is one of Portugal’s historic wine regions and was officially demarcated in the early twentieth century. For much of that century production was concentrated in cooperatives, which made most of the area’s wine, limiting the diversity of styles and the presence of independent producers. After Portugal joined the European Economic Community in 1986 the sector was liberalised and many private quintas and wineries emerged, renewing the region’s image and highlighting its native grape varieties.

Territory and climate

The region occupies a plateau around the city of Viseu, crossed by the Dão and Mondego rivers. It is ringed by mountains: the Caramulo and Buçaco ranges to the west, the Nave to the north and the Serra da Estrela, the highest in mainland Portugal, to the south-east. These barriers protect it both from direct Atlantic influence and from the winds of the interior. The climate combines cold, rainy winters with warm, dry summers, and a marked difference between day and night temperatures that helps the grapes keep their acidity. Vineyards are often scattered in small plots among pine forests. The appellation recognises several subregions, including Alva, Besteiros, Castendo, Serra da Estrela, Silgueiros, Terras de Azurara and Terras de Senhorim.

Soils

Sandy, poor granite soils predominate, with some areas of schist. Their good drainage and low fertility limit the vigour of the vines.

Grapes and styles

Touriga Nacional is thought to have originated in this area, and it forms the basis of many of its best reds. It is joined by Alfrocheiro, Jaen (Mencía) and Tinta Roriz (Tempranillo), among others. The leading white grape is Encruzado, which gives well-structured wines, often fermented or aged in barrel, alongside Malvasia Fina, Bical and Cercial. Dão reds are usually medium-bodied and fresh, with fine tannins and floral and red-fruit notes, and they have a reputation for developing well in bottle. Rosé and sparkling wines are also made.

Traditionally, many Dão reds spent long periods in tank or old wood before release. Today that classic style coexists with fruitier wines, with varietal Touriga Nacional or Encruzado and with blends of several grapes, often from specific plots. The Comissão Vitivinícola Regional do Dão, based in Viseu, oversees the appellation.
