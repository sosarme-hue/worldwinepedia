Martinborough

Martinborough, in the south of the Wairarapa region on the North Island, is a small wine area that pioneered quality Pinot Noir in New Zealand.

History

Martinborough is a small town in the Wairarapa region, little more than an hour from Wellington, separated from the capital by the Rimutaka range. Although there were some vines in the region in the late nineteenth century, modern winegrowing began in 1978, when a study by the scientist Derek Milne pointed out that the area’s climate and soils resembled those of Burgundy in some respects. From then on a group of pioneers, including the founders of Ata Rangi, Dry River and Martinborough Vineyard, planted vines on the terraces surrounding the town.

In the 1980s and 1990s these small producers showed that New Zealand could make Pinot Noir of high quality, and their wines served as a benchmark for other regions of the country. Martinborough has kept a modest scale, with many family wineries and a small share of New Zealand’s total production, and has become a weekend destination for people from Wellington.

Climate and soils

The climate is cool and dry for the North Island, thanks to the sheltering effect of the Tararua and Rimutaka ranges, which hold back rain coming from the west. Summer days can be warm and nights cool, and autumns are usually dry. The wind, often strong, especially in spring during flowering, reduces yields and gives small, concentrated bunches. The heart of the appellation is the so-called Martinborough Terrace, an old river terrace of deep, very free-draining gravels covered by a layer of loess and silt. This combination of poor soils, dry climate and wind limits the vigour of the vines.

Styles

Pinot Noir is the main variety and gives reds with somewhat more structure than many others in New Zealand, with red and black fruit, notes of spice, herbs and forest floor, firm but fine tannins and good ageing potential. Among the whites, Sauvignon Blanc, more textured than that of Marlborough, Chardonnay, Pinot Gris and Riesling are made, the last sometimes with a little residual sugar. The small scale of many wineries favours very careful winemaking, and many producers work with old vines planted in the region’s first decades. Several wineries offer tastings in the town itself, whose original street plan in the shape of the Union Jack is one of its curiosities.
