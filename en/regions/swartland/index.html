Swartland

Swartland, north of Cape Town, went from being an area of wheat and bulk wine to becoming the focus of South Africa’s wine renewal, with old, dry-farmed bush vines of Chenin Blanc, Syrah and Mediterranean varieties.

History

The name Swartland, “black land”, is traditionally said to come from the dark colour taken on at certain times by renosterbos, a native shrub that covered its hills. For centuries it was mainly a region of wheat and livestock, with vineyards supplying cooperatives and large bulk wine companies. That lack of commercial interest had a fortunate consequence: many old vineyards of Chenin Blanc, Cinsault, Grenache and other varieties survived because nobody invested in pulling them out and replanting.

In the late 1990s and early 2000s a group of young winemakers, among them Eben Sadie, discovered the potential of those vines and began making wines with minimal intervention. They were followed by other producers such as Chris and Andrea Mullineux and Adi Badenhorst, and the region became the symbol of a new generation of South African wine. A group of wineries created the Swartland Independent Producers association, with its own rules on grape origin and winemaking, and the large informal festivals organised in the region drew international attention.

Climate and soils

The climate is warm and dry, with hot summers and rainfall concentrated in winter, while Atlantic breezes cool the westernmost areas somewhat. Much of the vineyard is bush-trained and dry-farmed, giving low yields and concentrated grapes. Soils are very varied: shales and slates of the Malmesbury formation over much of the region, decomposed granite on the Paardeberg massif, and sands and iron-rich soils in other areas. Well-known places and areas include Riebeek-Kasteel, Malmesbury and the Kasteelberg mountain.

Styles

Old-vine Chenin Blanc gives textured, complex whites, often fermented in used barrels, concrete vats or amphorae, and is the basis of many white blends that include varieties such as Grenache Blanc, Clairette, Palomino, Semillon and Viognier. Among the reds, spicy, fresh Syrah, Grenache, Cinsault, Mourvèdre and Carignan stand out, alone or in Mediterranean-inspired blends. The dominant style among the independent producers seeks freshness, gentle extraction and discreet use of new oak. Many of these wines come from specific plots, sometimes very small, which producers lease or buy from local farmers, and the label often identifies the vineyard or soil of origin. The region’s success has also led to the revaluation of old vines in other parts of the Cape.
