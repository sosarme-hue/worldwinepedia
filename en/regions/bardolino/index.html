Bardolino

Bardolino is an appellation on the eastern shore of Lake Garda, in the province of Verona, known for its light reds and its Chiaretto rosés made mainly from Corvina and Rondinella.

Location

The Bardolino area extends over the hills surrounding the Veronese shore of Lake Garda, from the town that gives it its name to the inland municipalities lying between the lake and the Adige valley. To the east it borders Valpolicella, with which it shares grape varieties, although the styles are very different. The wines of the Garda shore have a long local tradition, and the Bardolino DOC was recognised in 1968. In 2001 the Bardolino Superiore DOCG was created, reserved for wines with a higher minimum alcohol level and longer ageing before release.

Climate and soils

The lake moderates temperatures, with mild winters and summers that are less hot than on the nearby plain, and allows olive trees to grow on its shores. The soils are of glacial origin: these are morainic hills formed by the deposits left by retreating glaciers, with mixtures of gravel, sand, silt and clay and generally good drainage. These conditions favour wines of moderate colour, fresh aromas and soft tannins.

Grape varieties

The backbone of Bardolino wines is Corvina, usually accompanied by Rondinella and, to a lesser extent, by Molinara and other permitted varieties. Corvina contributes aromas of cherry and spice and good acidity; Rondinella adds colour and reliable yields. Unlike Valpolicella, Bardolino does not seek the concentration that comes from drying the grapes, but rather freshness and lightness.

Styles

Red Bardolino is a light ruby wine, light in body, with aromas of cherry, red berries and spices such as pepper, intended to be drunk young and often served slightly cool. Chiaretto is the appellation's rosé, made with brief skin contact; it has become one of Italy's best-known rosés, and in recent years many producers have made it paler in colour. There is also a sparkling version of Chiaretto. Bardolino Novello, a young wine made by carbonic maceration, was fairly widespread in the past. For Bardolino Superiore, three subzones have recently been recognised, La Rocca, Montebaldo and Sommacampagna, through which producers seek to express the differences between the hills of the appellation and to give the area's reds a clearer sense of place.
